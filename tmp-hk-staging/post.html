Holiday Kindness Wheel: One Good Deed a Day in December

A December kindness wheel gives a class one small deed a day: a note, a secret helper job, a Friday chain. Spin in the morning, do the deed, and watch the month add up.

On December 1st last year, I taped a paper chain to my classroom wall. It had 20 links, one for each school day left before winter break. The plan was simple. Every day, the class would do one good deed together, and we would add a link for it. By December 3rd, the chain had stalled. Nobody could agree what the deed should be. Half the class wanted to write cards. The other half wanted to clean the reading corner. I spent ten minutes settling it, and we lost the whole morning slot to the debate.

Here is the thing. Kids like doing kind things. They do not like arguing about which kind thing to do. So I stopped asking the room to pick. I loaded a short list of deeds into the Classroom Reward Wheel and spun it at morning meeting. One spin, one deed, no debate. The chain started moving again, and it kept moving all the way to break.

Spin for today’s deed

This is the morning spin, and it takes about 30 seconds. Before the first lesson, spin the wheel in front of the class. Whatever lands is the deed for the day. The list stays short on purpose. Six deeds is plenty: write a note, help a classmate, share a book, tidy a corner, sing a song for the class, read to a younger student.

Keep the deeds small enough to finish in one school day. A deed that needs a whole week will sit half done on the wall and start to feel like homework. A deed that takes ten minutes feels doable, and kids will ask for another one tomorrow. That is the whole trick. Small deeds, done daily, beat one big project that never gets finished.

One rule helps here. The deed lands for the whole class, not for one student. If the wheel lands on “write a note,” everyone writes one. If it lands on “tidy a corner,” the class tidies together for five minutes before lunch. Shared deeds avoid the awkward moment where one kid does all the work while the rest watch.

Spin for who gets the note

Some days the deed is a note, and then the next question arrives right on schedule. Who gets it? Left to the room, the same two popular kids get every note, and the quiet kids get none. That is the opposite of what a kindness wheel is for.

Spin for the recipient. Put every student name on the wheel and spin once. The name that lands gets the note that day. The class writes together, or one student writes on behalf of the class, and the note goes into that student’s cubby before lunch. I have seen a shy fourth grader carry a note like that around all afternoon, reading it twice at recess.

Use the Student Picker the same way if your names already live there. The point is the same either way. The wheel picks, so no teacher has to play favorites and no student has to wonder why they were skipped.

One guard keeps this fair. Cross a name off after it lands, and spin again only among the names left. When every name has had a turn, start a fresh round. Without that guard, random chance can hand one student three notes while another student waits all month. A kindness routine that skips kids is worse than no routine at all.

Spin for the secret helper

The secret helper is my favorite version of this wheel, and it runs on a different kind of spin. Each morning, spin a classroom job: tidy the bookshelf, water the plant, stack the chairs, sweep the art corner, sharpen the pencils, straighten the supply shelf. The student who draws the job does it in secret, before anyone notices.

Here is how it plays out. At the end of the day, the class guesses who the secret helper was. They look around the room for clues. The bookshelf is straight. The plant soil is damp. Someone sharpened all the pencils, and nobody saw it happen. The reveal takes two minutes, and the class spends those two minutes talking about work someone did without being asked.

Keep the secret helper jobs invisible by design. A job like “hand out papers” is not secret at all, since everyone sees it happen. Pick jobs that show up in the room, not jobs that happen in front of the room. The mystery is what makes kids want the job.

Spin for the deed that helps the whole room

Some deeds help one person. Others help everyone at once, and those deserve their own spin. Once a week, spin a room deed instead of a person deed. The options look like this: reorganize the book bins, wipe the tables, sort the art supplies, set up the reading corner for the week, label the lost-and-found box.

Room deeds work well on Mondays, when the classroom is at its messiest after the weekend. Five minutes of shared work before the first lesson sets a tone for the week that no speech about responsibility ever will. Kids notice when a room they helped clean stays clean longer, and they start to treat it like theirs.

The Chore Wheel fits this slot if you want the room deeds on their own wheel. Split the room jobs across the wheel, spin once, and the class knows the job for the week. One wheel for daily deeds, one for room deeds, and neither one needs a meeting to run.

Spin for the Friday chain

Friday is chain day. Every Friday afternoon, the class looks back at the week and spins for one shared deed to close it out. The options are group activities, not solo jobs: add a paper chain link for each deed done that week, sing a song together, play a short game, read a story aloud, make a craft, share a snack.

The chain is the record. Each link carries a date and a deed, written in marker. By the third week of December, the chain runs across the whole wall, and kids can point to the link from the day they wrote a note to a classmate or tidied the shelf in secret. That visible record matters more than any speech about being kind. Kids can see the month they built, link by link.

I will be honest, the chain also saved me on the last Friday before break. The class was restless, the lessons were done, and I had 20 minutes to fill. We spun for the chain, added the final links, and read every deed on the wall out loud. The room went quiet for the first time that week. Not a planned lesson. Just a list of things they had actually done.

Spin for the take-home deed

Kindness does not have to stop at the classroom door, and a take-home deed is a simple way to carry it home. Once a week, spin a deed the class takes home: thank someone at home for a meal, help set the table without being asked, read a story to a younger sibling, write a thank-you note to a grandparent, share a toy with a neighbor.

Keep the take-home list short and optional. A take-home deed is an invitation, not homework. No student should lose points for a deed their family could not fit into an evening. The next morning, kids who did the deed can tell the class about it in one sentence. Kids who did not can listen, and the spin moves on.

This spin also solves a December problem teachers know well. Parents ask what the class is doing for the holidays, and “we spin a kindness wheel” is a better answer than a worksheet. A few parents told me their kids started setting the table without being asked, and they wanted to know what changed. The wheel changed. Nothing else did.

Spin for the catch-up day

December is messy. Snow days happen. Assemblies eat the morning. A field trip wipes out a whole day, and suddenly the chain has a gap in it. Kids notice gaps, and they will ask about them. The catch-up spin answers that question before it becomes a complaint.

When a day gets missed, spin twice the next morning. The first spin is the missed deed, done that day. The second spin is the new deed. Two deeds in one day sounds like a lot, but December deeds are small, and kids treat a catch-up day like a bonus round. The chain stays complete, and nobody has to explain why December 12th has no link.

One more guard for catch-up days. If two missed days stack up, do not spin three times to catch up all at once. Spin once for the missed deeds as a group, and move on. Three deeds in one day turns a kindness routine into a chore list, and the class will feel it. The chain is a record of good days, not a debt to repay.

Try the holiday kindness wheel

Twenty school days, one spin a morning, and a paper chain across the wall. A note for a classmate. A secret helper nobody saw. A room deed on Monday, a chain link on Friday, a take-home deed for the weekend, and a catch-up spin when December gets in the way.

Load the deeds into the Classroom Reward Wheel and spin tomorrow morning. Start with six deeds, not twenty. The wheel does the picking, the class does the deed, and the chain does the talking.

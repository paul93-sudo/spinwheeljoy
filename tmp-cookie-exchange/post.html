Holiday Cookie Exchange: Draw Who Bakes What

Last year four people brought chocolate chip cookies to our exchange and nobody brought gingerbread. One wheel draw fixes the duplicates, the dietary misses, and the swap order in about ten minutes.

Last December our cookie exchange had a problem, and the problem was chocolate chip cookies. Four people brought them. Four trays of the same brown circles, sitting next to each other like they were carpooling. Nobody brought gingerbread. Nobody brought snickerdoodles. My aunt brought a tin of store-bought shortbread and looked so guilty about it that we all pretended not to notice, which is worse than noticing.

The exchange itself was fine. The cookies were fine. But half the fun of a cookie exchange is going home with six kinds of cookies you did not bake, and we went home with two kinds and a lot of chocolate chips.

This year I fixed it with a draw. Not a group chat poll, not a spreadsheet nobody opens, just a wheel and ten minutes. Here is how it works, and why it beats every other way of organizing one.

The draw that ends the chocolate chip pileup

The old way goes like this. Someone texts, “Cookie exchange Saturday, bring a batch!” Everyone replies with a thumbs-up. Nobody says what they are bringing, because saying it feels like committing, and committing feels like homework. Then Saturday arrives and four people bring chocolate chip because chocolate chip is what everyone knows how to make without a recipe card.

The fix is to assign the cookies before anyone preheats an oven. Load a Random Name Picker with your guest list, load a second wheel with cookie types, and pair them up: spin a name, spin a cookie, write it down. Ten minutes, done. Nobody has to announce anything in the group chat. Nobody has to remember a spreadsheet.

I’ll be honest, the first time I suggested this, my sister said it took the fun out of it. Then she drew gingerbread, which she had never made, and spent a whole Sunday afternoon cutting out little men with raisin buttons. She now claims gingerbread is her signature cookie. It was not, until the wheel said so.

Spin for who bakes what

This is the core draw, and it takes about two minutes for a group of eight.

Put every guest’s name on the wheel. Spin once. That person gets the first cookie type, drawn from a second wheel loaded with your cookie list: gingerbread, snickerdoodle, sugar, oatmeal raisin, peanut butter, shortbread, thumbprint, molasses, spritz, cranberry white chocolate. Spin the cookie wheel, write down the pair, repeat until everyone has one.

Two practical notes. First, cap the cookie list at about twelve types, even if your group is smaller. A wheel with thirty segments is a wheel nobody can read. Second, if someone genuinely cannot bake their draw, say, a nut allergy in the house and they drew peanut butter, let them trade with one other person, once, no committee meeting about it. The draw is supposed to remove decisions, not create a new set of them.

If your group is bigger than twelve, split it into two draws of six and run them back to back. The second group gets the leftover cookie types, which sounds like a raw deal until you remember the leftovers include thumbprint and molasses.

One cookie each, no doubles

This is the rule that actually saves the exchange. One guest, one cookie type, no repeats. It sounds strict. It is the whole point.

Without it, you get the chocolate chip pileup. With it, the swap table looks like a bakery case: eight different cookies, eight different colors and shapes, and everyone goes home with a tin that looks like they raided a Christmas market. The variety is what people photograph. Nobody photographs four trays of the same cookie.

The wheel enforces this better than a sign-up sheet does, because a sign-up sheet lets the first five people all pick chocolate chip before anyone notices. The wheel removes each cookie type as it is drawn. Once gingerbread is gone, it is gone. The last person to draw gets whatever is left, which is fair in the way that a lottery is fair: everyone had the same odds at the start.

For a bigger group, you can bend the rule slightly: allow a duplicate only after every type on the list has been claimed once. With fourteen guests and twelve types, two people will double up, but they will double up on two different types, not the same one.

Dietary needs without the awkward ask

Here is the thing nobody likes to bring up in the group chat: three of your guests cannot eat what everyone else bakes. One is gluten-free, one is allergic to tree nuts, and one keeps kosher and cannot do the lard-based shortbread your uncle swears by.

The usual solution is that those guests quietly bring their own safe cookies and quietly eat only those, which is a lonely way to attend a party. The wheel solution is simpler. Before the draw, mark which cookie types on the list are safe for which diets, and make sure the list has at least two safe options per need. Gluten-free: flourless chocolate cookies, coconut macaroons. Nut-free: snickerdoodles, sugar cookies, gingerbread. Then, when you spin, if a guest with a restriction draws something they cannot eat, they spin again. No announcement, no explanation to the group. Just a second spin.

It also works the other way. If you want everyone to go home with something they can eat, add a rule: at least two guests draw from the dietary-safe list on purpose. Those tins become the safe table at the swap, labeled and separate, so nobody has to interrogate anyone about ingredients. I did this last year and the gluten-free macaroons were gone before the gingerbread. Turns out people just like macaroons.

How many dozen each person brings

The second argument at every cookie exchange is quantity. Someone brings two dozen. Someone brings six dozen and a story about staying up until 2am. Someone brings one dozen, apologizes the whole night, and everyone says it is fine while mentally noting it was not fine.

Settle it with a spin too, before the draw for cookie types. Load a wheel with 2 dozen, 3 dozen, and 4 dozen, and spin once for the whole group. Whatever it lands on, that is the batch size for everyone. One number, no negotiations, no 2am heroes.

The math is worth doing once, out loud, so nobody feels shorted. Eight guests times 3 dozen is 24 dozen cookies. Everyone takes home a tin of about 2 dozen mixed cookies, and the host keeps the rest for the table. If your group is twelve or more, 2 dozen each is plenty. If it is six or fewer, go with 4 dozen so the swap table does not look bare.

And if someone wants to bring extra, let them, but the extra goes on the host’s table for eating at the party, not into the swap tins. That keeps the swap even and the party fed at the same time.

The swap order spin

The swap itself needs an order, or it turns into a polite stampede. Everyone hovers around the table, nobody wants to go first, then two people lunge at the gingerbread at the same time and both apologize for a full minute.

Spin the name wheel once to set the pick order. First name picks first, then second, and so on down the line. Each person takes one cookie per round until the tins are filled. It takes about fifteen minutes for eight people, and it is weirdly fun to watch, because by round three everyone has a strategy. The gingerbread person guards their own tin. The snickerdoodle person trades two for one molasses.

If you want to add a little drama, spin for one “steal” per person: once during the swap, anyone can trade one cookie from their tin for one from someone else’s, if the other person agrees. It keeps the table lively and it gives the quiet guests a way to get the cookie they actually wanted. Keep it to one steal each, or the swap never ends.

For groups that like a little more structure, a Decision Wheel can settle the side questions too: what time the swap starts, whether the host provides the tins, and who brings the hot cocoa.

The best cookie vote, without a fight

Every exchange ends with someone saying “these are the best cookies here” and everyone else nodding while thinking about their own. Make it official and harmless: after the swap, everyone votes for their favorite cookie, not their own, by dropping a dried bean into a jar labeled with each cookie type. Count the beans, announce a winner, and give that baker the honor of picking first next year.

The wheel can pick the tie-breaker if two cookies tie, which they will, because gingerbread and snickerdoodle always tie. Spin the cookie wheel once between the tied types. Winner takes the first-pick honor. No recounts, no campaign speeches.

Sounds silly, works great. Last year the winner was the macaroons, baked by the guest who had never baked before the draw assigned them to her. She now runs the exchange. That is the whole argument for the draw in one sentence: it turns the person who would have brought chocolate chip into the person who brings the cookie everyone remembers.

Try the draw for your exchange

Load your guest list into the Random Name Picker, load your cookie list into a second wheel, and run the draw before anyone shops for butter. Ten minutes of spinning saves you from the chocolate chip pileup, the awkward dietary conversation, the quantity argument, and the swap stampede, all in one go.

Then, when the tins are packed and the table is cleared, spin once more for who hosts next year. Fair is fair.

Harvest Festival Wheel: Plan the Fall Fair Visit

It is 9am on a Saturday in October, the car is half packed, and the family cannot agree on which fall fair to visit, when to leave, or what to do first. A harvest festival wheel settles the fair, the start time, the ride, the food, and the photo before anyone gets in the car.

It is 9am on the second Saturday in October, and the kitchen table is covered in fair flyers. Maya wants the county fair with the ferris wheel. Tom wants the small harvest festival with the pumpkin patch and the hay bales he can climb on. Mom wants the fair that is twenty minutes away, because last year the drive to the state fair took an hour and Tom asked “are we there yet” fourteen times. Dad wants any fair that opens before 10, because the parking lot fills up by 11 and he refuses to pay for parking in a field that turns to mud if it rains.

Nobody is wrong, and nobody is giving in. This is the part of the outing that usually eats half an hour at the table, with everyone checking their phones for fairs they have never been to. Put it on the Decision Wheel instead. One spin for the fair. One for the start time. One for the first ride, one for the food, one for the game, one for the show, and one for the photo. Seven spins, and the car backs out of the driveway before the argument starts.

Spin for which fair to visit

Start with the fair, because the fair decides the drive, the ticket price, and what else there is to do. A county fair has rides and a midway. A small harvest festival has a pumpkin patch and a short walk from the car. Both are fine. The fight is never about which fair is better. It is about who got to choose last year.

Load the wheel with county, state, harvest, river, church, and school. County means the county fairgrounds with the ferris wheel, the livestock barns, and the midway games that eat quarters. State means the big state fair, which is a full day and a long drive, and the right pick only when nobody has to be home early. Harvest means the farm festival with the pumpkin patch, the corn maze, and cider by the cup. River means the riverside fair with the craft booths and the band stage by the water. Church means the church fall festival, small and close, with a cake walk and a raffle table. School means the school harvest night, which is the right pick when the group has younger kids and the evening needs to end by 8.

Spin once, and that is the fair. No second spin because someone read a review at the table. The wheel lands on county, which suits Maya fine and disappoints Tom for about three minutes, until he hears the county fair has a pumpkin patch by the livestock barn and a hay bale maze for younger kids. Write the fair name on a sticky note and stick it to the fridge. When the car is on the highway and someone asks “are we going to the pumpkin place,” the note ends it.

Check the fair hours before you leave. Plenty of fairs open at 10 on Saturdays and close the midway at 8, and the last ferris wheel ride runs an hour before closing. A spin that picks a fair is no good if the fair is already winding down when you get there.

Spin for what time to arrive

Every fair has a crowd curve, and the curve is the same every year. Early means short lines and open parking. Late means a full lot and a line for every ride. The arrival spin picks a time so the family leaves the house once, on time, instead of drifting out the door in three waves.

Load the wheel with 9 am, 10 am, 11 am, noon, 2 pm, and 4 pm. 9 am means the gates just opened, the parking is close, and the ferris wheel has no line, but the food stands may still be setting up. 10 am means the fair is awake, the stands are open, and the lot still has rows near the gate. 11 am means the crowds are building and the lunch lines are starting, which is fine if the group eats early. Noon means the fair is at full noise, which suits a family that only wants the afternoon. 2 pm means the morning crowd is leaving and the afternoon crowd is arriving, which is the right pick when the group slept in. 4 pm means the late afternoon, with shorter lines as families with toddlers head home, but less time before the midway closes.

Spin once, and that is the departure plan. The wheel lands on 9 am, which means the car leaves at 8:30 for a twenty-minute drive, and Dad gets the parking row near the gate he wanted. One rule keeps this spin honest: the arrival time is the time the car leaves, not the time everyone starts getting ready. Say it at the table, not at 8:25 when one kid is still looking for shoes.

I’ll be honest, the early spin is the one adults resist and kids love. The fair at 9 am smells like kettle corn and cut hay, and the ferris wheel operator waves you on instead of pointing at a line.

Spin for the first ride

The fair is open, the tickets are bought, and now the kids want every ride at once. The ride spin picks one first ride so the group walks to one place instead of standing at the gate debating while the lines grow.

Load the wheel with ferris, bumper, merry, swing, train, and slide. Ferris means the ferris wheel, which is slow, high, and gives a view of the whole fair at once. Bumper means the bumper cars, which are loud and short, and the right pick when the group has energy to burn. Merry means the merry-go-round, which suits the younger kids and the grandparents who want to sit down. Swing means the chair swings, which spin gently and give a breeze on a warm afternoon. Train means the small train that loops the fairgrounds, which is the right pick when the group has a toddler who is done walking. Slide means the giant slide, which is fast and has a line that moves quicker than it looks.

Spin once for the group, and that is the first ride. The wheel lands on ferris, which means the group walks straight to the wheel and rides before the line builds. If it lands on bumper, the group rides the cars and skips the wheel line for later. One rule helps: the first ride is one ride, not a tour of the midway. After the first ride, the group can wander, but the first ride is settled before the gate.

Wear shoes you can walk in. A fair is a lot of standing on gravel and grass, and the ride spin does not fix sore feet at noon.

Spin for fair food

Every fair has a food row, and every food row has a line. The food spin picks one treat per person so the group orders once and sits down, instead of six people ordering six different things while the line grows behind them.

Load the wheel with corn dog, funnel, lemonade, pie, popcorn, and apple. Corn dog means a corn dog from the stand, which is the right call when the group wants lunch and not a snack. Funnel means funnel cake, warm and dusted with sugar, which sells out at some stands by late afternoon. Lemonade means a cup of lemonade, which is the drink every adult orders while pretending it was the kids’ idea. Pie means a slice of pie from the baking tent, which is the right pick when the group is tired and wants to sit at a picnic table. Popcorn means a bag of popcorn, which comes big enough to share and salty enough that everyone reaches in twice. Apple means a caramel apple from the stand, which is the snack that survives the car ride home.

Spin once per person, and the treat you land on is the treat you order. The wheel lands on funnel for Maya, lemonade for Dad, and popcorn for Tom, which means the order takes one minute and the group sits down with food while the next family is still deciding. Order the funnel cake early if the stand has a fryer line. When the sign says sold out, no spin fixes it.

If the group is still hungry after the fair, settle dinner on the way home with the What to Eat Wheel instead of starting a second argument in the car. One spin for dinner, and the fair day ends on a full stomach.

Spin for the game to play

The midway games are where fair budgets go to die, one ring toss at a time. The game spin picks one game so the group plays once, laughs about it, and moves on, instead of drifting from booth to booth while the ticket money drains away.

Load the wheel with ring toss, balloon darts, duck pond, basketball, water race, and strength test. Ring toss means the bottle ring game, which is hard and cheap, and the right pick when the group wants a quick try. Balloon darts means the dart board with balloons, which suits an older kid with a steady hand. Duck pond means the floating duck game, which is the right pick for younger kids because every duck wins a small prize. Basketball means the hoop shot, which is the game Dad always wants and rarely wins. Water race means the squirt gun race, which is fast and loud, and the game the group plays together instead of one at a time. Strength test means the hammer bell, which is the game Tom wants because he thinks he can ring it, and the game the group laughs about after.

Spin once for the group, and that is the game. If the wheel lands on duck pond, the younger kids play and everyone leaves with a plastic duck. If it lands on water race, the group lines up side by side and the winner gets bragging rights until dinner. Set the money rule before the booth: one game, one try each, and the prize goes home in the car, not in a second round of “one more try.”

If the group is bigger than six and the game pick turns into its own debate, settle who chooses with the Random Name Picker before the midway. One name, one choice, and the group walks to that booth.

Spin for the show to watch

Every fair has a stage or a barn show, and the show is the part of the day adults remember and kids sit still for. The show spin picks one show so the group sits down at one time, instead of wandering past three stages and catching the end of each one.

Load the wheel with pigs, pies, crafts, band, magic, and quilt. Pigs means the pig races, which are short, silly, and the show the younger kids ask about on the way home. Pies means the pie contest judging, where the group watches the judges taste slices and argue in low voices about crust. Crafts means the craft tent, where local makers show woodwork and quilts, and the right pick when the group wants shade and a slow hour. Band means the stage band, which plays sets all afternoon and suits a group that wants to sit on a hay bale and listen. Magic means the magic show, which is the right pick when the group has kids under ten and an hour to fill before dinner. Quilt means the quilt display, which is quiet and detailed, and the show the grandparents linger at.

Spin once, and that is the show. The wheel lands on pies, which means the group walks to the baking tent at judging time and watches the slices come out. One rule helps: check the show time before the spin, not after. A spin that picks a show that already happened is a spin wasted, and the group ends up watching an empty stage.

Spin for the photo spot

Someone will want a photo, and the photo argument is the quiet one: where to stand, who holds the prize, and whether the sun is in everyone’s eyes. The photo spin picks the spot so the group takes one photo and moves on, instead of taking twenty photos in five spots while the kids lose interest.

Load the wheel with gate, wheel, pumpkins, barn, sunflowers, and scarecrow. Gate means the wooden arch at the entrance, which is the classic shot and the one with the line. Wheel means the ferris wheel at dusk, which is the photo with the lights on and the sky behind it. Pumpkins means the pumpkin pile by the stand, where everyone sits on pumpkins and holds one in their lap. Barn means the red barn wall, which gives a clean background and shade on a bright day. Sunflowers means the sunflower row at the edge of the fairgrounds, which blooms late into October at some fairs and looks good behind a family photo. Scarecrow means the scarecrow by the maze entrance, which is the photo the kids ask for themselves.

Spin once, walk to the spot, and take the photo. One rule helps: the person who won the game prize holds it in the photo, and the person who drove gets to stand in the shade. A photo where everyone is squinting is a photo nobody frames, and the spin does not fix the sun. Stand with the sun behind the camera, not behind the family.

Sounds silly, works great. One spin at the gate saves the day from turning into a negotiation at every booth, and the photo at the spot the wheel picked is the one that ends up on the fridge.

Try the harvest festival wheel

The fair day starts at the kitchen table, and the table is where the wheel earns its keep. Load the Decision Wheel with county, state, harvest, river, church, and school, and spin for the fair before anyone gets in the car. If the group is bigger than the pumpkin pile, settle who chooses the game with the Random Name Picker before the midway. If dinner on the way home turns into a second debate, put that call on the What to Eat Wheel and drive.

Spin for the fair today. Arrive at 9, ride the wheel first, eat the funnel cake, watch the pie judging, and take the photo at the spot the wheel picked. The plastic ducks will be on the dashboard by dark, and nobody will remember who argued about the fair at the kitchen table.

Christmas Eve Box Wheel: What Goes in the Box

It is December 23, the kids are asking about the Christmas Eve box for the third night in a row, and Mara and Tom have not agreed on what goes in it yet. A Christmas Eve box wheel settles the pajamas, the movie, the snack, the drink, the book, the one small gift, and who builds the boxes, so the box is ready before the wrapping paper runs out.

It is 8:15 on the night of December 23, and Lucy is asking about the Christmas Eve box again. She is seven, and she has asked three nights in a row. Her brother Ben is five, and he has one question only: does the box have candy in it. Mara and Tom have not agreed on what goes in the box yet. Mara wants new pajamas and a movie. Tom wants snacks and a book. Lucy wants everything. Ben wants candy. The box is still empty, and Christmas Eve is tomorrow night.

Here’s the thing. A Christmas Eve box sounds simple until you pack it. Five small picks turn into five small debates, and each debate ends with someone saying “fine, whatever” in a tone that means the opposite. The box is supposed to make Christmas Eve fun. It does the opposite when the grown-ups spend the night before arguing about pajamas.

Put it on the Decision Wheel instead. One spin for the pajamas. One for the movie. One for the snack. One for the drink. One for the book. One for the small gift. One for who builds the boxes. Seven spins, and the boxes get packed before bedtime, because the wheel picks and the packing starts.

Spin for the pajamas

Start here, because the pajamas are the part of the box everyone sees first. Christmas Eve pajamas are a tradition in a lot of houses: new pajamas on the night before, worn to bed, worn again on Christmas morning for photos. The argument starts when two kids want different pajamas and the store has twelve kinds.

Load the wheel with striped, flannel, fleece, matching set, solid, and new. Striped means striped pajamas, which is the right pick when the kids like a classic look, because stripes photograph well on Christmas morning and hide a juice stain better than a solid color. Flannel means flannel pajamas, which suits a cold house, because flannel is warm without a blanket on top. Fleece means fleece pajamas, which is the right pick when the kids run cold at night, because fleece stays warm even if a kid kicks the blanket off. Matching set means the whole family matches, which suits a family that takes a Christmas morning photo, because matching pajamas in a photo look planned even when the morning is chaos. Solid means solid-color pajamas, which is the right pick when the kids have strong opinions about patterns, because a solid color offends nobody. New means brand-new pajamas from the store, which suits a family that treats the box as a gift, because new pajamas feel like a present and old pajamas feel like laundry.

Spin once, and that is the pajama plan. The wheel lands on striped, which means Mara buys two striped sets on the December 23 grocery run, one red and white for Lucy, one green and white for Ben, and the pajamas go in the boxes first so they sit at the bottom. One rule keeps this spin honest: the pajamas go in the box the night before, not on Christmas morning. A box opened on Christmas morning with pajamas inside is a box that missed its whole point.

Spin for the movie

The pajamas are settled, and now the movie needs a call. Christmas Eve has a long evening in it. Dinner ends, the dishes sit in the sink, and the kids have energy left. A movie gives the evening a shape. The argument starts when Lucy wants a cartoon and Ben wants the same movie he watched last week, and Tom wants something the grown-ups can stand too.

Load the wheel with comedy, classic, cartoon, musical, new, and reread. Comedy means a funny Christmas movie, which is the right pick when the evening needs laughs, because a comedy keeps a seven-year-old and a five-year-old in the same room for ninety minutes. Classic means a classic Christmas movie, which suits a family with a tradition, because a classic is a movie the grown-ups quote and the kids learn. Cartoon means an animated Christmas movie, which is the right pick when Ben is tired, because a cartoon holds a five-year-old better than a long live-action film. Musical means a movie with songs, which suits a family that sings along, because a musical turns the couch into a sing-along and nobody minds. New means a movie nobody has seen, which is the right pick when the family is bored of the same three films, because a new movie makes Christmas Eve feel different from last year. Reread means the same movie as last year, which suits a family that loves a ritual, because a reread is a tradition and traditions are the point of the box.

Spin once, and that is the movie plan. The wheel lands on classic, which means the family watches the same classic they watched two years ago, Lucy quotes the funny parts before they happen, and Ben falls asleep on Tom’s lap halfway through. One rule helps: the movie starts by 7:00. A movie that starts at 9:00 ends with a kid asleep on the couch and a grown-up carrying a sleeping child to bed, and that is a fine ending too, but it is not a movie night.

Spin for the snack

The movie is picked, and now the snack needs a call. A Christmas Eve movie without a snack is a movie with two kids asking for food every ten minutes. A snack picked in advance keeps the movie running and the kitchen quiet. The argument starts when Lucy wants cookies and Ben wants candy and Tom wants something that does not end up ground into the couch.

Load the wheel with popcorn, cookies, candy, nuts, fruit, and chips. Popcorn means a big bowl of popcorn, which is the right pick for a movie night, because popcorn is cheap, fills a bowl fast, and suits a couch full of people. Cookies means Christmas cookies, which suits a family that baked that week, because cookies from the family’s own baking taste like Christmas Eve and cost nothing extra. Candy means a small bowl of candy, which is the right pick when the kids want a treat, because a small bowl ends and a big bag does not. Nuts means mixed nuts, which suits a family with older kids, because nuts are quiet to eat during a movie and do not crunch over the dialogue. Fruit means apples and grapes, which is the right pick when the snack table already has sweets, because fruit balances a table full of sugar. Chips means chips and dip, which suits a family that wants salty with the sweet, because chips give the snack table a second flavor and keep the cookies from vanishing in ten minutes.

Spin once, and that is the snack plan. The wheel lands on popcorn, which means Tom pops two bags before the movie, Mara puts the big bowl on the coffee table, and the cookies stay in the tin for Christmas Day. One rule helps: the snack bowl stays in the living room. A snack bowl that travels to the bedroom ends up as crumbs in the sheets, and nobody wants crumbs in the sheets on Christmas Eve.

Spin for the drink

The snack is settled, and now the drink needs a call. This one sounds small until someone pours a drink that stains the couch or hands a five-year-old a mug that is too hot. A drink picked in advance keeps the evening calm and the couch clean. The argument starts when Lucy wants hot chocolate with marshmallows and Tom wants something that does not take twenty minutes to make.

Load the wheel with cocoa, cider, tea, milk, juice, and water. Cocoa means hot chocolate, which is the right pick for a cold night, because cocoa is warm, sweet, and feels like Christmas in a mug. Cider means warm apple cider, which suits a family that already has cider in the fridge, because cider warms up in five minutes and smells like the season. Tea means herbal tea, which is the right pick for the grown-ups, because tea is warm without sugar and helps a grown-up wind down. Milk means warm milk, which suits a tired five-year-old, because warm milk is gentle and helps a kid fall asleep after the movie. Juice means juice boxes, which is the right pick when the kids want something cold, because a juice box spills less than a full cup. Water means water for everyone, which suits a night that already has plenty of sugar, because water keeps the kids hydrated and the couch safe.

Spin once, and that is the drink plan. The wheel lands on cocoa, which means Mara makes a pot of cocoa after dinner, Lucy gets marshmallows, Ben gets his in a mug with a lid, and Tom drinks his black because he says marshmallows are for kids and then eats Lucy’s spare ones. One rule helps: the kids’ mugs have lids. A lid on a kid’s mug is the difference between cocoa on the couch and cocoa in the kid, and on Christmas Eve the couch wins.

If the drink debate turns into a breakfast debate for Christmas morning, the What to Eat Wheel settles that one too. One spin for pancakes or casserole, and the morning has a plan before the presents start.

Spin for the book

The drink is picked, and now the book needs a call. The movie ends, the kids are in their new pajamas, and there is still time before bed. A book gives the night a quiet ending. The argument starts when Lucy wants a long chapter book and Ben wants a picture book and Mara wants everyone in bed by 9:30.

Load the wheel with picture, chapter, poetry, comic, reread, and new. Picture means a picture book, which is the right pick when Ben is tired, because a picture book is short and a tired five-year-old listens to pictures better than to chapters. Chapter means one chapter of a longer book, which suits a seven-year-old who wants a story that continues, because one chapter ends on a good spot and gives tomorrow a reason to read again. Poetry means a book of poems, which is the right pick for a quiet night, because poems are short and a family can read three before bed. Comic means a comic book, which suits a kid who reads pictures first, because a comic keeps a reluctant reader turning pages. Reread means a favorite book from last year, which is the right pick when the family wants comfort, because a reread is a story everyone already loves. New means a brand-new book, which suits a family that treats the box as a gift, because a new book feels like a present and a reread feels like homework.

Spin once, and that is the book plan. The wheel lands on picture, which means Mara reads a picture book aloud, Lucy turns the pages, Ben points at the pictures, and the book ends before anyone gets sleepy. One rule helps: the book is read in bed, not on the couch. A book read in bed ends with lights out, and a book read on the couch ends with a kid asking for one more chapter.

Spin for the small gift

The book is settled, and now the small gift needs a call. The Christmas Eve box holds one small gift, not a second Christmas. The argument starts when someone puts a big toy in the box and Christmas morning feels smaller by comparison. A small gift keeps the box fun and the morning big.

Load the wheel with puzzle, socks, bath, crayons, card, and ornament. Puzzle means a small puzzle, which is the right pick for a quiet evening, because a puzzle keeps two kids busy at the table while the grown-ups wrap. Socks means fun socks, which suits a kid who likes silly clothes, because socks are cheap, useful, and funny in a stocking shape. Bath means a bath toy or bubble bath, which is the right pick when the kids bathe before bed, because a bath gift turns bath time into part of the fun. Crayons means a new pack of crayons, which suits a kid who draws, because new crayons feel like a gift and old crayons feel like school. Card means a card game, which is the right pick for a family that plays together, because a card game gives the evening one more activity. Ornament means a small ornament, which suits a family that hangs one each year, because an ornament goes on the tree and stays part of Christmas after the box is gone.

Spin once, and that is the gift plan. The wheel lands on puzzle, which means each box gets a small puzzle, Lucy and Ben race to finish theirs, and the puzzles stay on the table until Christmas morning. One rule helps: the small gift costs less than the pajamas. A small gift that costs more than the pajamas is not a small gift, and Christmas morning deserves the big moment.

Spin for who builds the boxes

The box contents are settled, and now one last spin decides who builds the boxes. This sounds like a chore, and it is, but it is also the part that makes the box feel fair. When one grown-up builds both boxes alone, that grown-up ends up tired and a little resentful. When the job is shared, the boxes get built faster and nobody feels stuck with the work.

Load the wheel with mom, dad, both, kids, grandma, and rotate. Mom means Mara builds both boxes, which is the right pick when Tom is still at work, because a box built by one person on time beats a box built by two people late. Dad means Tom builds both boxes, which suits a night when Mara is out, because the boxes still need building and one person can do it. Both means Mara and Tom build the boxes together, which is the right pick for most families, because two people packing two boxes takes half the time. Kids means Lucy and Ben help build the boxes, which suits a family that wants the kids involved, because kids who help build the box enjoy opening it more. Grandma means grandma builds the boxes, which is the right pick when grandma is visiting, because a grandma-built box feels like a gift from grandma. Rotate means the job rotates each year, which suits a family that wants fairness over time, because a job that rotates never belongs to one person forever.

Spin once, and that is the builder plan. The wheel lands on both, which means Mara and Tom build the boxes together after the kids are asleep, Mara folds the pajamas, Tom wraps the small gifts, and the boxes sit by the fireplace before midnight. One rule helps: the boxes are built the night before, not on Christmas Eve afternoon. A box built in a rush on Christmas Eve afternoon ends up missing the book or the snack, and a box missing the snack is a box Ben will notice.

If the house cannot agree who builds the boxes this year, settle it with the Random Name Picker first. One draw for the builder, one spin for the contents, and the boxes get built without a debate.

Try the Christmas Eve box wheel

It is 9:40 on the night of December 23, and the boxes are packed. Two striped pajama sets sit at the bottom of each box. The classic movie is picked for 7:00 tomorrow. The popcorn is in the pantry, the cocoa mix is by the mugs, the picture book is on the shelf, the small puzzles are wrapped, and Mara and Tom will build the boxes together after the kids are asleep. Christmas Eve still has a movie, a snack, and two kids who will ask for candy anyway, because it is Christmas Eve and that part does not change. The box just stopped being the hard part.

Try it on the Decision Wheel the week before Christmas. Load the pajamas, spin for the movie, and write the builder plan on a note in your phone. Sounds silly, works great. The boxes get built, the evening has a shape, and Christmas morning starts with kids in new pajamas and a house that feels ready, which is more than most December 23 nights manage.

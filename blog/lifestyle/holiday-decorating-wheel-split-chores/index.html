Holiday Decorating Wheel: Split the Decorating Chores

It's the first Saturday in December, the boxes are in the living room, and four people have four different ideas about what goes up first. Put the jobs on a wheel, spin, and start with whatever lands.

It’s the first Saturday in December, the boxes are stacked in the living room, and nobody has agreed on anything yet. One person wants the tree up first. Another wants the outside lights done while it is still light out. The youngest is already pulling ornaments out of tissue paper, and the oldest is asking why the mantel garland is still in a bag from last year. You have not hung a single thing, and the morning is already a negotiation.

Here’s the thing: decorating is not hard work. It is a lot of small jobs, and the arguing about who does which one takes longer than the jobs themselves. So put every job on a Chore Wheel, spin, and start with whatever lands. The wheel does not care who decorated last year or who is loudest this year, and that is exactly why it works in a house full of opinions.

Spin the big jobs first, before anyone opens a box

The mistake most families make is opening all the boxes at once. Then the living room looks like a storage unit exploded, the tree is half out of its bag, and someone is untangling lights on the floor while someone else asks where the stockings went. Nobody knows what to do first, so everyone does a little of everything, and nothing gets finished.

Spin for the big jobs before a single box opens. Load the wheel with the six jobs that define the whole house: tree, lights, mantel, wreath, yard, table. One spin picks the first job, and the whole household works on that one thing until it is done. Then you spin again for the next one.

I’ll be honest, the first time we did this, the wheel picked the yard, and I wanted to argue with it. The living room was still full of boxes, and going outside felt backwards. But the yard took forty minutes with everyone helping, and when we came back inside, the house felt started instead of just messy. A spin you disagree with is still a decision, and a decision beats a morning of everyone standing around boxes.

Split the house into inside and outside crews

Once the big jobs are listed, the next problem is bodies. Some people want to be outside in the cold air. Some people want to stay inside where it is warm and the cocoa is. If you let people drift, you end up with four people inside hanging one wreath and nobody outside doing the lights, or the other way around.

Spin for crews instead of letting them form by habit. Put four zones on the wheel: inside, outside, porch, stairs. Spin once per person, and that person owns that zone for the morning. The inside crew handles the tree, the mantel, and the table. The outside crew handles the yard lights and the wreath on the front door. The porch and stairs crews handle the smaller spots that usually get forgotten until the last day.

This also fixes the quiet unfairness of decorating day. In most houses, the same person ends up outside on the ladder every year because nobody else volunteers, and the same person ends up inside doing the fiddly table settings. A spin hands those jobs out fresh each year. If you drew outside last year, you might draw inside this year, and nobody has to make a speech about it.

Give the kids jobs they will actually want

Kids do not hate decorating. They hate being handed the boring leftover job after the adults took the fun ones. “You can put the tissue paper back in the box” is not a decorating job. It is cleanup dressed up as participation, and kids can tell.

Put the kid-sized jobs on their own wheel and let them spin for them. Ornaments, stockings, garland, table. Those four jobs are real, visible, and done at kid height, which matters more than adults admit. When a kid spins ornaments, that kid owns the lower half of the tree for the afternoon, and the ornaments go exactly where that kid puts them, even the glittery ones that clash.

One rule makes this work: do not rearrange a kid’s work after the spin. If you move every ornament to a better spot the minute they leave the room, you have taught them that spinning was pointless. Leave it. The tree looks like a family tree, not a store display, and that is the point. If your household also argues about who picks the music while you decorate, the Random Name Picker settles the DJ question the same way, one spin, no campaign speeches.

Let the wheel pick the ladder person, and keep it to adults

Every decorating day has one job that should not be a kid job and should not be a volunteer job: the ladder. Roofline lights, the high wreath, the top of the tree if it is tall. Someone has to go up, and in most houses that someone is whoever feels guiltiest saying no.

Make a separate adult-only wheel for ladder jobs. Put the adults’ names on it: Sam, Alex, Jo, Pat, or whoever the adults in your house are. Spin once, and that person does the ladder work for the day, with someone else holding the ladder and someone else handing up clips. The spin does two useful things. It stops the same person from climbing every year by default, and it stops the awkward silence where everyone waits for someone else to offer.

A word about safety here, plain and simple: the wheel picks who climbs, not whether the climb is safe. If the roof is icy, if the ladder wobbles, if the wind picks up, the job waits. Spin again tomorrow. No decoration is worth a fall, and the wheel will still be there when the weather clears.

Spin the order, so the tree goes up before the ornaments come out

Even with crews assigned, order still causes fights. The ornaments come out before the tree is up. The garland goes on the mantel before the mantel is dusted. The table centerpiece lands on a table that still has mail on it. Everyone is working, but everyone is working out of order, and the afternoon turns into rework.

Spin the order at the start of the day, one job at a time. The first spin picks the first job for the whole house. When it is done, spin again for the next job. This sounds slow, and it is faster than the alternative, because nobody has to redo a job that got done too early. The tree goes up, then the lights go on it, then the ornaments go on the lights. That sequence is obvious once you say it out loud, and a wheel is a good way to say it out loud without one person lecturing the rest of the house.

If two jobs do not depend on each other, they can run at the same time, one per crew. The wheel still decides which crew takes which job, so the parallel work does not turn into a second negotiation. You can borrow the same trick for the rest of December: the Decision Wheel picks the order for any day with too many jobs and too many opinions.

Spin the takedown plan now, while everyone is still in a good mood

Here is the part every family skips, and every family regrets skipping. Nobody plans takedown on decorating day, because decorating day feels fun and takedown feels like January. Then January arrives, the tree is dropping needles, the lights are still on the roof, and the same argument starts again, except now nobody is in a holiday mood.

So spin the takedown plan on decorating day, while everyone is still happy and the boxes are still out. Put four takedown jobs on the wheel: tree down, lights down, boxes, donate. Spin once per person, and write the result on a piece of tape stuck inside the biggest box lid. When January comes, there is no meeting. The tape says who does what, and the wheel said it back when everyone agreed.

The donate spin matters most. Every house has decorations nobody uses anymore, the chipped angel, the garland that sheds, the set of lights with half the bulbs out. Put donate on the wheel as a real job, not a suggestion. The person who spins it fills one bag for donation before the boxes close. Next December starts with fewer boxes, and the boxes that remain hold things people actually want to hang.

One rule that keeps the whole thing fair

All of this only works with one rule, and it is worth saying out loud before the first spin: a spin stands, even when you do not like it. If the wheel gives you the yard in the cold, you do the yard. If it gives your teenager the table setting, the teenager does the table setting, and nobody swaps jobs after the fact without a second spin.

Sounds strict for a fun day, and it is the reason the day stays fun. The minute one person trades away a job they spun, everyone else wants to trade too, and you are back to negotiating in a living room full of boxes. A spin that stands is a spin people trust, and a wheel people trust gets used again next year. That is how decorating day stops being an argument and starts being a tradition, one spin at a time.

Try the holiday decorating wheel

Load your household’s jobs onto the Chore Wheel, spin for the big jobs first, split into inside and outside crews, and let the wheel pick the ladder person and the takedown plan before the day starts. One spin ends the debate about what goes up first, and the rest of the day gets to be what it was supposed to be: boxes opening, lights going on, and a house that looks like December.

Christmas Lights Drive Wheel: Pick Tonight's Route

It's 6:45 on a December evening, the kids are already arguing about which neighborhood has the best lights, and you haven't left the driveway. Put the route on a wheel, spin once, and drive.

It’s 6:45 on a December evening, the car is already warm, and the back seat has split into two camps. One kid wants the big house on Maple Street with the inflatable snowman. The other wants the cul-de-sac where every house syncs its lights to music. You haven’t left the driveway yet, and the trip is already a debate.

Here’s the thing: a lights drive is supposed to be the easy part of December. Nobody has to cook, nobody has to wrap anything, and the whole point is sitting in a warm car looking at other people’s electric bills. So put the route on a Decision Wheel, spin once before you back out, and let the wheel settle it. The kids argue with the wheel for about four seconds, and then they argue about which house is best, which is the argument you actually wanted.

Spin the neighborhood first, not the whole route

The mistake most families make is trying to plan the entire night at the kitchen table. Six neighborhoods, three must-see houses, a hot chocolate stop, and a route that loops back past the good street twice. By the time you agree on all of it, the youngest is asleep and the oldest has changed her mind.

Spin for one thing first: tonight’s neighborhood. Load the wheel with the streets your family actually likes, Maple Street, Oak Lane, River Road, the Hilltop loop, downtown, Lakeview, and spin once. Wherever it lands, that’s tonight. The other neighborhoods keep for other nights, and knowing that takes the pressure off this one drive. You don’t need to see every light in town tonight. You need one good street, done well.

This also ends the annual “but we always go to Maple first” fight. The wheel has no favorites and no memory of last year, which is exactly why it works. If Maple wins three nights in a row, that’s the wheel’s doing, not yours, and somehow that makes it fine.

Let the wheel set the order of stops

Once the neighborhood is picked, the next argument is order. Do you start at the big house and work down, or save it for last? Does the cul-de-sac come before or after the park lights? Everyone has a theory, and every theory sounds reasonable at 6:45 in a parked car.

Spin for the order instead. Put your three or four stops on the wheel, spin, and drive to whatever comes up first. Then spin again at the next corner for the second stop. Spinning stop by stop keeps the night feeling like an adventure instead of a schedule, and it solves the real problem with a fixed route: the moment one stop disappoints, the whole plan feels ruined. A spun order has no plan to ruin. Each stop is just the next surprise.

I’ll be honest, the first time we did this, the wheel sent us to the quietest street first, and I thought the night was a bust. Then the second spin landed on the house with the train set in the front yard, and the kids forgot all about the quiet street. A fixed route would have saved the train house for last. The wheel put it second, and the night peaked early, which turned out to be fine, because we were home by eight.

Give everyone one must-see nomination

A wheel works best when every person in the car has a stake in it. Before you leave, let each person name one house or street they want to see, no matter how small. Your six-year-old’s pick might be the house with the blow-up dinosaur in a Santa hat. Your teenager’s pick might be the house that plays music. Yours might be the old Victorian that does the whole thing with plain white lights and no inflatables at all.

Put all the nominations on the wheel, spin once, and that house becomes the night’s must-see stop. The rest of the night is bonus. This matters more than it sounds. When a kid’s pick wins the spin, that kid watches the whole drive differently, pointing out houses, keeping score, narrating like a tour guide. When nobody’s pick is on the wheel, the drive is just something adults decided, and kids can tell the difference from the back seat.

If the must-see house is dark when you get there, spin again for a backup from the same list. One dark house should not end the night. The wheel already gave you a second pick, and the second pick counts just as much.

Spin for the snack stop, too

Every lights drive needs a warm drink stop, and every family has a different opinion about where. One person wants the coffee shop downtown. Another wants the drive-through with the peppermint shakes. The kids want whatever place has the biggest cups.

Put the options on the wheel and spin for it at the halfway point. Coffee shop, drive-through, grocery store cocoa packets to make at home, the bakery that stays open late in December. Spinning for the snack stop does two things. It gives the driver a break, which matters on a cold night, and it gives the kids something to look forward to halfway through, which matters even more when the back seat starts getting restless.

Keep the snack stop simple. This is not dinner. It’s a cup of something warm and a cookie, eaten in the car with the heat on, while you decide whether to do one more street. If the snack stop turns into a sit-down meal, the lights drive is over, and nobody will want to get back in the car.

If your family also argues about dinner before the drive, the What to Eat Wheel settles that one the same way, one spin, no committee meeting.

Set the one-more-street rule before you leave

Every lights drive ends the same way: someone in the back seat says “just one more street” three times, and the driver ends up circling a subdivision at 9:30 with a sleeping toddler and a teenager who has a test tomorrow. The fix is a rule you agree on before the car moves.

Spin for the number of extra streets at the start of the night. Load the wheel with one, two, or three, spin once, and write the number on your hand or the dashboard. When the must-see stops are done and the snack is finished, you drive exactly that many extra streets, and then you go home. No negotiation at the corner, because the number was decided when everyone was still in a good mood.

Sounds silly, works great. The kids accept a limit they watched the wheel set, in a way they never accept a limit a parent announces at 9 p.m. in a dark car. And the driver gets to enjoy the last street instead of dreading the argument about the next one.

Spin a backup loop for rain and cold nights

December weather does not care about your plans. Some nights it rains sideways. Some nights the windchill drops and the youngest starts shivering before the second street. Canceling outright feels like a letdown, but driving the full route in a storm feels worse.

Keep a short backup loop on the wheel for those nights: the downtown lights you can see from the car without getting out, the one big house on the main road, the drive-through light display at the park. Three or four stops, all visible from a warm car, no walking, no rolling windows down for long. Spin the backup loop when the weather turns, and the night still counts. The kids still saw lights, still drank cocoa, still got the drive. It was a shorter version, not a canceled one.

This is also the night to let someone else pick the music. Spin the Random Name Picker for the car’s DJ, and let that person control the playlist for the whole loop. A short drive with good music beats a long drive with everyone cold and quiet.

Keep a running list for next year

The best part of a lights drive is not tonight. It’s next December, when someone says “remember the house with the train set” and everyone knows exactly which house that is. Keep the list. After each drive, write down the street, the best house, and the snack stop that won the spin. One line per night, on the fridge or in a phone note.

By the end of the season you’ll have a list of six or eight proven stops, and next year’s wheel loads itself. The streets that won twice stay on. The street that was dark two nights in a row comes off. The snack stop that made everyone happy goes straight back on. A wheel gets better the more you use it, because the options on it are all places your family actually liked, not places a list on the internet told you to like.

Start tonight, even if it’s late, even if it’s just one street. Spin the neighborhood, drive to whatever comes up, and let the wheel decide the rest. The lights will be there. The argument about which ones to see first does not have to be.

Try the lights drive wheel

Load your family’s favorite streets onto the Decision Wheel, spin for tonight’s neighborhood before you leave the driveway, and let the wheel pick the order, the must-see stop, and the snack stop. One spin ends the debate, and the drive gets to be what it was supposed to be: a warm car, a cold night, and a street full of lights.

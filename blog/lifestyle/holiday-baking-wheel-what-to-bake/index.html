Holiday Baking Wheel: What to Bake This Weekend

It's a Saturday morning in December, the pantry is full, and everyone wants a different bake. Put the bakes, the oven order, the jobs, and the dietary swaps on a wheel, spin, and start baking.

It’s a Saturday morning in December, the pantry is full, and nobody agrees on what to bake. Your sister wants snickerdoodles, the ones with the crackled tops. Your partner wants sourdough, which is a two-day project and he knows it. Your kid wants cinnamon rolls, mostly because he wants to eat the icing straight from the bowl. You have butter, flour, sugar, and about six hours before someone has to leave for a party. You also have twenty minutes of arguing ahead of you, unless you stop it now.

Here’s the thing: the argument is not really about cookies versus bread. It is about too many open questions at once. What to bake, in what order, who does what, what about your cousin who can’t eat gluten. Close them one at a time with a What to Eat Wheel. Spin for the bake first. Then spin for the oven order. Then spin for the jobs. By the third spin the flour is out and the arguing is over.

Spin what to bake first, before the flour comes out

The first decision is the whole weekend, and it is the one that starts the most arguments. Cookies, cake, bread, pie, brownies, cinnamon rolls. Everyone has a favorite, and everyone can explain at length why their favorite is the right one for a December weekend. Meanwhile the butter is still in the fridge, the oven is cold, and the morning is slipping.

Put the bakes on the wheel and spin once. One spin, one bake. The wheel landed on cookies for us last December, and my partner, the sourdough campaigner, was quiet for about three seconds before he decided the cookies needed a brown butter base anyway. That is fine. A spin picks the main bake, and bakers can still add their own twist. What the spin stops is the twenty-minute debate before a single egg gets cracked.

I’ll be honest, part of why this works is that nobody has to give up in public. Nobody loses a vote. The wheel picked cookies, the bread fan can grumble at the wheel instead of at his sister, and the kitchen moves on. That matters more than adults like to admit, especially in December when everyone is already a little tired and a little sugar-deprived.

Spin the oven order, so nothing burns while something else proofs

Once the bake is set, the next problem is the oven. Most December weekends involve more than one bake, and every bake wants a different temperature. Bread wants a hot oven, 425 degrees. Cake wants a steady 350. Cookies want 375 and a short stay. If you wing the order, the bread goes in too late, the cake waits on the counter, and something burns while you are washing a bowl.

Load the bakes on the wheel and spin for the order. Bread first, because bread takes the longest and forgives a wait on the cooling rack. Cake second, while the oven is still hot and steady. Cookies last, because cookies are fast, and a tray of cookies can slide in while the cake cools and the kitchen smells like a bakery. That order came off our wheel two weekends in a row, and I have stopped fighting it.

One practical tip, whichever order the wheel picks: write it on a sticky note and put it on the oven door. Oven order is the kind of plan that lives in one person’s head and gets forgotten the minute the timer rings. A note on the oven door survives the noise, the music, and the third cup of coffee.

Spin who bakes what, so one person does not do everything

Here is the pattern in most kitchens: one person ends up doing the whole bake while everyone else tastes, or tastes while everyone else watches. The baker gets tired, the tasters get bored, and someone says “this isn’t fun anymore” while holding a wooden spoon.

Put the jobs on the wheel instead: mixer, oven, decorate, cleanup. Spin once per person, and that person owns that job until the bake is done. The mixer person measures and stirs. The oven person watches the temperature and the timer. The decorate person handles the icing, the sprinkles, the powdered sugar snow. The cleanup person washes the bowls as they empty, which is the job everyone skips and the job that saves the evening.

This also works when the bakers are different ages. Give the youngest baker the decorate job, because decorating is forgiving. A crooked icing line still looks like a snowdrift, and sprinkles cover a multitude of sins. Give the steadiest hand the oven job, because the oven job is the one where timing shows. Spin the flavor twist, so the third batch is not a copy of the first

A December baking weekend rarely stops at one batch. The first batch is the classic. The second batch is for the party. The third batch is where people run out of ideas and make the same cookies again, and then nobody is excited about the third batch.

Spin the twist before the second batch starts. Chocolate chips, dried cranberries, orange zest, chopped pecans, sea salt on top, crushed peppermint. One spin, one twist, and the second batch is a different cookie. Orange zest won our second batch last year, and I will say this for it: orange zest in a snickerdoodle sounds wrong until you taste it, and then it sounds like the plan all along.

The twist wheel also saves the pantry. Instead of buying a new bag of something for every batch, you spin from what is already on the shelf. Six twists, all already in the pantry, one spin. The third batch stops being a copy, and the pantry stops growing a new bag of pecans every December.

Spin the dietary swap, so one tray works for everyone

Every December table has someone with a food rule, and every December baker dreads the separate batch. Gluten-free for your cousin. Dairy-free for your aunt. Nut-free for the kid next door. Vegan for your brother’s new partner. Four rules, four separate batches, and a baker who spends the weekend making five versions of one cookie.

Put the swaps on the wheel and spin for the one tray that covers the most people. Gluten-free won our table last year, and it turned out to be the swap that mattered: the gluten-free batch used oat flour, and everyone ate it, even the people with no food rules at all. One tray, one recipe, no separate batch, no sad plate at the end of the table.

The rule that makes this work: spin the swap before you shop, not after you bake. A swap picked after the shopping is a swap that needs a second trip to the store, and a second trip in December is a trip nobody wants. Spin on Friday night, shop on Saturday morning, bake one tray that everyone can eat.

Spin what keeps until the party, and what has to wait

Not every bake survives until the party, and that is where December weekends go wrong. Cookies baked on Friday taste fine on Sunday. Bread baked on Friday tastes like a brick by Sunday. Cake with cream cheese icing needs the fridge, and a fridge full of party food has no room for a cake.

Spin the timing before the weekend starts. Cookies and brownies are Friday bakes, because they keep for days in a tin. Bread and cinnamon rolls are Sunday bakes, because they are best warm and they go stale fast. Cake is a Saturday bake, because it needs one night in the fridge and no more. The wheel picks the timing, and the timing picks the shopping list.

This is also the section where the freezer earns its keep. A double batch of cookie dough, scooped and frozen on Saturday, is a fresh tray of cookies on party day with ten minutes of work. Spin for the freezer batch while the first batch is in the oven, and the party-day bake is already half done before the weekend ends.

One rule that keeps the weekend fun

All of this only works with one rule, and it is worth saying out loud before the first spin: a spin stands, even when you do not like it. If the wheel gives you cookies and you wanted bread, you bake cookies. If it gives you the cleanup job and you wanted to decorate, the bowls are yours.

Sounds strict for a baking weekend, and it is the reason the weekend stays fun. The minute one person trades away a spin they did not like, everyone else wants to trade too, and you are back to twenty minutes of talking about a bake nobody has started. A spin that stands is a spin people trust, and a wheel people trust gets used again next December.

Try the holiday baking wheel

Load your bakes, your oven order, your jobs, and your dietary swaps onto the What to Eat Wheel, spin the bake first, and let the wheel settle the order, the jobs, and the timing before the first bag of flour opens. One spin ends the what-to-bake debate, and the rest of the weekend gets to be what it was supposed to be: warm cookies, a hot oven, and a tin full of something good by Sunday night.

Pumpkin Patch Wheel: Pick the Patch Trip

It is 9am on a Saturday in October, the car is packed, and two kids are already arguing about which pumpkin patch to visit. A pumpkin patch wheel settles the patch, the first pick, the pumpkin, and the cider stop before anyone gets in the car.

It is 9am on the second Saturday in October, and the car is already loaded with a blanket, two water bottles, and a tote bag for whatever comes home. Maya wants the big patch with the corn maze. Tom wants the small farm with the goats, because last year a goat ate his map and he still talks about it. Mom wants the patch that is twenty minutes away, not fifty. Dad wants any patch that opens before 10, because the parking lot fills up by 11 and he refuses to park in a field that turns to mud if it rains.

Nobody is wrong, and nobody is giving in. This is the part of the trip that usually eats half an hour in the driveway, with everyone checking their phones for reviews of places they have never been to. Put it on the Decision Wheel instead. One spin for the patch. One for who picks first. One for the pumpkin, one for the ride, one for the cider stop, and one for the photo. Six spins, and the car backs out of the driveway before the argument starts.

Spin for which patch to visit

Start with the patch, because the patch decides the drive, the price, and what else there is to do. A big patch has a maze and a long line for cider. A small farm has goats and a short walk from the car. Both are fine. The fight is never about which patch is better. It is about who got to choose last year.

Load the wheel with hillside, river, farm, u-pick, corner, and barn. Hillside means the big place on the hill with the corn maze and the wagon ride. River means the farm by the river with the goat pen and the low wooden bridge the kids cross twice. Farm means the working farm with the produce stand and the pumpkin field out back. U-pick means the field where you cut your own pumpkin off the vine with a borrowed pair of clippers. Corner means the lot on the corner in town, small and close, with pumpkins stacked on pallets. Barn means the red barn place with the hay bales stacked for photos and the cider stand inside.

Spin once, and that is the patch. No second spin because someone read a review in the car. The wheel lands on hillside, which suits Maya fine and disappoints Tom for about three minutes, until he hears the wagon ride is included and the goats are at the petting pen by the barn. Write the patch name on a sticky note and stick it to the dashboard. When the car is on the highway and someone asks “are we going to the goat place,” the note ends it.

Check the patch hours before you leave. Plenty of patches open at 10 on Saturdays and close the field at 4, and the last wagon ride leaves an hour before closing. A spin that picks a patch is no good if the patch is already closed when you get there.

Spin for who picks first

Every patch trip has a first pick, and the first pick matters more than adults admit. The first kid into the field walks past fifty pumpkins to find the one. The second kid gets the leftovers, or so it feels at age eight. Last year Tom picked first and took the giant one by the fence, and Maya talked about it until Thanksgiving.

Load the wheel with names, not categories. Maya, Tom, Dad, Mom, Nana, and Lily. Maya means the oldest picks first and sets the pace. Tom means the youngest picks first, which is fair once in a while and chaos the rest of the time. Dad means Dad picks first, which usually means he picks the one closest to the car. Mom means Mom picks first, which means she picks the one that will actually fit on the porch. Nana means Nana picks first, which means she picks the round one and tells a story about the pumpkin patch from when she was a kid. Lily means the cousin picks first, which is the right call when she only comes once a year.

Put the names on a Random Name Picker wheel if the group is bigger than six, or just load them on the Decision Wheel with the patch choices. Either way, one spin, and the name that lands walks into the field first. The wheel lands on Maya, and Tom accepts it because he picked first last year and the wheel remembers even when he pretends not to.

One rule keeps this spin honest: the first picker gets one pumpkin, not the whole field. First pick means first choice, not every choice. Say it before the gate, not after the first pumpkin is already in the wagon.

Spin for which pumpkin to take home

A pumpkin field looks the same from the gate: orange everywhere, and every kid runs to the biggest one. The spin gives each person a type, which turns a grab for the biggest pumpkin into a hunt for a shape.

Load the wheel with big, tall, round, warty, tiny, and white. Big means the heaviest pumpkin you can carry without help, which is the one that ends up on the porch and never gets carved because it is too heavy to move twice. Tall means a tall pumpkin with room for a long face, which suits a carving plan with teeth. Round means the classic round pumpkin, the one that looks like the drawing on the school worksheet. Warty means the bumpy green-orange pumpkin that looks strange and carves into a face with character. Tiny means the pumpkin that fits in one hand, which is the right pick for a kid under five and for a windowsill. White means the pale pumpkin that paints well, because orange paint on an orange pumpkin is a waste of a Saturday.

Spin once per person, and the type you land on is the type you hunt for. Tom lands on tall, and he spends twenty minutes walking rows looking for a tall one while Maya finds her round one in five minutes and sits on a hay bale eating a snack she smuggled in. That is the point of the spin. It slows the trip down in a good way, and the field stops being a race to the biggest pumpkin.

Check the stem before you buy. A pumpkin with a soft or missing stem rots from the top in a week, and a pumpkin that looks fine on Saturday can cave in by the next weekend. Pick it up by the sides, not the stem. A stem is a handle in pictures and a weak spot in real life.

Spin for hayride or corn maze

The pumpkin is picked, the wagon is loaded, and now the kids want everything else the patch sells. The ride spin picks one paid activity so the afternoon does not turn into a line for every ride on the property.

Load the wheel with hayride, maze, petting, slide, corn, and train. Hayride means the wagon ride around the field, which is slow, bumpy, and gives a view of the whole patch at once. Maze means the corn maze with a paper map at the entrance and a flag at the exit for kids who give up halfway. Petting means the goat pen, where the goats eat from your hand and one of them will try to eat your sleeve. Slide means the giant slide on the hill, which is fast and has a line that moves quicker than it looks. Corn means the corn pit, a box of dried corn kernels the younger kids play in for an hour while the older kids roll their eyes. Train means the barrel train that loops the field, which is the right pick when the group has a toddler who is done walking.

Spin once for the group, and that is the activity. If the wheel lands on hayride, the group rides the wagon and skips the maze line. If it lands on maze, the group takes the map and agrees on one rule first: nobody splits up to find a shortcut, because the shortcut is how a family spends forty minutes looking for each other in corn.

I’ll be honest, the maze is where most patch trips go sideways. Bring water, wear shoes you can walk in, and leave the stroller at the entrance if the maze paths are narrow. A stroller in a corn maze is a decision you regret at the first turn.

Spin for the cider stop

Every patch has a food stand, and every food stand has a line. The cider spin picks one treat per person so the group orders once and sits down, instead of six people ordering six different things while the line grows behind them.

Load the wheel with cider, donuts, corn, pie, apples, and fudge. Cider means a cup of hot cider, which is the right call on a cold morning and the drink every adult orders while pretending it was the kids’ idea. Donuts means cinnamon sugar donuts, warm from the fryer, which sell out by noon at most patches. Corn means kettle corn, which comes in a bag big enough to share and salty enough that everyone reaches in twice. Pie means a slice of pumpkin pie from the stand, which is the right pick when the group is tired and wants to sit at a picnic table. Apples means a bag of apples from the stand, which is the snack that survives the car ride home. Fudge means pumpkin fudge, which is sweet enough that one piece per person is plenty.

Spin once per person, and the treat you land on is the treat you order. The wheel lands on cider for Dad, donuts for Maya, and kettle corn for Tom, which means the order takes one minute and the group sits down with food while the next family is still deciding. Order the donuts early if the stand has a fryer. When the sign says sold out, no spin fixes it.

If the group is still hungry after the patch, settle dinner on the way home with the What to Eat Wheel instead of starting a second argument in the car. One spin for dinner, and the patch trip ends on a full stomach.

Spin for the photo spot

Someone will want a photo, and the photo argument is the quiet one: where to stand, who holds the pumpkin, and whether the sun is in everyone’s eyes. The photo spin picks the spot so the group takes one photo and moves on, instead of taking twenty photos in five spots while the kids lose interest.

Load the wheel with gate, pile, wheelbarrow, barn, sunflowers, and scarecrow. Gate means the wooden arch at the entrance, which is the classic shot and the one with the line. Pile means the big pumpkin pile by the stand, where everyone sits on pumpkins and holds one in their lap. Wheelbarrow means the wheelbarrow full of small pumpkins, which suits a photo of the younger kids. Barn means the red barn wall, which gives a clean background and shade on a bright day. Sunflowers means the sunflower row at the edge of the field, which blooms late into October at some patches and looks good behind a family photo. Scarecrow means the scarecrow by the maze entrance, which is the photo the kids ask for themselves.

Spin once, walk to the spot, and take the photo. One rule helps: the person who picks the pumpkin holds it in the photo, and the person who drove gets to stand in the shade. A photo where everyone is squinting is a photo nobody frames, and the spin does not fix the sun. Stand with the sun behind the camera, not behind the family.

Spin for the one rule for the ride home

The trip is not over at the gate. The ride home has its own argument, and it starts the minute the car doors close: can we carve tonight, can we paint instead, whose pumpkin goes on the porch, and who carries the heavy one inside.

Load the wheel with one each, carve tonight, porch vote, paint, donate, and photo first. One each means each person keeps one pumpkin, and the extras stay at the patch, which is the rule that ends the backseat bargaining. Carve tonight means the carving happens the same evening, with newspaper on the table and seeds in a bowl for roasting. Porch vote means the family votes on which pumpkin goes by the front door and which one goes by the garage. Paint means the pumpkins get painted instead of carved, which is the right call when the group has a toddler or a pumpkin with a thin wall. Donate means the extra pumpkins go to a neighbor or a classroom, which is a good end for a patch trip that bought too many. Photo first means the porch photo happens before any carving, because a carved pumpkin on the porch is a photo nobody takes.

Spin once in the parking lot, before the car starts. The wheel lands on carve tonight, which means the newspaper goes down the minute the pumpkins come inside, and nobody leaves a pumpkin in the trunk overnight. A pumpkin left in a warm trunk softens fast, and a soft pumpkin carves badly.

Sounds silly, works great. One spin in the parking lot saves the evening from turning into a second negotiation after a long day outside.

Try the pumpkin patch wheel

The patch trip starts in the driveway, and the driveway is where the wheel earns its keep. Load the Decision Wheel with hillside, river, farm, u-pick, corner, and barn, and spin for the patch before anyone gets in the car. If the group is bigger than the pumpkin pile, settle who picks first with the Random Name Picker before the gate. If dinner on the way home turns into a second debate, put that call on the What to Eat Wheel and drive.

Spin for the patch today. Pick one pumpkin per person, ride the wagon, drink the cider, and take the photo at the spot the wheel picked. The pumpkins will be on the porch by dark, and nobody will remember who argued about the patch in the driveway.

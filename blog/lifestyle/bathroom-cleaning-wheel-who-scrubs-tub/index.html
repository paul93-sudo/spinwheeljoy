Bathroom Cleaning Wheel: Who Scrubs the Tub This Week

Saturday morning, the tub has a ring, the toilet needs a scrub, and everyone in the house has somewhere else to be. One spin hands out the tub, the toilet, the sink, and the mirror.

It is 9:04 on a Saturday morning. The tub has a grey ring around the waterline from last night’s baths, the toilet has a spot under the rim that nobody wants to look at too closely, and the mirror still has toothpaste flecks on it from Thursday. Mara is standing in the doorway with her arms crossed. Ben has gone very quiet in his room, which is how you know he heard the word bathroom.

Here is the thing about the bathroom. It is the smallest room in the house, and it gets dirty faster than any other room. Four people use it every day, and nobody cleans it unless someone says so out loud. So the ring stays, the flecks stay, and by Sunday the whole room smells like damp towels.

That is what a spin on the Chore Wheel is for. Load the four jobs, put the names on the wheel, spin once for each job, and the bathroom has a plan before anyone can slip out the back door. No meeting. No pointing. One spin each, and the room is clean by lunch.

Four jobs, one spin each

Load the Chore Wheel with the four jobs and spin for them in order. Tub means the whole tub: the ring, the walls, the drain cover, and a rinse. Toilet means the bowl, under the rim, the seat, the base, and the floor around it. Sink means the basin, the faucet handles, and the counter. Mirror means the glass and the light fixture above it, because toothpaste gets everywhere.

On a house of five or six, the wheel holds six. Floor gets its own spin, because a bathroom floor collects hair and dust in the corners, and towels get one too, because someone has to hang fresh ones and toss the damp ones in the wash. Six jobs, six spins, and the room is done in one pass.

Spin for the jobs in order. Tub first, because it takes the longest and the person who gets it needs the hot water running. Then toilet, then sink, then mirror. The mirror spin comes last, because it takes three minutes and the person who gets it can finish while the tub is still draining. Order matters here. A mirror cleaner with a wet tub to walk around is a person with wet socks.

One person can hold two jobs on a small morning. A house of two spins twice each. A house of four spins once each. The wheel does not care how many people there are. It cares that every job has a name on it before anyone leaves the room.

The mirror job is the one people forget to count, and it is the one that makes the bathroom look done. A sink with toothpaste in it is not a clean bathroom, even if the tub is spotless. Wipe the glass, wipe the counter, and the room looks finished. Three minutes, and the whole morning feels different.

The tub job, and why the ring fights back

Everyone thinks the toilet is the worst job. I will be honest, the tub runs it close. The ring sits right at the waterline, and it does not come off with one wipe. It takes soap, hot water, and a brush, and the person with the brush has to lean over the edge the whole time.

The tub person starts with hot water. Run the shower for a minute, let the steam loosen the ring, then put soap on the brush and work from the top down. The walls first, then the ring, then the floor of the tub, then the drain cover. Top down, because the dirty water runs down and you rinse it once at the end.

Never scrub a dry tub. A dry tub fights back, and the person scrubbing it ends up with a sore arm and a ring that is still there. Hot water first, then the brush. If the ring survives, a paste of baking soda and water sits on it for five minutes while the person does the walls, and the brush takes it off before the rinse.

The tub spin comes with a trade. The person who scrubs the tub gets the mirror next week, which is the shortest job on the wheel. Fair is fair. The tub is the longest job this week, so it pays for the shortest job next week.

The tub person gets the shower head to rinse. That is the trade for leaning over the edge. Rinse from the top, watch the soap run to the drain, and the job is done when the water runs clear. Ten minutes, maybe twelve, and the tub is white again.

The toilet job nobody trades for

Nobody volunteers for the toilet. The toilet sits there while the rest of the bathroom gets clean around it, and by noon it is the only dirty thing left in the room. That is why the wheel hands it out. Nobody asks for volunteers, because nobody raises a hand.

So the toilet gets its own spin. One name, one toilet, and the rest of the house stays out of it. The person who gets it puts on the rubber gloves first, then lifts the seat, then works the brush under the rim where the spots hide. The rim first, then the bowl, then the seat, then the base, then the floor around the bottom. The floor around the base is part of the job, and it is the part people skip.

The toilet person gets the gloves and the good brush. That is the trade. The brush lives in its holder by the toilet, and it gets rinsed and put back when the job is done, not left in the bowl. A brush left in the bowl is a brush nobody wants to touch next week.

Two rules keep the toilet job short. First, the gloves go on before the brush comes out, not after. Second, the seat gets wiped on both sides, because the underside is where the spots land.

If the brush is falling apart, toss it and open a fresh one. The toilet person is allowed to call for a new brush, new gloves, or a second pair of hands for the base, and nobody gets to argue about it. The toilet is the toilet person’s station for ten minutes.

The sink and mirror: small jobs that count

The sink and mirror take five minutes together, and they are the jobs that make the bathroom look clean. A guest walks in, sees a clean mirror and a clean sink, and thinks the whole room is clean. A guest walks in, sees toothpaste flecks on the glass, and thinks nobody cleans in this house. Five minutes decides which one it is.

The sink person clears the counter first. Toothbrushes in the cup, hair out of the drain, soap dish wiped, faucet handles wiped. The drain gets the hair out before the last wipe, not after, because nobody wants to fish hair out with clean hands.

The mirror person sprays and wipes top to bottom. Top to bottom, because the drips run down and you catch them on the way. A mirror wiped in circles ends up with streaks, and a mirror with streaks is a mirror that needs wiping again. One pass, top to bottom, and the glass is clear.

The light fixture above the mirror is part of the job too. Toothpaste gets up there, somehow, and a dusty bulb makes the whole room look dim. One wipe, and the light comes back.

These are the jobs kids can do. A nine-year-old can wipe a mirror and clear a sink, and a twelve-year-old can do both in five minutes flat. Give a kid a real job with a real spin, and the kid does it. Give a kid a made-up job, and the kid knows.

The one-scrub rule

The one-scrub rule is what keeps the bathroom from eating the whole morning. One scrub, in one go, and it counts. Nobody comes back an hour later to redo the tub because someone else thinks the ring could be cleaner. One real scrub, and the job is done.

The rule has a clear line. A scrub means a wet surface, soap, and a brush, then a rinse. A wipe with a dry cloth is not a scrub. A splash of water is not a scrub. The person who holds the job decides when the scrub is real, and the rest of the house takes their word for it.

The rule covers the mornings when someone is out late or feeling sick. Their job goes back on the wheel, someone else spins for it, and they owe the mirror job the next week. One week of owing, no more. An IOU that runs for a month is not an IOU, it is a new chore chart with one name missing from it.

Write the rule on the inside of the bathroom door if it helps: one spin, one job, one scrub. The bathroom closes when the last spin is done, and the next morning starts with a clean tub instead of last week’s ring.

The rule also covers the ring that survives. If the ring is still there after one real scrub, it gets a soak next week, not a second scrub today. A ring that needs a soak is a job for next Saturday, and the wheel hands it to someone new.

The weekly rotation, and who keeps track

The tub takes twelve minutes. The mirror takes three. On any given week, somebody gets the long job and somebody gets the short one, and that is fine, as long as it is not the same somebody every week. The wheel forgets, so the house has to remember.

A sheet on the inside of the bathroom door does the remembering. Four columns, one for each job, and a tick next to the name. At the end of the month, anyone can see who scrubbed the tub three times and who never touched the toilet. The sheet does not argue. It just sits there with the ticks on it.

Spin the names first and the jobs second, or the jobs first and the names second. Either way works. The names go on the wheel one at a time, and a spin on the Random Name Picker settles the second pick when the wheel has already handed out the first job and two names are left.

Four weeks, four jobs, and everyone has done all of them. That is the whole point of the rotation. Nobody owns the toilet, and nobody owns the mirror. The jobs move around the house, and the gross jobs land on a different person each week.

When the house grows to five or six, floor and towels join the wheel. Floor takes eight minutes with a mop and a bucket, and towels take two minutes of hanging fresh ones. Six jobs, six weeks, and the rotation still works. The sheet gets two more columns, and the ticks keep going.

Guests, kids, and the Saturday morning rule

Guests do not spin. Guests get the couch and a second cup of coffee, and the house handles the bathroom. A guest who offers to scrub gets a polite no and a clean towel. The wheel is for the people who live there, not the people who came for breakfast.

Kids spin for the sink and the mirror, and the adults take the tub and the toilet until the kids are older. A ten-year-old can wipe a mirror. A ten-year-old should not be scrubbing a toilet, and the wheel knows it. The jobs wait until the kid is tall enough to reach the faucet without a step stool and old enough to wear the gloves without a fuss.

The Saturday morning rule is the one that holds it all together. The bathroom gets done before lunch, before screens, before anyone leaves the house. One spin per job, and the room is clean by 11. A bathroom that closes clean on Saturday stays clean until Tuesday, and Tuesday leaves a toothpaste fleck and a damp towel, not a ring.

And when the house cannot agree on Saturday or Sunday in the first place, a spin on the Decision Wheel settles it before anyone picks up a brush. One spin, one answer, and the bathroom gets done on time.

Try it Saturday morning

Load the Chore Wheel with the four jobs, or six on a big week, and the names of the people in the house. Spin once for the tub, once for the toilet, once for the sink, once for the mirror, and once for the floor if it is on the wheel. Five spins, and the bathroom has a plan before anyone can slip out the back door.

If the wheel hands you the tub, run the hot water first and let it steam. If it hands you the toilet, put the gloves on and start under the rim. If it hands you the mirror, you are done in three minutes and the glass is yours. And if it hands you the sink, clear the counter, wipe the handles, and enjoy the five minutes of hot water. The bathroom was going to need cleaning anyway. The wheel just made it fair.

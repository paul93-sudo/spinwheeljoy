Thanksgiving Breakfast Wheel: Fuel Up Before the Feast

It is 8 in the morning on Thanksgiving, the turkey still needs four hours, and half the house is already hungry while the other half says to wait for dinner. A Thanksgiving breakfast wheel settles light or hearty, who cooks, what time to eat, and the coffee rule, so nobody is stuffed by 3pm and nobody is starving at noon.

It is 8:05 on Thanksgiving morning, and the kitchen already smells like sage and butter. The turkey went into the oven at 7, which means dinner is still seven hours away. Uncle Ray is standing in front of the fridge eating cold cranberry sauce straight from the bowl with a spoon. Maya is nine and says she is starving. Tom is sixteen and says he is saving room, which is a lie, because Tom ate two granola bars in the car on the way here and will eat again in an hour.

Here’s the thing. Nobody argues about Thanksgiving dinner. Dinner is planned down to the gravy boat. Breakfast is the meal nobody planned, and that is why it turns into a negotiation at the worst possible time. Mom wants something light so nobody ruins dinner. Dad wants something hearty because he got up at 6 to put the turkey in and he is hungry now. Grandma wants everyone to sit down together, which is sweet and also impossible when half the house is still in pajamas and the other half is already peeling potatoes.

Put it on the What to Eat Wheel instead. One spin for light or hearty. One for who cooks. One for what time to eat. One for the coffee rule. One for the kids. One for the make-ahead dish. One for who cleans up before the guests arrive. Seven spins, and breakfast happens before the turkey timer goes off, instead of after three separate arguments about toast.

Spin for light or hearty

Start here, because this is the real fight, and it is not really about food. Light means fruit, yogurt, toast, and coffee, and it means everyone still has room at 3pm. Hearty means pancakes, eggs, sausage, and potatoes, and it means the cook who got up early gets a real meal for once. Both sides have a point. Mom is right that a full breakfast at 9 makes a 3pm dinner feel like a chore. Dad is right that a bowl of fruit after a 6am start is not breakfast, it is a snack with good intentions.

Load the wheel with pancakes, casserole, cereal, fruit, oatmeal, and bagels. Pancakes means a stack of pancakes with butter and syrup, which is the right pick when kids are up early and need something that feels like a holiday, because pancakes take twenty minutes and keep a table of kids quiet for at least that long. Casserole means an egg and sausage casserole from the fridge, which suits a house with a crowd, because a casserole feeds twelve people from one pan and the cook only washes one pan. Cereal means cold cereal and milk, which is the right pick when the kitchen is already full of turkey prep, because cereal needs no stove and no counter space. Fruit means a fruit plate with yogurt, which suits a house where dinner is the main event, because fruit fills a stomach without filling it up for the day. Oatmeal means a pot of oatmeal with brown sugar and apples, which is the right pick on a cold morning, because oatmeal is warm and cheap and a pot of it feeds whoever wanders in. Bagels means bagels with cream cheese, which suits a house with late risers, because bagels wait on the counter without drying out and nobody has to time anything.

Spin once, and that is the breakfast plan. The wheel lands on pancakes, which means Dad wins this round and the griddle comes out. Mom gets her say in the next spin, because the wheel does not stop at one choice. One rule keeps this spin honest: the light-or-hearty call is made once, before anyone opens the fridge. A house that reopens the question at 9:30 ends up cooking pancakes and fruit and oatmeal, and then dinner really is ruined.

Spin for who cooks

The food is picked, and now someone has to cook it. On Thanksgiving this question is loaded, because the person who cooks breakfast is usually also the person who cooks dinner, and that person is already tired at 8 in the morning. In our house that person is Mom, and Mom cooked last year, and the year before, and she did not complain, which is exactly why someone else should cook this year.

Load the wheel with Mom, Dad, Grandma, kids, takeout, and rotate. Mom means Mom cooks, which is the right pick only if Mom actually wants the kitchen to herself for an hour, because some cooks like the quiet before the house wakes up. Dad means Dad cooks, which suits a house where Dad is already up, because Dad is up anyway and the griddle is already hot from his own coffee routine. Grandma means Grandma cooks, which is the right pick when Grandma brought her own pancake recipe and has been waiting all week to use it, because a recipe a grandparent wants to cook is a gift, not a chore. Kids means the kids cook with one adult watching, which suits a house with a nine-year-old who wants to flip pancakes, because a kid who flips her own pancake eats it without a single complaint about the shape. Takeout means someone drives to the diner and brings back breakfast sandwiches, which is the right pick when the kitchen is full of turkey prep and there is no stove space left. Rotate means the cook changes every hour, which suits a house where everyone wants to help and nobody wants the whole job.

Spin once, and that is the cook plan. The wheel lands on Dad, which means Dad takes the griddle and Mom sits down with coffee for the first time that morning. One rule helps: the cook does not also clean up. A cook who cooks and cleans ends up doing two jobs while everyone else eats, and that is how a holiday morning turns sour before noon.

Spin for what time to eat

The cook is picked, and now the time needs a call. This sounds simple until you count the house. The early risers are up at 7 and hungry by 8. The teenagers sleep until 10 and then want breakfast at 11, which is lunch in every practical sense. Grandma wants everyone at one table at one time. The turkey timer does not care about any of this.

Load the wheel with 8 AM, 9 AM, 10 AM, 11 AM, noon, and skip. 8 AM means breakfast at 8, which is the right pick when the cook is up early anyway and the kids are already circling the kitchen, because an 8am breakfast ends before the potato peeling starts. 9 AM means breakfast at 9, which suits most houses, because 9 is late enough for the teenagers to drag themselves downstairs and early enough that dinner at 3 still feels like dinner. 10 AM means breakfast at 10, which is the right pick when the house slept in after a late Wednesday night, because a 10am breakfast is really brunch and nobody pretends otherwise. 11 AM means breakfast at 11, which suits a house with a late dinner, because an 11am meal pushes dinner to 4 without anyone going hungry in between. Noon means breakfast at noon, which is the right pick only if dinner moved to 5 or later, because a noon breakfast and a 3pm dinner are the same meal with a nap in between. Skip means no sit-down breakfast at all, just snacks until dinner, which suits a small house where everyone grazes, because a grazing house does not need a table plan.

Spin once, and that is the time plan. The wheel lands on 9 AM, which means the teenagers get one wake-up call at 8:30 and no second call. One rule helps: the time is written on a sticky note on the fridge. A time that lives only in someone’s head gets argued about at 8:45, and a sticky note does not argue back.

Spin for the coffee rule

The time is set, and now coffee needs a rule. Coffee on Thanksgiving morning is not a drink, it is a system, and every house has a different one. Dad drinks two cups before he talks to anyone. Uncle Ray drinks decaf and pretends it counts. Maya wants hot cocoa and calls it coffee because she is nine and wants to sit at the adult table. Tom drinks an energy drink, which is a choice nobody in the house approves of and Tom does anyway.

Load the wheel with coffee, tea, cocoa, juice, water, and decaf. Coffee means a full pot of regular coffee, which is the right pick for a house full of early risers, because a full pot stops the line at the coffee maker and lets the cook actually cook. Tea means a kettle of tea, which suits a house where half the guests do not drink coffee, because tea covers the non-coffee drinkers without a second pot. Cocoa means hot cocoa for the table, which is the right pick when kids outnumber adults, because cocoa makes a kid feel included at a grown-up table. Juice means orange juice and coffee on the side, which suits a house with a big breakfast, because juice pairs with pancakes and the coffee drinkers still get their pot. Water means water first, coffee second, which is the right pick after a late night, because a glass of water before coffee keeps the morning headache away. Decaf means a pot of decaf for everyone, which suits a house where dinner runs late, because decaf at 9 does not keep anyone up at midnight.

Spin once, and that is the coffee plan. The wheel lands on coffee, which means a full pot goes on at 8:15 and Uncle Ray gets his decaf from a separate small pot so nobody has to hear about it. One rule helps: the coffee pot stays full until 10. A pot that runs dry at 9:20 sends four people to the store for a coffee run, and a coffee run on Thanksgiving morning is a forty-minute errand disguised as a five-minute trip.

Spin for the kids table breakfast

Coffee is settled, and now the kids need their own call. Kids do not eat Thanksgiving breakfast the way adults do. Adults sit, talk, and eat slowly. Kids eat two bites, run off, come back, eat one more bite, and ask when dinner is. A plan that expects kids to sit for forty minutes will fail by minute six, and then someone will spend the morning chasing a nine-year-old around the table with a fork.

Load the wheel with pancakes, cereal, fruit, toast, eggs, and snack plate. Pancakes means pancakes for the kids table, which is the right pick when the kids cook helped flip them, because a kid eats the pancake she flipped. Cereal means cereal in bowls the kids can carry, which suits a house where kids eat in shifts, because a bowl of cereal travels to the living room without a plate breaking. Fruit means a fruit plate on the kids table, which is the right pick when dinner is the big meal, because fruit keeps kids going without a sugar crash at 11. Toast means toast with butter and jam, which suits a house with picky eaters, because toast is the one breakfast every picky eater agrees on. Eggs means scrambled eggs in a big bowl, which is the right pick when the cook already has eggs out for the casserole, because one more pan of eggs costs nothing. Snack plate means a plate of cheese, crackers, and grapes the kids pick at all morning, which suits a house with grazers, because a snack plate never gets cold and never needs reheating.

Spin once, and that is the kids plan. The wheel lands on pancakes again, which is fine, because the kids table gets its own stack and the adults stop negotiating over who gets the last pancake. One rule helps: the kids eat first, before the adults sit down. Kids who eat first stop asking when breakfast is, and adults who eat second get a quiet table for once.

Spin for the make-ahead dish

The kids are fed, and now the smart move needs a call. The best Thanksgiving breakfast dish is the one nobody cooks on Thanksgiving morning. A casserole assembled on Wednesday night goes into the oven on Thursday and comes out while the cook drinks coffee. A dish that needs chopping, mixing, and timing on Thursday morning competes with the turkey for stove space, and the turkey always wins that fight.

Load the wheel with casserole, overnight oats, muffins, fruit salad, strata, and store-bought. Casserole means an egg casserole made Wednesday night, which is the right pick for a crowd, because a casserole goes from fridge to oven in one move. Overnight oats means oats soaked overnight in jars, which suits a small house, because jars wait in the fridge and nobody cooks anything. Muffins means muffins baked Wednesday night, which is the right pick when the oven is free on Wednesday, because muffins reheat in five minutes on Thursday. Fruit salad means a fruit salad cut Wednesday night, which suits a house that wants something light, because fruit salad keeps overnight and needs no heat. Strata means a bread and egg strata soaked overnight, which is the right pick when there is leftover bread, because strata turns stale bread into breakfast. Store-bought means pastries from the bakery, which suits a house that ran out of Wednesday, because a bakery box on Thursday morning is still a plan.

Spin once, and that is the make-ahead plan. The wheel lands on casserole, which means Wednesday night ends with a pan in the fridge and Thursday morning starts with an oven timer instead of a cutting board. One rule helps: the make-ahead dish is the only dish the cook makes on Thursday morning. A cook who makes two dishes on Thursday morning is really making dinner prep late, and dinner prep does not forgive lateness.

Spin for who cleans up before guests arrive

Breakfast is eaten, and now the kitchen needs to be clean before the guests arrive at noon. This is the spin everyone forgets, and it is the one that matters most, because a kitchen full of pancake plates at noon is a kitchen where nobody can set out the appetizers. Last year the plates sat until 2, and the appetizers went on top of the mail pile, which is a sentence no host wants to say out loud.

Load the wheel with Dad, Mom, kids, all, later, and host. Dad means Dad cleans, which is the right pick when Dad cooked, because a cook who cleans his own pans knows where everything goes. Mom means Mom cleans, which suits a house where Mom likes the kitchen reset her own way, because some people clean faster alone than with helpers. Kids means the kids clear the table, which is the right pick when the kids ate first, because kids who ate first can clear while the adults finish coffee. All means everyone cleans for fifteen minutes, which suits a big house, because fifteen minutes with six people beats an hour with one person. Later means cleanup waits until after dinner, which is the right pick only if the guests arrive after 2, because a dirty kitchen at noon is a problem and a dirty kitchen at 2 is a disaster. Host means the host cleans no matter who cooked, which suits a house where the host wants control of the kitchen before guests arrive, because a host who resets the kitchen knows where the serving dishes are.

Spin once, and that is the cleanup plan. The wheel lands on all, which means everyone clears for fifteen minutes, the dishwasher runs, the counters get wiped, and the appetizers have a clean table at noon. One rule helps: cleanup starts the minute the last person stands up. A table that waits ten minutes for cleanup waits an hour, because ten minutes is exactly long enough for everyone to leave the room.

Try the Thanksgiving breakfast wheel

Thanksgiving dinner gets all the planning, and breakfast gets none, which is backwards, because breakfast is the meal that sets the mood for the whole day. A house that argues about breakfast at 8 is a house that starts dinner prep annoyed. A house that spins seven times and eats at 9 starts dinner prep fed, caffeinated, and on time.

Load the What to Eat Wheel with your own light-or-hearty picks, your own cook rotation, and your own coffee rule. If the morning has a second decision hiding in it, like who drives to the store for the forgotten cranberries, hand that one to the Decision Wheel too. And if the house cannot agree on who gets the first shower before guests arrive, settle that with the Random Name Picker before it becomes a third argument. Seven spins, one sticky note on the fridge, and breakfast is done before the turkey timer goes off.

Christmas Tree Decorating Wheel: Pick the Tree Look

It is 6:15 on a Saturday in early December, the tree is up in the corner, the lights are tangled in the box, and Mark, Jen, Mia, and Leo cannot agree on a theme, a topper, or who climbs the ladder. A tree decorating wheel settles the theme, the lights, the topper, the garland, the ornament order, who hangs what, and the skirt, so the tree gets finished before bedtime.

It is 6:15 on a Saturday in early December, and the tree stands bare in the corner of the living room. Mark carried it in from the car an hour ago. Jen straightened it twice. Mia is nine and has already opened the ornament box. Leo is six and has already asked three times when the star goes on. The lights sit tangled in last year’s box, the ornament box holds four kinds of decorations that do not match, and the family has one evening to finish the tree, because tomorrow the grandparents arrive and the tree is the first thing they will see.

Here’s the thing. Nobody in this house disagrees about having a tree. They disagree about what the tree should look like. Mark wants white lights and a star. Jen wants colored lights and an angel she grew up with. Mia wants every ornament she ever made in school on the front, where everyone can see them. Leo wants the candy canes low, where he can reach them. Four people, one tree, and every choice turns into a small vote that nobody wins, because the person who cares most about that choice argues longest.

Put it on the Decision Wheel instead. One spin for the theme. One for the lights. One for the topper. One for the garland. One for the order the ornaments go on. One for who hangs what. One for the skirt under the tree. Seven spins, and the tree has a plan before anyone touches a branch, because the wheel picks and the hands follow.

Spin for the theme

Start here, because the theme settles every later choice. A tree with no theme ends up with red balls next to blue snowflakes next to a school photo ornament, and the tree looks like four small trees pushed together. A theme picked in advance gives every later spin a direction, and it stops the argument about whether the angel matches the lights, because the theme already answered it.

Load the wheel with classic, cozy, white, kids, gold, and blue. Classic means red balls, gold ribbon, and a star, which is the right pick for a family that wants the tree to look like the trees in old photos, because red and gold go with almost any living room. Cozy means warm lights, wooden ornaments, and a red ribbon, which is the right pick for a house that runs cold in December, because a cozy tree makes the room feel warm before the heater catches up. White means white and silver ornaments with white lights, which is the right pick for a modern room with light furniture, because white ornaments stand out against green branches without adding another color. Kids means every school ornament on the front, paper chains, and candy canes low, which is the right pick for a house with small kids, because a kids tree is a tree the kids can point at and say they helped build. Gold means gold balls, gold ribbon, and a gold star, which is the right pick for a formal living room, because gold reads as one color from across the room and the tree looks planned. Blue means blue and silver ornaments with white lights, which is the right pick for a house that already owns blue ornaments, because blue ornaments bought over ten years should get used, not stored.

Spin once, and that is the theme. The wheel lands on cozy, which means warm lights, wooden ornaments, and a red ribbon for this tree, and the blue snowflakes stay in the box for a year when blue wins. One rule keeps this spin honest: the theme covers the whole tree, not one side. A tree that is cozy on the front and blue on the back is two themes, and two themes is the same as no theme.

Spin for the lights

The theme is picked, and now the lights need a call. Lights sound simple until the box opens and three strands come out tangled, one strand has a dead bulb, and nobody remembers which strand worked last year. A light color picked in advance keeps the tree from getting two kinds of lights, because a tree with warm lights on one side and white lights on the other looks like two trees wired together.

Load the wheel with warm, white, multi, blue, off, and twinkle. Warm means warm white bulbs, which is the right pick for a cozy theme, because warm bulbs make green branches look deep green and the room looks lit by lamps, not by a screen. White means cool white bulbs, which is the right pick for a white or blue theme, because cool white bulbs match silver ornaments and the tree looks crisp in daylight. Multi means colored bulbs in red, green, blue, and yellow, which is the right pick for a kids tree, because colored bulbs are the first thing a kid notices from the doorway. Blue means blue bulbs only, which is the right pick for a blue theme, because blue bulbs on a green tree look cold in a good way, like a winter night. Off means no lights at all, which is the right pick for a tree that sits by a bright window, because a tree in full sun does not need bulbs to be seen. Twinkle means bulbs that blink on a timer, which is the right pick for a party night, because blinking lights draw eyes to the tree the second guests walk in.

Spin once, and that is the light plan. The wheel lands on warm, which means two strands of warm white bulbs go on this tree, and the colored strand goes back in the box. One rule helps: test each strand on the floor before it goes on the tree. A strand tested on the floor takes two minutes. A strand tested on the tree takes twenty minutes and a ladder.

Spin for the topper

The lights are on, and now the topper needs a call. The topper is the item every guest looks at first, and it is the item that causes the most trouble, because the star and the angel both sit in the same box and both have a person who loves them. A topper picked by a spin ends the debate before it starts, because the wheel has no favorite and nobody can argue with a spin.

Load the wheel with star, angel, bow, flake, hat, and none. Star means a gold star on the top branch, which is the right pick for a classic or cozy tree, because a star sits straight on a top branch and stays put all month. Angel means a small angel with cloth wings, which is the right pick for a family that owns an angel from childhood, because an angel on top of the tree carries a memory that a new star cannot. Bow means a big ribbon bow tied to the top, which is the right pick for a tree with ribbon garland, because a bow on top matches the ribbon around the tree and the tree looks finished from the doorway. Flake means a large snowflake ornament wired to the top, which is the right pick for a white or blue theme, because a snowflake topper matches silver ornaments better than a gold star does. Hat means a small Santa hat perched on the top branch, which is the right pick for a kids tree, because a hat on top makes the kids laugh and the tree feels like theirs. None means no topper at all, which is the right pick for a short tree under a low ceiling, because a topper that brushes the ceiling bends the top branch and the tree looks squashed.

Spin once, and that is the topper. The wheel lands on star, which means the gold star goes on this tree, and the angel stays in the box for a year when star wins. One rule helps: the topper goes on last, after the lights and the garland. A topper put on first gets knocked off three times before the tree is done, and a topper knocked off three times ends up with a bent point.

Spin for the garland

The topper is settled, and now the garland needs a call. Garland is the item people forget until the ornaments are on, and then the garland has nowhere to go, because every branch already holds an ornament and the garland ends up draped over the front like a rope. A garland picked in advance goes on before the ornaments, and the ornaments hang over it, which is how a tree ends up looking layered instead of crowded.

Load the wheel with beads, ribbon, popcorn, tinsel, none, and lights. Beads means a string of wooden or glass beads, which is the right pick for a cozy tree, because beads add color without covering branches and a bead strand is easy to wind. Ribbon means a wide ribbon wound around the tree in swags, which is the right pick for a classic or cozy tree, because ribbon fills gaps between branches and the tree looks full even where the branches are thin. Popcorn means a popcorn string made at the kitchen table, which is the right pick for a family afternoon, because stringing popcorn takes an hour and the family spends that hour together at the table. Tinsel means thin silver strands draped over branches, which is the right pick for a house without pets, because tinsel and cats do not mix and a house with a cat should skip it. None means no garland at all, which is the right pick for a tree with a lot of ornaments, because a tree covered in ornaments has no room for garland and garland on top of ornaments looks like clutter. Lights means a second strand of lights used as garland, which is the right pick for a dark corner, because a second strand of lights brightens a tree that sits far from a window.

Spin once, and that is the garland plan. The wheel lands on ribbon, which means one wide red ribbon winds around this tree in swags, and the bead strand stays in the box. One rule helps: wind the garland from the top down, not from the bottom up. A garland wound from the top down keeps even swags all the way down. A garland wound from the bottom up runs out of ribbon halfway and the top looks bare.

Spin for the ornament order

The garland is on, and now the order needs a call. Ornament order sounds fussy until the box opens and Mia hangs her school photo ornament first, Leo hangs a candy cane second, and the big red balls end up in the back, because the front branches filled up before the big ornaments came out. An order picked in advance keeps the front branches open for the ornaments that deserve them, and it keeps the tree from looking front-heavy on one side.

Load the wheel with big first, kids first, heirloom last, color groups, one box at a time, and free-for-all. Big first means the big balls go on first, spread around the whole tree, which is the right pick for most trees, because big ornaments set the shape and small ornaments fill the gaps after. Kids first means the school ornaments go on first, on the front branches, which is the right pick for a kids tree, because the school ornaments are the ones the kids ask about and they deserve the front. Heirloom last means the old ornaments go on last, in safe spots, which is the right pick for a family with ornaments from grandparents, because an heirloom hung last goes where it cannot fall and nobody bumps it while hanging the rest. Color groups means one color goes on at a time, which is the right pick for a tree with a strict color theme, because one color at a time shows gaps before the next color covers them. One box at a time means the family opens one box, hangs it all, then opens the next, which is the right pick for a small living room, because one open box at a time keeps the floor clear. Free-for-all means everyone grabs ornaments and hangs them wherever they fit, which is the right pick for a relaxed evening, because a free-for-all is fast and the tree still looks fine when the lights go on.

Spin once, and that is the order. The wheel lands on big first, which means the big red and gold balls go on first, spread around the whole tree, and the school ornaments go on after, in the gaps near the front. One rule helps: step back after each round of ornaments. A tree checked from the doorway after each round shows a bare side before the box is empty, and a bare side found early is easy to fix.

Spin for who hangs what

The order is set, and now one spin decides who hangs what. This sounds like a chore chart, and it is, but it is also the part that keeps the peace. When Mark hangs everything, Jen watches and corrects. When Mia hangs everything, Leo complains that he got nothing. When the job is split by a spin, everyone gets a zone and nobody stands around holding an ornament with nowhere to put it.

Load the wheel with dad top, mom middle, kids bottom, swap, ladder jobs, and sides. Dad top means Mark handles the top third from a step stool, which is the right pick when the top branches are out of reach, because an adult on a stool reaches the top without standing on a chair. Mom middle means Jen handles the middle third, which is the right pick when the middle holds the most ornaments, because the middle third is where guests look first. Kids bottom means Mia and Leo handle the bottom third, which is the right pick for most families, because the bottom branches are at kid height and the kids can reach them without climbing. Swap means everyone rotates zones every ten minutes, which is the right pick for a family that argues about zones, because a swap gives everyone a turn at the front branches. Ladder jobs means one person on the ladder hangs ornaments handed up by everyone else, which is the right pick for a tall tree, because one person on a ladder is safer than three people on chairs. Sides means each person takes one side of the tree, which is the right pick for a tree in a corner, because a corner tree has two sides that show and two sides that face the wall.

Spin once, and that is the job plan. The wheel lands on swap, which means everyone starts in one zone and rotates every ten minutes, so Mia gets the front branches for one round and Leo gets them for the next. If the house cannot agree who climbs the ladder first, settle it with the Random Name Picker before anyone touches a stool. One draw for the ladder, one spin for the zone, and the hanging starts. One rule helps: the person holding the ladder does not hang ornaments. A ladder held by nobody wobbles, and a wobbling ladder ends the decorating night early.

Spin for the skirt and the step-back check

The ornaments are on, and now the last spin handles the skirt and the final check. The skirt is the item people skip, and a tree with no skirt shows the stand, the cord, and a pile of fallen needles, which is the part of the tree nobody photographs. A skirt picked in advance finishes the tree, and the step-back check catches the one crooked branch before the grandparents arrive.

Load the wheel with red skirt, white skirt, blanket, basket, none, and tree collar. Red skirt means a red cloth skirt around the stand, which is the right pick for a cozy tree, because a red skirt matches the red ribbon and the tree looks finished from the doorway. White skirt means a white skirt that looks like snow, which is the right pick for a white or blue theme, because a white skirt under a white tree reads as one snowy scene. Blanket means a folded blanket tucked around the stand, which is the right pick for a house that already owns a spare plaid blanket, because a blanket skirt costs nothing and looks soft. Basket means a woven basket that hides the stand, which is the right pick for a tree in a basket stand, because a basket hides the stand and the cord in one move. None means no skirt at all, which is the right pick for a tree on a raised stand, because a raised stand looks fine bare and a skirt under it bunches up. Tree collar means a stiff collar that rings the stand, which is the right pick for a modern room, because a collar looks tidy and holds its shape all month.

Spin once, and that is the skirt plan. The wheel lands on red skirt, which means the red cloth skirt goes around this stand, and the gifts go on top of it on Christmas morning. Then everyone walks to the doorway and looks at the tree from ten feet back. That is the step-back check. One crooked ornament gets moved. One bare branch gets a ball from the leftover box. The star gets straightened. The tree is done when it looks good from the doorway, not when the box is empty, because the doorway is where the grandparents will stand.

Try the tree look wheel

It is 8:40, and the tree is done. The theme is cozy. The warm lights glow. The gold star sits straight. The red ribbon winds around in even swags. The big balls spread around the whole tree, the school ornaments sit near the front, and the red skirt hides the stand. Mia points at her school ornament from the doorway. Leo points at a candy cane he can reach. Nobody argued about the angel, because the wheel picked the star and the angel stays in the box for a year when star wins.

Try it on the Decision Wheel the night the tree comes home. Load the theme, spin for the lights, and write the swap zones on a note in your phone. Sounds silly, works great. The tree gets a plan before anyone touches a branch, and the decorating night ends with a finished tree, which is more than most early December Saturdays manage. If the tree night turns into a gift night too, the Secret Santa page keeps the name draw fair while the tree skirt holds the gifts.

Easter Brunch Wheel: Pick the Spring Table

Eleven people, one Easter morning, and no plan for brunch. I put the menu on a wheel and let it decide the egg dish, the bread, the fruit, and dessert, one spin at a time.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 28, 2026 Updated October 28, 2026

It was 10:30 on Easter morning and eleven people were about to walk into my house. The kids were already in the yard with baskets. They were hunting eggs I had hidden too well the night before. My sister texted from the driveway: “What are we eating?” I looked at my fridge. Eggs, yes. A bag of croissants going stale. Half a melon. No plan at all.

Here’s the thing. Easter brunch sounds simple until you count the people. One person eats no pork. Two kids will only eat food they can hold in one hand. My dad wants something hot. My sister wants something light because she ate a full dinner the night before and still talks about it. I could have stood in the kitchen for twenty minutes working out a menu that pleased nobody fully. Instead I opened the What to Eat Wheel and spun for each course, one at a time. Twenty minutes later we had a menu, and nobody argued with it, because nobody had written it. The wheel had.

Spin for the egg dish

The egg dish is the center of an Easter brunch, and it is also the course people fight about most. Quiche people say it feels like a real occasion. Frittata people say it is faster and feeds a crowd. Pancake people say the kids will actually eat it, which is a strong argument when the kids are the ones at the table.

Our wheel holds six: quiche, frittata, pancakes, French toast, egg casserole, and bagels with cream cheese for the morning nobody wants to cook. One spin, and the argument ends. Last Easter the wheel landed on quiche, and I will be honest, I groaned a little. Quiche takes time. But it also sits happily in the oven while you do everything else, and it slices into neat portions for eleven people without anyone standing at the stove flipping things one by one. That is a real advantage when the guests arrive in waves and the cook wants to sit down at some point.

One rule makes this spin work: the egg dish spin happens the night before, not on Easter morning. A quiche needs an hour in the oven. A casserole needs to set overnight. Pancakes need a griddle and a free cook. If you spin at 10:30 on the morning itself, the wheel might hand you a dish you cannot finish before people sit down. Spin on Saturday night, shop for what the wheel picks, and Sunday morning stays calm.

Spin for the bread

Bread is the course everyone forgets until the table looks bare. You have the egg dish, you have the fruit, and then someone says “is there any bread?” and you realize the answer is a sad bag of sliced white from three days ago. Give bread its own spin and it stops being an afterthought.

Our wheel holds six: croissants, muffins, coffee cake, toast, bagels, and scones. The wheel landed on croissants last year, and croissants turned out to be the right call for a crowd with mixed tastes. Half the table ate them plain. Two kids tore them apart and ate the insides. My dad put jam on his, which is a crime in some houses and completely normal in ours. Nobody asked for anything else, because croissants do not need a topping debate. They are done as they are.

A tip from a year I got this wrong: spin for bread when you spin for the egg dish, on Saturday night. Bread from a bakery needs a Saturday order or a Sunday morning trip, and a Sunday morning trip during an egg hunt is a trip you will regret. If the wheel picks muffins or coffee cake, you bake on Saturday afternoon, and Sunday morning you just warm them for ten minutes while the quiche finishes.

Spin for the fruit side

Fruit is the easiest course to get wrong, because people buy what looks good in the store and then serve a bowl of melon cubes nobody touches. A fruit spin fixes the shopping list before you leave the house. You buy what the wheel picks, and you buy enough of it, instead of a little of everything.

Our wheel holds six: berries, melon, citrus, grapes, apples, and peaches. Berries won last Easter, and berries were the first bowl to empty. I put out a big bowl of strawberries and blueberries with nothing on them, no yogurt, no cream, and they were gone before the quiche cooled. Kids eat berries with their fingers. Adults eat berries while standing in the kitchen talking. Nobody has to ask how to serve them.

Here is a small rule that saves money: the fruit spin also picks the quantity. Berries for eleven people means three big punnets, not one. Grapes for eleven means two bags, because grapes vanish the way berries do. Citrus for eleven means a bag of oranges cut into wedges, not three oranges sliced thin. The wheel picks the fruit, and you buy like you mean it. A half-empty fruit bowl looks like an afterthought. A full one looks like spring showed up.

Spin for dessert

Brunch dessert is a strange course. Half the table says they are too full, and then the same half eats dessert anyway. The trick is to pick one dessert, not three, and to pick it before anyone starts negotiating. A dessert spin does that job in ten seconds.

Our wheel holds six: carrot cake, lemon bars, fruit tart, cookies, brownies, and pavlova. Carrot cake landed last year, which felt almost too on theme for Easter, but it worked. One cake, sliced thin, served with coffee. Nobody asked for a second option. A single dessert also means one thing to buy or bake, one plate to wash, and no leftover tray of brownies sitting on the counter until Tuesday.

Carrot cake has one more advantage for Easter: it keeps. I baked it on Saturday, and it tasted better on Sunday after a night in the fridge. Lemon bars keep well too. Pavlova does not, so if the wheel hands you pavlova, bake it Sunday morning and serve it within the hour, or it softens into something sad. That is the only dessert on our wheel with a timing warning, and it is worth knowing before the spin, not after.

Spin for drinks

Drinks are where an Easter brunch quietly falls apart. Everyone gets coffee, and then the kids want juice, and then someone wants sparkling water, and then you are pouring five different drinks while the food gets cold. One drinks spin, done the night before, settles the whole table.

Our wheel holds six: orange juice, coffee, lemonade, sparkling juice, tea, and milk. Orange juice won last year, and orange juice plus coffee covered the whole table. I bought two big cartons of juice and made a full pot of coffee, and that was it. No one asked for lemonade, because lemonade was not on the table to ask for. That sounds strict, and it is a little strict, but a brunch table with two good drinks beats a brunch table with six half-finished bottles.

The one exception is water. Water is not on the wheel. Water is always on the table, in a big jug, with ice. Kids spill juice. Adults drink coffee and then want water. A jug of water costs nothing and saves three trips to the tap. Spin for the fun drink, and let water take care of itself.

Spin for who brings what

Eleven people and one cook is a bad plan. Someone always offers to bring something, and then three people bring the same salad and nobody brings dessert. A spin fixes the assignments before the group chat turns into a negotiation.

I put the names on the Random Name Picker and the dishes on a second list, and I spin one name and one dish at a time. My sister got the fruit. My cousin got the bread. My dad got drinks, which meant two cartons of juice and a bag of ice, a job he could do on the way over. Nobody got assigned a dish they hate, because anyone can trade after the spin, but a trade has to be a real swap, one dish for another. The wheel assigns. People trade. The table still ends up covered.

Two rules keep this fair. First, the host keeps the egg dish, because the egg dish needs the host’s oven and the host’s timing. Second, nobody brings the same dish two years in a row. Last year my cousin brought bread. This year the wheel gave bread to my sister, and my cousin got dessert, and he brought lemon bars that were gone in ten minutes. A rotation keeps the table interesting and keeps one person from becoming the permanent bread person, which is a real job nobody thanks you for.

Spin for the table plan

The last spin is not about food at all. It is about the order of the morning, and it matters more than people think. Does the egg hunt happen before brunch or after? Do people eat inside or on the patio? Do the kids sit at their own table or mixed in with the adults? Every one of those questions can eat twenty minutes of a morning that is supposed to feel relaxed.

Our wheel for this spin holds the order of events: hunt first then eat, eat first then hunt, patio if the weather holds, inside if it rains, kids table, mixed table. Last Easter the wheel said hunt first, then eat, and it was the right call. The kids burned off energy in the yard, came inside hungry, and ate twice as much quiche as they would have after a full brunch. The adults got twenty quiet minutes with coffee while the hunt happened, which is the closest thing to peace an Easter morning offers.

If the weather is the question, spin for it on Saturday night too, and set the table where the wheel says. Moving a table for eleven people on Easter morning, with food already on it, is a job nobody wants. A spin on Saturday night means the chairs are where they need to be before the first guest arrives. The Decision Wheel handles these bigger either-or calls well when the menu wheel has done its part.

Try the Easter brunch wheel

Easter brunch does not need a perfect menu. It needs a menu decided before the guests arrive, and a table where nobody spent the morning arguing about it. Put your egg dishes on the What to Eat Wheel: quiche, frittata, pancakes, French toast, casserole, bagels. Spin for the bread, the fruit, the dessert, and the drinks. Hand the bring-a-dish jobs to the Random Name Picker and the morning order to the Decision Wheel. One spin per course, Saturday night, and Sunday morning belongs to the egg hunt, not the menu debate.

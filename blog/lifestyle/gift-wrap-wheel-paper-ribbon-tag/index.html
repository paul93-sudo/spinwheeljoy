Gift Wrap Wheel: Pick the Paper, Ribbon, and Tag

It is December 20, the dining table is buried under fourteen unwrapped gifts, and Jess and Rob have spent twenty minutes debating paper patterns instead of wrapping anything. A gift wrap wheel settles the paper, the ribbon, the tag, the bow, who wraps whose gift, and what to do with the odd-shaped one, so the pile shrinks instead of growing.

It is 7:10 on the night of December 20, and the dining table has disappeared. Under the pile are fourteen gifts, three rolls of wrapping paper, two pairs of scissors, and one roll of tape that is already half gone. Jess bought the paper. Rob bought the ribbon. Mia is nine and wants every gift wrapped in stripes. Leo is six and wants every gift wrapped in gold. Grandma arrives on the 23rd, and the gifts need to be wrapped before she gets here, because Grandma wraps slowly and asks about every tag.

Here’s the thing. Nobody in this house disagrees about wrapping gifts. They disagree about fourteen small choices, one per gift, and each choice takes five minutes. Paper pattern. Ribbon color. Tag style. Bow or no bow. Who wraps whose gift. Which gift first. What to do with the soccer ball that will not fit in a box. Seven choices times fourteen gifts is a whole evening gone, and the tape is still half gone.

Put it on the Decision Wheel instead. One spin for the paper. One for the ribbon. One for the tag. One for the bow. One for who wraps whose gift. One for which gift gets wrapped first. One for the odd-shaped gift. Seven spins, and the wrapping starts, because the wheel picks and the scissors come out.

Spin for the paper pattern

Start here, because the paper is the choice everyone argues about first. The store sells forty patterns, and the house bought three rolls. Mia wants stripes. Leo wants gold. Jess wants kraft paper with a ribbon, because kraft paper looks calm on a table full of bright gifts. Rob wants whatever roll is closest to his hand.

Load the wheel with kraft, stripes, dots, plaid, gold, and plain. Kraft means brown kraft paper, which is the right pick when the table already looks busy, because kraft paper calms a pile of bright gifts and a red ribbon pops against it. Stripes means striped paper, which suits a kid who wants a loud gift, because stripes read as festive from across the room and hide a crooked fold better than a solid color. Dots means polka-dot paper, which is the right pick for a younger kid, because dots look playful and a dot pattern forgives tape in the wrong spot. Plaid means plaid paper, which suits a traditional Christmas table, because plaid matches the tree skirt and the stockings without trying. Gold means metallic gold paper, which is the right pick for one special gift, because gold paper marks a gift as the big one before anyone reads the tag. Plain means solid-color paper with no pattern, which suits a house that ran out of fancy rolls, because plain paper plus a good ribbon still looks finished.

Spin once, and that is the paper plan. The wheel lands on stripes, which means Mia wins this round and the striped roll comes out first. Jess wraps the first four gifts in stripes, Rob wraps the next four, and the kraft roll stays in the closet for the gifts that need a quiet look. One rule keeps this spin honest: one pattern per wrapping session. A session that switches paper every gift ends with six open rolls, paper sliding off the table, and someone stepping on a roll.

Spin for the ribbon color

The paper is settled, and now the ribbon needs a call. Ribbon sounds like a detail until the gifts sit under the tree and every gift wears the same red curl. A ribbon picked in advance gives the pile some variety, and it stops the annual debate about whether green ribbon clashes with striped paper. It does not clash. It matches fine, and the debate wastes ten minutes anyway.

Load the wheel with red, green, gold, silver, blue, and twine. Red means classic red ribbon, which is the right pick for striped paper, because red and stripes are the standard Christmas pair and nobody questions it. Green means green ribbon, which suits kraft paper, because green on brown reads as a pine branch and looks planned. Gold means gold ribbon, which is the right pick for a plain paper gift, because gold lifts a plain wrap and makes a simple gift look dressed up. Silver means silver ribbon, which suits a cool-colored gift, because silver pairs with blue paper and with white paper and stays out of the way. Blue means blue ribbon, which is the right pick when the pile is all red and green already, because blue breaks up a table that looks like a candy cane. Twine means plain twine instead of ribbon, which suits a kraft paper gift, because twine plus kraft paper looks like a farmhouse gift and costs almost nothing.

Spin once, and that is the ribbon plan. The wheel lands on red, which means the striped gifts get red ribbon, the kraft gifts get red ribbon too, and Rob ties the bows because his bows hold and Jess’s bows fall apart by morning. One rule helps: cut the ribbon long. A ribbon cut too short ends up as a knot instead of a bow, and a knot on a gift looks like the gift gave up.

Spin for the tag style

The ribbon is picked, and now the tag needs a call. Tags sound trivial until Christmas morning, when Leo opens a gift meant for Mia and Mia opens a gift meant for Grandma. A tag style picked in advance stops the morning mix-up, and it stops the argument about whose handwriting is readable. Rob’s handwriting is not readable. Everyone knows it. Rob denies it.

Load the wheel with handwritten, printed, photo, sticker, joke, and plain. Handwritten means a hand-written tag in pen, which is the right pick when the giver wants a personal touch, because a hand-written tag feels like part of the gift. Printed means a printed label from the computer, which suits a house with unreadable handwriting, because a printed tag reads clearly on Christmas morning. Photo means a small photo of the receiver taped to the gift, which is the right pick for a big family, because a photo tag works even for a relative who cannot read the handwriting. Sticker means a sticker tag from a pack, which suits a house in a hurry, because a sticker tag takes five seconds and still names the receiver. Joke means a joke tag with a wrong name or a silly message, which is the right pick for a sibling gift, because a joke tag makes the opening funnier. Plain means no tag at all, just a colored ribbon code, which suits a house that wraps in secret, because a ribbon code hides the receiver from snooping kids.

Spin once, and that is the tag plan. The wheel lands on photo, which means Jess prints small photos from her phone, tapes one to each gift, and Leo stops asking which gifts are his because his own face is on three of them. One rule helps: the tag goes on before the gift joins the pile. A gift that joins the pile untagged joins a pile of mystery gifts, and a pile of mystery gifts ends with someone unwrapping their own present.

Spin for bow or no bow

The tag is settled, and now the bow needs a call. This one splits the house cleanly. Mia wants a bow on every gift. Rob says bows are a waste of ribbon and fall off in the car anyway. Jess wants bows on the gifts Grandma sees and no bows on the gifts that travel. Leo wants a bow he can pull off and wear as a hat.

Load the wheel with big bow, small bow, no bow, tag only, twine loop, and double bow. Big bow means a large looped bow on top, which is the right pick for a gift that sits under the tree, because a big bow makes a gift look finished from across the room. Small bow means a small neat bow, which suits a gift that travels in a car, because a small bow survives a car ride and a big bow does not. No bow means ribbon only, flat against the paper, which is the right pick for a gift that stacks, because flat gifts stack and bowed gifts slide. Tag only means no ribbon at all, just a tag, which suits a gift wrapped in loud paper, because loud paper plus a bow is too much and the tag alone lets the paper do the work. Twine loop means a simple twine loop instead of a bow, which is the right pick for kraft paper, because twine and kraft paper belong together. Double bow means two bows side by side, which suits the one gift that matters most, because a double bow marks the gift everyone should notice first.

Spin once, and that is the bow plan. The wheel lands on big bow, which means Mia gets her way and every gift under the tree wears a big bow. Rob ties them, because his bows hold, and the gifts that travel get wrapped last in a separate bag so the bows survive the car. One rule helps: bows go on last, after the gifts move to the tree. A bow tied before a gift travels is a bow that arrives flat, and a flat bow is just ribbon with regrets.

Spin for who wraps whose gift

The bow is picked, and now one spin decides who wraps whose gift. This sounds like a chore chart, and it is, but it is also the part that keeps the surprises intact. When Jess wraps her own gifts, she knows what she is getting. When Rob wraps Leo’s gifts, Leo watches from the doorway and learns too much. When the job is split by a spin, nobody wraps their own gift and nobody peeks on purpose.

Load the wheel with mom, dad, grandma, kids, swap, and draw. Mom means Jess wraps the next batch, which is the right pick when Rob is still at work, because a batch wrapped by one person on time beats a batch wrapped by two people late. Dad means Rob wraps the next batch, which suits a night when Jess is out, because the gifts still need wrapping and one person can do it. Grandma means Grandma wraps when she arrives, which is the right pick for the gifts Grandma bought, because a gift Grandma wraps feels like a gift from Grandma. Kids means Mia and Leo wrap the easy gifts, which suits a family that wants the kids involved, because kids who wrap a gift enjoy giving it more. Swap means everyone swaps: Jess wraps Rob’s gifts, Rob wraps Jess’s gifts, and the kids wrap each other’s, which is the right pick for most families, because a swap keeps every gift a surprise. Draw means draw names from a hat, which suits a big pile, because a draw assigns each gift to a wrapper and nobody argues about who got the easy ones.

Spin once, and that is the wrapper plan. The wheel lands on swap, which means Jess wraps Rob’s gifts in the bedroom with the door shut, Rob wraps Jess’s gifts at the dining table after the kids are in bed, and Mia wraps Leo’s gift in stripes with a big bow. If the house cannot agree who draws first, settle it with the Random Name Picker before the wrapping starts. One draw for the wrapper, one spin for the paper, and the pile starts shrinking.

Spin for which gift gets wrapped first

The wrappers are assigned, and now one spin decides the order. A pile of fourteen gifts has an order problem. Everyone grabs the easy rectangular box first, and the hard gifts sit until the end, when patience is gone and the tape is almost out. Wrapping order sounds like a small thing until it is 10:30, the tape is gone, and the soccer ball is still unwrapped on the table.

Load the wheel with biggest, smallest, hardest, kids first, grandma first, and last-minute. Biggest means the largest gift first, which is the right pick when the table is crowded, because a big gift wrapped first frees table space for the rest. Smallest means the smallest gifts first, which suits a house that wants quick wins, because three small gifts wrapped fast make the pile look smaller. Hardest means the hardest gift first, which is the right pick for most families, because the hard gift takes the most patience and patience runs out by 10:00. Kids first means the kids’ gifts first, which suits a house with early bedtimes, because kids’ gifts wrapped early can hide before the kids wake up. Grandma first means Grandma’s gifts first, which is the right pick when Grandma arrives soon, because her gifts need to be ready the moment she walks in. Last-minute means the gift bought yesterday first, which suits a house with one gift still in a store bag, because a gift in a store bag is a gift someone will forget.

Spin once, and that is the order plan. The wheel lands on hardest, which means the soccer ball gets dealt with first, while Jess still has patience and Rob still has tape. The easy boxes wait, and the easy boxes are easy at 10:30 too. One rule helps: wrap the hard gift before the second cup of cocoa. A hard gift started after the second cup of cocoa ends up wrapped in a towel, and a towel is not wrapping paper, no matter what Rob says.

Spin for the odd-shaped gift

The order is set, and now the last spin handles the gift that will not fit in a box. Every pile has one. This year it is a soccer ball. Last year it was a skipping rope. The year before that, a toy dinosaur with a tail that poked through two layers of paper. An odd-shaped gift breaks a wrapping session, because paper does not bend around a ball and tape does not stick to a tail.

Load the wheel with box it, bag it, towel wrap, two sheets, wrap loose, and skip it. Box it means find a box the gift fits in, which is the right pick when a spare box exists, because a boxed gift wraps like any other gift. Bag it means a gift bag with tissue paper, which is the right pick for a ball, because a bag holds a round shape and tissue hides the shape from the receiver. Towel wrap means wrap the gift in a towel, which suits a gift that is itself soft, because a towel wrap doubles as part of the gift. Two sheets means two sheets of paper taped together, which is the right pick for a long gift, because one sheet never covers a skipping rope. Wrap loose means wrap it loose and let the shape show, which suits a gift the receiver will guess anyway, because a loose wrap saves paper and the guessing is half the fun. Skip it means no paper at all, just a bow on top, which is the right pick for a gift too big to wrap, because a bow on a big gift still marks it as a gift.

Spin once, and that is the odd-gift plan. The wheel lands on bag it, which means the soccer ball goes in a big gift bag, tissue paper goes on top, and the ball stops rolling off the table. If the gift is a secret gift for a name draw, the Secret Santa page keeps the draw fair while the bag keeps the shape hidden. One rule helps: the bag gets a tag before the tissue goes in. A bag with tissue but no tag is a bag nobody can claim, and Christmas morning is no time for a bag nobody can claim.

Try the gift wrap wheel

It is 9:40, and the pile is gone. Fourteen gifts sit wrapped on the dining table, striped paper with red ribbon, big bows on top, photo tags on each one. The soccer ball sits in a gift bag with tissue paper, and it has not rolled off the table since. Mia wrapped Leo’s gift. Rob wrapped Jess’s gifts. Jess wrapped Rob’s. The tape roll is empty, and that is fine, because the wrapping is done and the store sells more tape.

Try it on the Decision Wheel the night the gifts come home. Load the paper, spin for the ribbon, and write the wrapper swap on a note in your phone. Sounds silly, works great. The pile shrinks, the tags stay honest, and the dining table comes back, which is more than most December 20 nights manage.

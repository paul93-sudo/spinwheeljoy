Christmas Stocking Stuffer Wheel: Fill the Stocking Without the Guesswork

It is December 23, four stockings hang empty by the fireplace, and Dan and Priya have one hour in a store tomorrow to fill them all. A stocking stuffer wheel settles the small toy, the candy, the socks, the gift card, the one practical item, who fills whose stocking, and the surprise hidden at the toe, so nobody ends up with an empty stocking on Christmas morning.

It is 8:40 on the night of December 23, and four stockings hang empty by the fireplace. Dan hung them. Priya straightened them. Noah is eight and has already asked twice what goes in his. Zoe is five and has asked four times, because Zoe counts stockings the way other kids count sleeps. The stockings are supposed to be filled by Christmas morning, and the store trip tomorrow lasts one hour, because Dan works until noon and Priya has a dentist appointment at two.

Here’s the thing. Nobody in this house disagrees about stockings. They disagree about what counts as a stuffer. Dan thinks a stuffer is anything small. Priya thinks a stuffer is something small and cheap, and a twenty-dollar toy is a gift, not a stuffer. Noah wants a toy in his stocking. Zoe wants candy in hers. Grandma, who arrives on the 24th, wants every stocking to hold one practical item, because Grandma believes a stocking should be useful, and candy alone does not count. Four stockings, five or six small things each, one hour in a store. That is twenty small decisions, and the store closes at nine.

Put it on the Decision Wheel instead. One spin for the small toy. One for the candy. One for the socks. One for the gift card. One for the practical item. One for who fills whose stocking. One for the surprise hidden at the toe. Seven spins, and the shopping list writes itself, because the wheel picks and the cart fills up.

Spin for the small toy

Start here, because the toy is the item every kid checks first. A stocking without a toy feels like a sock with snacks in it, and a stocking with the wrong toy feels worse, because the kid holds it up and asks who picked this. The store sells a whole wall of small toys, and the wall is where the hour disappears.

Load the wheel with dino, car, ball, yo-yo, puzzle, and stickers. Dino means a small plastic dinosaur, which is the right pick for a kid who lines toys up on a windowsill, because a dino stands on its own and does not need batteries. Car means a small die-cast car, which suits a kid who races cars down the hallway, because a car fits in a pocket and survives a hallway crash. Ball means a bouncy ball, which is the right pick for a stocking that needs noise, because a bouncy ball bounces and that is the whole point. Yo-yo means a simple yo-yo, which suits an older kid, because a yo-yo takes practice and a five-year-old tangles it in ten minutes. Puzzle means a mini puzzle in a bag, which is the right pick for a quiet Christmas morning, because a puzzle keeps a kid busy while the adults make coffee. Stickers means a sticker sheet, which suits a stocking that already has a noisy toy, because stickers make no noise and still count as a toy.

Spin once per stocking, and that is the toy plan. Noah’s spin lands on dino, which means a green dino goes in his stocking, and Zoe’s spin lands on stickers, which means a sheet of star stickers goes in hers. One rule keeps this spin honest: the toy must fit inside the stocking with room to spare. A toy that stretches the stocking out of shape is a gift, not a stuffer, and it belongs under the tree.

Spin for the candy

The toy is settled, and now the candy needs a call. Candy sounds easy until the stocking gets filled with four kinds of sugar and the kid eats all of it before breakfast. A candy picked in advance keeps the stocking sweet without turning Christmas morning into a sugar rush, and it stops the debate about whether a full-size candy bar counts as a stuffer. It does not. A full-size bar is too big for a stocking and it crowds out everything else.

Load the wheel with chocolate, cane, gummies, mints, coins, and jelly. Chocolate means a small foil-wrapped chocolate or a mini bar, which is the right pick for most stockings, because chocolate feels like Christmas and a mini bar fits anywhere. Cane means a candy cane, which suits a stocking hung by the fireplace, because a cane hooks over the top of the stocking and looks like part of the decoration. Gummies means a small pack of gummy bears, which is the right pick for a kid who likes chewy candy, because a small pack is enough and a big bag is not. Mints means a tin of mints, which suits an adult stocking, because a tin of mints lasts past Christmas and fits in a coat pocket. Coins means chocolate coins in gold foil, which is the right pick for a younger kid, because gold coins feel like treasure and unwrap one at a time. Jelly means jelly beans in a small bag, which suits a stocking that already has chocolate, because jelly beans add color without adding another chocolate.

Spin once per stocking, and that is the candy plan. The spins land on chocolate, which means a mini chocolate bar goes in every stocking, and Zoe’s candy cane hooks over the top of hers because the cane was already in the pantry. One rule helps: one candy per stocking, plus the cane. A stocking with three candies is a candy bag with a sock around it, and a candy bag with a sock around it is not a stocking.

Spin for the socks

The candy is picked, and now the socks need a call. Socks in a stocking sound like a joke until December 25, when every kid in the house is walking around in new socks and nobody is complaining. Grandma started this tradition, and Grandma was right, because socks are the one practical item kids actually use on Christmas morning. The trick is picking a pair that reads as a gift and not as laundry.

Load the wheel with stripes, dots, plain, fuzzy, sport, and holiday. Stripes means striped crew socks, which is the right pick for most stockings, because stripes look fun in a drawer and a striped pair is easy to match. Dots means polka-dot socks, which suits a kid who likes bright colors, because dots read as playful and a dot pair stands out from the plain pairs. Plain means solid-color socks, which is the right pick for an adult stocking, because plain socks go with everything and nobody argues about plain socks. Fuzzy means fuzzy socks, which suits a cold house, because fuzzy socks are slippers and socks in one pair. Sport means ankle sport socks, which is the right pick for a kid who plays soccer or runs around all day, because sport socks get used the same week. Holiday means socks with trees or snowmen on them, which suits a December-only pair, because holiday socks are fun for two weeks and then they wait until next year.

Spin once per stocking, and that is the sock plan. The spins land on stripes, which means striped pairs go in all four stockings, in four different colors so nobody mixes up a pair. Dan gets navy stripes. Priya gets red stripes. Noah gets green stripes. Zoe gets pink stripes. One rule helps: the socks go in the stocking, not in a box. A pair of socks in a box is a gift, and a pair of socks rolled into a stocking is a stuffer, and the stocking is where the tradition lives.

Spin for the gift card

The socks are settled, and now the gift card needs a call. A gift card in a stocking is the item that causes the most trouble, because one stocking gets twenty dollars and another gets five, and the kid who got five notices. A card amount picked in advance keeps the stockings fair, and it keeps the store trip inside a budget, because four cards at twenty dollars each is eighty dollars and nobody planned on eighty dollars.

Load the wheel with $5, $10, $15, $20, book, and cafe. $5 means a five-dollar card, which is the right pick for a younger kid, because five dollars buys a treat and a treat is enough. $10 means a ten-dollar card, which suits most stockings, because ten dollars buys a small book or a snack run and still feels like a real amount. $15 means a fifteen-dollar card, which is the right pick for an older kid, because fifteen dollars covers a movie snack budget. $20 means a twenty-dollar card, which suits the oldest kid only, because twenty dollars in a stocking is a lot and the younger kids will compare. Book means a bookstore card, which is the right pick for a reader, because a bookstore card turns into a book the same week. Cafe means a coffee shop card, which suits an adult stocking, because a coffee card gets used on the way to work.

Spin once, and that is the card plan. The wheel lands on $10, which means every stocking gets a ten-dollar card, and the store stays the same for everyone: a bookstore card for Noah, a bookstore card for Zoe, and a coffee card for Dan and Priya at ten dollars each. One rule helps: the same amount in every stocking. A stocking card plan with different amounts ends with a comparison on Christmas morning, and a comparison on Christmas morning is the opposite of a stocking.

Spin for the one practical item

The card is picked, and now Grandma’s item needs a call. Grandma wants one practical item per stocking, and Grandma checks. Last year Dan skipped it, and Grandma noticed before breakfast, because Grandma notices everything before breakfast. A practical item picked in advance keeps Grandma happy, and it keeps the stocking useful past December 25, because a lip balm gets used in January and a toy dino sits on a windowsill.

Load the wheel with lip balm, hand cream, pens, comb, mirror, and clips. Lip balm means a lip balm stick, which is the right pick for winter, because dry lips in December are real and a lip balm stick fits anywhere. Hand cream means a small tube of hand cream, which suits an adult stocking, because hand cream gets used at a desk and in a car. Pens means gel pens, which is the right pick for a school kid, because gel pens disappear from pencil cases and a fresh pack is always welcome. Comb means a pocket comb, which suits a stocking for someone who travels, because a pocket comb fits in a coat pocket. Mirror means a small mirror, which is the right pick for a purse stocking, because a small mirror gets used more than anyone admits. Clips means hair clips, which suits a younger kid, because hair clips get lost and a fresh set is a small win.

Spin once, and that is the practical plan. The wheel lands on lip balm, which means a lip balm stick goes in every stocking, in four different flavors so the kids can trade. Noah gets mint. Zoe gets strawberry. Dan gets plain. Priya gets cherry. One rule helps: the practical item goes in last, on top. A practical item buried under candy gets found in February, and a practical item found in February is a practical item wasted.

Spin for who fills whose stocking

The practical item is settled, and now one spin decides who fills whose stocking. This sounds like a chore chart, and it is, but it is also the part that keeps the surprises intact. When Dan fills his own stocking, he knows what he is getting. When Priya fills Zoe’s stocking alone, Zoe watches from the doorway and learns too much. When the job is split by a spin, nobody fills their own stocking and nobody peeks on purpose.

Load the wheel with mom, dad, swap, draw, kids, and grandma. Mom means Priya fills the next batch, which is the right pick when Dan is still at work, because a batch filled by one person on time beats a batch filled by two people late. Dad means Dan fills the next batch, which suits a night when Priya is out, because the stockings still need filling and one person can do it. Swap means everyone swaps: Dan fills Priya’s stocking, Priya fills Dan’s, and the parents fill the kids’, which is the right pick for most families, because a swap keeps every stocking a surprise. Draw means draw names from a hat, which suits a big family, because a draw assigns each stocking to a filler and nobody argues about who got the easy ones. Kids means Noah and Zoe pick small items for each other with help, which suits a family that wants the kids involved, because kids who fill a stocking enjoy giving it more. Grandma means Grandma fills one stocking when she arrives, which is the right pick for the stocking Grandma bought gifts for, because a stocking Grandma fills feels like a stocking from Grandma.

Spin once, and that is the filler plan. The wheel lands on swap, which means Dan fills Priya’s stocking after the kids are in bed, Priya fills Dan’s at the kitchen table, and the kids’ stockings get filled together, with the door shut. If the house cannot agree who draws first, settle it with the Random Name Picker before the filling starts. One draw for the filler, one spin for the toy, and the stockings start filling. If the stockings belong to a name draw that covers the whole family, the Secret Santa page keeps the draw fair while the swap keeps the stockings secret.

Spin for the toe surprise

The fillers are assigned, and now the last spin handles the surprise hidden at the toe. Every stocking has one item at the very bottom, past the socks and the candy, and that item is the one the kid finds last. A toe surprise sounds small, and it is small, but it is the item the kid remembers, because it is the last thing out of the stocking and the last thing talked about at breakfast.

Load the wheel with puzzle, ring, coin, note, marble, and keychain. Puzzle means a mini puzzle in a small bag, which is the right pick for most stockings, because a puzzle at the toe gives the kid something to do while the adults open their own stockings. Ring means a plastic ring, which suits a younger kid, because a plastic ring gets worn all morning. Coin means a shiny dollar coin, which is the right pick for a stocking that needs a lucky feel, because a coin at the toe feels like treasure found at the bottom of a sock. Note means a handwritten note, which suits a stocking for someone older, because a note at the toe says more than a toy can. Marble means a glass marble, which is the right pick for a kid who collects small things, because a marble is small, round, and easy to keep. Keychain means a small keychain, which suits a backpack stocking, because a keychain goes straight onto a bag.

Spin once per stocking, and that is the toe plan. The spins land on puzzle, which means a mini puzzle in a small bag sits at the toe of every stocking, under the socks, under the candy, under the lip balm. Noah finds his puzzle last, after the dino and the chocolate, and he starts it on the living room rug before breakfast is ready. One rule helps: the toe item goes in first, before anything else. A toe surprise dropped in last sits on top, and a toe surprise on top is just another stuffer, not a surprise.

Try the stocking stuffer wheel

It is 9:10 on Christmas morning, and the stockings are empty. The dino stands on the windowsill. The chocolate is gone. The striped socks are on four pairs of feet. The ten-dollar cards sit on the kitchen counter for a bookstore trip later in the week. The lip balms are in four pockets. The mini puzzles are half done on the living room rug. Nobody got an empty stocking, nobody compared card amounts, and Grandma nodded at the lip balm before breakfast, which is Grandma’s way of saying the stockings passed.

Try it on the Decision Wheel the night before the store trip. Load the toy, spin for the candy, and write the filler swap on a note in your phone. Sounds silly, works great. The list writes itself, the hour in the store is enough, and the stockings fill up, which is more than most December 23 nights manage.

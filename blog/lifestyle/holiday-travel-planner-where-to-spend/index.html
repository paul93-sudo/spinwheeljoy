Holiday Travel Planner: Decide Where to Spend the Holidays

The family group chat goes silent every October when holiday plans come up. Here's how one wheel settles whose house, which holiday, and who hosts — once, in front of everyone, no annual re-fight.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 23, 2026 Updated October 23, 2026

On this page

  1. Stop relitigating it every October
  2. The every-other-year spin
  3. Split the holidays instead of splitting the difference
  4. The host year: everybody comes to you
  5. The neutral-ground Christmas
  6. Let the weather break the tie
  7. One spin, everyone watching

The group chat went quiet last Tuesday, which is how you know somebody’s feelings just got hurt. My sister-in-law suggested Christmas in Denver this year. My mother replied with a photo of the grandkids’ stockings, still hanging on her mantel from last December, like she keeps them up year-round specifically for moments like this. Nobody typed for eleven minutes.

This is not a logistics problem. Flights from Dallas to Denver are ninety minutes and cheap. It is a feelings problem wearing a logistics costume, and it comes back every single October because nobody ever settles it for good.

We did settle it. It took one phone call and a spin of the Decision Wheel, and nobody has re-litigated it since.

Stop relitigating it every October

Here is the thing nobody says out loud: the fight is never really about Denver versus Dallas. It is about the fact that it comes up again every year, which means somebody gets to feel slighted every year. A plan you made once, in front of everyone, removes the whole ritual. The wheel is not really deciding where Christmas is. It is deciding that you are done talking about it.

The every-other-year spin

The classic fix is alternating years, and the classic problem is that nobody writes it down. So do it on speakerphone, with everyone on the call, and let the wheel pick who gets this year.

Load the wheel with the two houses. Grandma’s. Your sister’s. Spin it on a video call where everybody can watch the pointer land. Whoever wins gets this Christmas. The other side gets next Christmas, guaranteed, in writing, in the group chat where nobody can pretend it never happened.

Sounds silly. Works great. Nobody argues with a spin they watched live. My mother now mentions “her year” the way people mention a reservation, which is to say, with complete confidence and zero guilt.

Split the holidays instead of splitting the difference

Not every family can give a whole side Christmas. Some grandparents are Thanksgiving people. The turkey matters more to them than the tree. So stop treating the holidays as one prize and split them on purpose.

Put each holiday on the wheel separately. One spin for Thanksgiving: Grandma’s or Denver? Another for Christmas. You can end up with Thanksgiving in Denver and Christmas at Grandma’s, and weirdly, that feels fairer to everyone than one side “winning” December and the other getting nothing but a photo text.

I’ll be honest: the family that insists on hosting both holidays is telling you something about control, and the wheel is the politest way I know to say no without saying no.

The host year: everybody comes to you

There is an option people forget because it sounds selfish. You. Your house. One year out of every few, everybody comes to you and you are the one with the guest towels and the air mattresses.

Spin for it. Load the wheel with the names of whoever has a house that can hold the crowd, and let the wheel pick the next host. Hosting is real work, and the person who always hosts accumulates a quiet resentment that leaks out in December. The wheel spreads the work the same way it spreads the travel.

For the quick calls inside the plan, like do we drive down Tuesday night or Wednesday morning, the Yes or No Wheel settles those in seconds without a whole family summit.

The neutral-ground Christmas

Sometimes nobody should win, including you. My cousin’s family has been doing this for three years now: nobody goes to anyone’s house. They rent a place.

The trick is picking the place, because left to the group chat, the “neutral” rental ends up suspiciously close to one side’s house. Load the wheel with three or four real options, actual listings with prices, and spin. The wheel does not care whose sister-in-law found which cabin. The listing with the hot tub and the twelve-person dining table won our spin last year, and it was the best Christmas we have had in a decade because nobody was on home turf.

Let the weather break the tie

December in Chicago is not the same proposal as December in Tampa. One is a travel plan, the other is an expedition. When both sides are emotionally even and the debate has run for three weeks, put the practical factors on the table and let them do the deciding.

Traveling with kids changes the math completely. A nine-hour drive with a toddler is a different sport from a nine-hour drive with teenagers who sleep through anything. If your kids are in the car-seat years, that alone should be carrying weight in the decision, and saying so out loud is not favoritism. It is logistics.

One spin, everyone watching

The ritual is the whole product. Not the wheel, the watching.

Get everyone on one call, early October, before feelings harden. Share your screen. Load the wheel with the real options, everyone can see them. Spin once. Screenshot the result into the group chat. Done.

Every family has a lobbyist, the person who has been campaigning for their preferred outcome since August. The wheel beats the lobbyist every time, because you cannot lobby a pointer. And the beautiful part is that the lobbyist usually accepts it. There is something about watching the spin live that converts even the most committed campaigner into a good sport.

When it comes to the food itself, like who brings the turkey versus who handles dessert, the What to Eat Wheel has already ended more than one side-dish standoff in our family.

So load up the Decision Wheel, get the family on one call, and spin once. Then put the stockings up wherever the pointer said, and stop talking about it until next October.

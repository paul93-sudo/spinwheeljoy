Summer Garden Watering Wheel: Who Waters the Plants

Our shared garden died twice from the same problem: everyone thought someone else had watered it. Now a wheel picks who waters, which beds go first, and whether rain means a day off.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 29, 2026 Updated October 29, 2026

  2. Spin for which beds go first
  3. The rain-day skip rule

  5. Spin for the deep-water day
  6. Spin for the pots and seedlings everyone forgets
  7. Keep the rotation where everyone can see it

Our garden sits on the fence line between our house and Dan and Ana’s place. Two families, one long bed of tomatoes, one patch of herbs, and a row of carrots that Leo planted and then forgot about. The first summer we shared it, the garden nearly died twice, and both times the cause was the same. Everyone thought someone else had watered it.

It went like this. I would walk past the beds at six in the evening, see dry soil, and think Dan must have watered that morning. Dan would walk past at seven, see the same dry soil, and think I had watered at lunch. Nobody watered. The tomatoes wilted, Leo’s carrots went woody, and we had a very polite argument about a hose. Polite, but still an argument.

So now we water with the Chore Wheel. The wheel holds six names: Mia, Sam, Dan, Ana, Leo, and Dad. One spin picks who waters that week. A second wheel picks which beds go first. A third settles the rain question before anyone argues about it. Six spins on a Sunday night, and the whole week is sorted.

Spin for who waters this week

The who wheel runs the whole system. Every Sunday night, after dinner, someone spins it at the kitchen table and the name it lands on waters the garden for the next seven days. Last week it landed on Mia, and Mia took the job seriously in a way that surprised all of us. She set a phone alarm for seven in the morning, and she watered before school without being asked once.

One person per week works better than one person per day. Daily rotation sounds fair on paper, but in real life it means six people each half-remembering whether today is their day. A full week means one person owns it. They learn the beds. They notice when the lettuce looks thirsty on Wednesday, and they remember it on Thursday. Nobody has to ask who is on duty, because the answer stays the same all week.

I’ll be honest, the first version of this wheel had twelve names on it, including cousins who visit twice a summer. That wheel was a mess. It landed on people who were not even in town, and then someone had to cover, and the cover person felt put out. Keep the wheel to people who actually live close enough to hold a hose. Six names is plenty.

The spin also ends the “I did it last time” debate, because the wheel does not care who did it last time. It cares about this week. If it lands on the same person two weeks in a row, that person waters two weeks in a row, and everyone else admits the wheel is the boss. It has landed on Dad twice in a row before. Dad watered twice in a row. Nobody heard a word about it, because the wheel had spoken.

Spin for which beds go first

Watering everything at once sounds fine until you try it with a ten-year-old holding a can and a garden that needs twenty minutes of steady watering. The beds wheel fixes the order. It holds six beds: tomatoes, herbs, lettuce, peppers, carrots, and flowers. Spin it, water the winner first, and work down the list from there.

Tomatoes go first most weeks in our house, because tomatoes drink more than anything else in the bed and they show it first. A tomato plant with dry soil droops by noon, and a drooping tomato plant is hard to bring back once the leaves crisp at the edges. Herbs go second, because herbs live in shallow soil that dries out fast. Carrots go last, because carrots sit deep and can wait a day without complaining.

The order matters on hot weeks. When the temperature hits the high nineties, the person watering runs out of steam before the last bed. If the order is random, the thirstiest plants sometimes end up last, and last means skipped. With the wheel, the thirsty beds get their turn first, and the beds that can wait get whatever energy is left. That is honest gardening for a 98-degree day, and honest beats perfect when the hose is heavy.

One rule keeps this spin fair: the beds wheel gets re-spun every week, right after the who wheel. Same order all summer means the flowers always go last, and flowers that always go last stop being flowers by August. Fresh order each week spreads the bad luck around.

The rain-day skip rule

Rain causes more garden arguments than drought does. Someone sees clouds, skips watering, and then it rains for ten minutes and stops, and the soil is dry again by dinner. Or someone waters right before a storm and the beds turn to mud. We settled it with a rule and a picture, and the rule is simple: if the soil is wet one inch down, skip the day.

Here is how we check. Push a finger into the soil near the tomatoes, about one knuckle deep. If the soil feels damp, skip. If it feels dry, water. The finger test takes five seconds and it beats every weather app on my phone, because the app tells me what fell on the whole town and my finger tells me what fell on my tomatoes. Last July the app said we got half an inch of rain. Our beds got a sprinkle and a lot of wind. The finger said dry, so we watered, and the tomatoes were fine.

The skip day still counts as a watering day for the person on duty. That part matters. If skipping feels like getting away with something, people start skipping on cloudy days when the soil is bone dry. So the person on duty checks, decides, and writes SKIP on the fridge chart. Everyone can see it, and nobody has to wonder whether the garden got checked at all. On mornings when the finger test comes out borderline, damp in one spot and dry in another, we let the Yes or No Wheel break the tie instead of debating it over coffee.

If it rains three days in a row, the person on duty still checks once a day. Three days of rain can still leave a raised bed dry under a thick tomato canopy, because the leaves catch the rain and the soil underneath stays dry. I learned that one the hard way, standing over a wilted pepper plant after a week I would have sworn was wet.

Spin for morning or evening

Morning watering wins in our house, but we still spin for it, because evening watering has its fans and the spin settles the debate without a meeting. The time wheel holds four options: morning, evening, noon, and night. Morning has landed more often than anything else, which suits me fine.

Morning works because the water soaks in before the sun gets strong, and the leaves dry off during the day. Evening watering works too, but wet leaves overnight invite trouble on tomatoes and lettuce, and I would rather not give slugs a damp garden to wander through after dark. Noon is the worst option on the wheel and everyone knows it. Water at noon evaporates before it reaches the roots, and the person holding the hose gets sunburned standing there. When the wheel lands on noon, the person on duty waters at noon anyway, once, and then we all agree the wheel has a sense of humor.

The time spin also fixes the “I forgot” problem. A person who waters at a set time builds a habit. Seven in the morning, before school or work, becomes automatic by the third day. A person who waters “sometime today” forgets by dinner, because sometime today is not a time. Pick a time, write it on the chart, and the garden gets watered.

If the morning person has an early shift, they trade the week, not the day. Trading one day turns into trading every day, and then the wheel means nothing. Trade the whole week with someone else on the wheel, spin again for the next week, and move on.

Spin for the deep-water day

One day a week, the person on duty gives the garden a long, slow soak instead of a quick sprinkle. We call it the deep-water day, and the wheel picks which day it falls on. The deep soak matters because a quick sprinkle only wets the top inch of soil, and roots that live in the top inch dry out fast. A slow soak reaches down where the roots actually are, and deep roots handle a hot afternoon far better than shallow ones.

Our deep-water day is usually Saturday, because Saturday has time in it. A slow soak takes thirty minutes with the hose on low, moving from bed to bed, and nobody has thirty spare minutes on a Tuesday morning before work. The wheel picks the day, and I weighted this one wheel toward the weekend: it holds Saturday twice and the other days once each. I built it around our schedule, and the wheel still does the picking.

A soaker hose makes the deep-water day easy. Lay it along the tomato row, turn it on low, and walk away for twenty minutes. Come back, move it to the peppers, and repeat. The water seeps out slowly instead of running off the surface, and the soil drinks it instead of watching it flow down the path. If you do not have a soaker hose, a regular hose on the lowest setting works. It just takes longer, and the person on duty gets to stand in the garden for half an hour, which is not the worst way to spend a Saturday.

Spin for the pots and seedlings everyone forgets

Pots dry out faster than beds, and seedlings dry out faster than everything. A pot on a sunny patio can go from damp to dry in one hot afternoon, while the beds next to it still hold moisture. Our wheel has a pots spin for exactly this reason: one spin picks which pots get checked first, and the person on duty checks every pot, every day, even on skip days.

The pots wheel holds six: the basil pot, the mint pot, the strawberry pot, the tomato pots, the seedling tray, and the hanging baskets. Seedlings land on this wheel more often than I would like, because seedlings are the neediest plants in the garden and they punish forgetfulness fast. A seedling tray left dry for one afternoon wilts, and a wilted seedling sometimes comes back and sometimes does not. The wheel makes sure someone looks at the tray before it gets to that point.

Leo owns the seedling tray this month, unofficially. He started the seeds, so he checks them, wheel or no wheel. That is the one exception to the system, and it works because starting seeds creates a bond that a wheel cannot. If you start it, you watch it. The wheel covers everything else.

Put a saucer under every pot. A pot without a saucer loses water out the bottom in minutes, and the person watering has to guess whether the soil got any of it. A saucer catches the runoff, the soil drinks it back up, and the pot stays damp twice as long. It is a fifty-cent fix for a problem that kills more patio plants than any drought.

Keep the rotation where everyone can see it

The whole system lives on one sheet of paper on the fridge. Week number, name, beds order, time, deep-water day, and a row of boxes for each day. The person on duty checks a box after watering, writes SKIP on rain days, and everyone else can see at a glance whether the garden got water today. No group chat. No asking. The fridge tells the truth.

When a week ends, the next spin happens at Sunday dinner, in front of everyone. Spinning in front of everyone matters, because a wheel spun alone in a bedroom looks rigged, and a wheel spun at the table is a small event. Mia spins with great ceremony. She closes her eyes. She announces the name like a prize draw. The garden has never been watered so reliably, and I think the ceremony is half the reason.

Try it with your own garden, shared or not. Put the names on the Chore Wheel, add the beds, and let the wheel pick the week. If you garden alone, put the beds and the times on the wheel instead of names, and let it pick your order each week. The wheel will not make you love watering. It will make sure the watering happens, and the tomatoes will know the difference.

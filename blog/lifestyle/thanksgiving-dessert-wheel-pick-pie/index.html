Thanksgiving Dessert Wheel: Pick the Pie

It is Wednesday night, the turkey is already in the fridge, and the family cannot agree on pumpkin or pecan, who bakes, or whether anyone actually eats the cake. A Thanksgiving dessert wheel settles the pie, the baker, the backup, and the leftovers before the oven gets crowded.

It is Wednesday night, the day before Thanksgiving, and the fridge is already full. The turkey is brining in a pot so big it pushed the milk to the front of the shelf. The cranberry sauce is in a bowl with plastic wrap over it. And on the kitchen counter, next to a bag of flour nobody has opened yet, there is a list that says “dessert” with a question mark after it.

Maya wants pumpkin pie, because that is what Thanksgiving tastes like to her. Tom wants pecan, because last year his uncle brought one and Tom ate two slices and talked about it for a week. Grandma says she will bake whatever the family wants, which means she will bake three pies and stay up too late. Dad says to buy one from the bakery and be done with it, which is the kind of thing a person says when they are not the one doing the baking.

Nobody is wrong, and the kitchen does not have room for three pies and a cake. Put it on the Decision Wheel instead. One spin for the pie. One for who bakes it. One for the second dessert, one for the store-bought backup, one for the toppings, and one for who takes the leftovers home. Six spins, and the flour bag gets opened with a plan instead of an argument.

Spin for the pie

Start with the pie, because the pie decides the shopping list, the baking time, and how many people get the dessert they actually want. A pie argument is never really about pie. It is about whose childhood Thanksgiving counts.

Load the wheel with pumpkin, pecan, apple, sweet potato, cherry, and chocolate cream. Pumpkin means the classic spiced pumpkin pie, which is the right pick when the table has traditionalists and the whipped cream is already in the fridge. Pecan means the sweet, nutty pecan pie, which is the right pick when the group likes a dessert that eats like candy. Apple means a lattice-top apple pie, which suits a family that wants something a little tart after a heavy meal. Sweet potato means the smooth sweet potato pie, which is the right pick when Grandma has a recipe she trusts and the oven is free for an hour. Cherry means a tart cherry pie, which suits a table that wants a bright flavor after turkey and gravy. Chocolate cream means the cold chocolate cream pie, which is the right pick when the oven is full and the dessert needs to wait in the fridge.

Spin once, and that is the pie. The wheel lands on pumpkin, which suits Maya fine and disappoints Tom for about a minute, until he hears the second dessert spin is still coming. One rule keeps this spin honest: the pie that wins is the pie that gets baked, not the pie that gets baked plus the two runners-up. Grandma gets to sit down this year.

Write the pie name on the shopping list before anyone leaves for the store. A spin that picks pumpkin is no good if the store run comes home with pecan filling because someone changed their mind in the baking aisle.

Spin for who bakes it

The pie is picked, and now someone has to bake it. The baker spin picks one person so the kitchen has one cook instead of four people giving advice about crust while nobody preheats the oven.

Load the wheel with the names of the adults who can bake, plus one slot that says “bakery” for the year nobody has time. Maya bakes when she has a free afternoon and a recipe she has made before. Tom bakes when someone hands him a recipe and stays out of the kitchen. Grandma bakes when she wants to, which is most years, but the spin lets her pass without a guilt trip. Dad bakes when the recipe is simple and the crust comes from the store. Aunt June bakes when she is hosting, and her kitchen has two ovens. Bakery means the pie comes from the bakery down the road, which is the right pick when the week was long and the oven is already booked for the turkey.

Spin once, and that is the baker. If the wheel lands on Grandma, she bakes one pie, not three, and everyone else stays out of her way. If it lands on bakery, Dad picks it up on Wednesday, and nobody pretends a store pie is homemade. One rule helps: the baker picks the recipe, and the rest of the family does not send links to other recipes after the spin.

If the baker list is longer than the table and the names turn into their own debate, settle who is on the wheel with the Random Name Picker first. One draw for the shortlist, one spin for the baker, and the kitchen has a cook.

Spin for the second dessert

Every Thanksgiving table has at least one person who does not eat pie. It might be a kid who hates the texture, an uncle who never liked pumpkin, or a cousin who is full after the turkey and only wants something small. The second dessert spin picks one extra so that person gets a plate too, instead of sitting at the table watching everyone else eat.

Load the wheel with cake, cookies, brownies, ice cream, cheesecake, and fruit. Cake means a simple spice cake, which is the right pick when the table wants something soft and easy to slice. Cookies means a plate of sugar cookies, which suits a table with kids, because kids eat cookies while the adults are still deciding on pie. Brownies means a pan of brownies, which is the right pick when the oven is free after the pie comes out. Ice cream means a tub of vanilla ice cream, which is the dessert that also doubles as a pie topping. Cheesecake means a cold cheesecake from the fridge, which suits a table that wants something rich in a small slice. Fruit means a bowl of sliced pears and grapes, which is the right pick when the group is stuffed and wants something light.

Spin once, and that is the second dessert. The wheel lands on cookies, which means Tom’s kids get a plate they actually finish, and the adults get cookies with their coffee later. One rule helps: the second dessert is one dessert, not a dessert table. A table with a pie, a cake, cookies, and brownies is a bakery, and the fridge is already full.

I’ll be honest, the second dessert is the spin that saves the most feelings. The person who does not eat pie rarely complains about it. They just sit there quietly while everyone else has seconds. One spin fixes that without a speech about it.

Spin for the store-bought backup

Here is the thing. Sometimes the pie burns. Sometimes the crust sticks to the pan. Sometimes Wednesday turns into a long day at work, and the baker gets home at 8pm with no energy left for dough. The backup spin picks the store-bought dessert before the emergency, so the backup is a plan instead of a panic run on Thursday morning.

Load the wheel with bakery pie, grocery pie, ice cream cake, cookies, donuts, and fruit tart. Bakery pie means a whole pie from the bakery, which is the right pick when the family still wants a pie on the table. Grocery pie means a frozen pie from the store, which bakes in the oven while the turkey rests and costs less than the bakery version. Ice cream cake means a cake from the freezer section, which suits a family that wants dessert without turning the oven on again. Cookies means a box of bakery cookies, which is the right pick when the backup needs to feed a crowd fast. Donuts means a box of donuts, which is the dessert nobody planned and everybody eats. Fruit tart means a tart from the bakery case, which is the right pick when the table wants something that looks fancy with zero work.

Spin once, and write the backup on the shopping list next to the pie. The wheel lands on grocery pie, which means a frozen pumpkin pie sits in the freezer all week, untouched, unless the real pie fails. If the real pie comes out fine, the frozen one waits for the weekend, and nobody calls that a waste. A backup you never use is still a good backup.

Spin for the toppings

The pie is on the table, the plates are out, and now everyone wants something different on top. The toppings spin picks the topping setup so the table has one plan instead of six people opening the fridge at once looking for cream, ice cream, and cheese.

Load the wheel with whipped cream, ice cream, plain, cheddar, caramel, and cinnamon sugar. Whipped cream means a can of whipped cream or a bowl of hand-whipped cream, which is the right pick for pumpkin pie and the one most people reach for first. Ice cream means vanilla ice cream, which suits a warm slice of apple or pecan pie. Plain means the pie as it is, which is the right pick when the pie is good on its own and the baker wants people to taste it. Cheddar means a slice of sharp cheddar on apple pie, which is the old-school pick that half the table loves and half the table thinks is a joke. Caramel means a drizzle of caramel sauce, which suits pecan pie and makes a plain slice feel dressed up. Cinnamon sugar means a sprinkle of cinnamon sugar over whipped cream, which is the small touch that makes a store pie taste like someone tried.

Spin once for the table, and that is the topping setup. The wheel lands on whipped cream, which means one bowl of cream goes on the table and the ice cream stays in the freezer for the kids who ask for it. One rule helps: the topping spin sets the main topping, not the only topping. A person who wants cheddar on apple pie can still have it. The spin just stops six toppings from crowding the table at once.

Sounds silly, works great. A table with one topping bowl looks calm. A table with six topping containers looks like the fridge exploded next to the pie.

Spin for who takes leftovers home

Thanksgiving night, the plates are cleared, and there is half a pie left. The leftovers spin picks who takes it home so the pie does not sit in the host’s fridge for a week, slowly drying out, while everyone who wanted a slice forgot to ask.

Load the wheel with the names of the guests, plus one slot that says “host keeps” for the year the host actually wants it. Maya takes the pie when she has room in her fridge and a plan for Friday breakfast. Tom takes it when his kids are still awake and asking for one more slice. Grandma takes it when she wants pie with her morning coffee, which is most years. Dad takes it when the office has a Friday lunch and a pie makes him popular. Aunt June takes it when she hosted last year and missed out. Host keeps means the pie stays, which is the right pick when the host has a quiet weekend and a fork.

Spin once, and that is the pie’s new home. The wheel lands on Tom, which means the pie goes in a foil pan with a lid, not balanced on a plate under plastic wrap in someone’s car. One rule helps: the leftovers spin happens before dessert is served, not after everyone is putting on coats. A spin at the table takes ten seconds. A negotiation at the door takes ten minutes, and someone always leaves with a slice in a napkin.

If Friday dinner turns into a second round of “what do we eat with the leftovers,” put that call on the What to Eat Wheel instead of opening the fridge and staring. One spin for Friday dinner, and the leftover pie gets a proper second act.

Spin for the dessert table jobs

One last spin, and it is the one nobody thinks of until Thursday afternoon. The dessert table needs plates, forks, coffee, and someone to cut the pie. The jobs spin picks who does what so the host is not cutting pie, pouring coffee, and finding forks at the same time while the pie gets cold.

Load the wheel with cutter, coffee, plates, server, cleaner, and host rests. Cutter means the person who slices the pie, which is the job that needs a steady hand and a thin knife. Coffee means the person who makes the coffee pot and sets out the mugs, which is the job that keeps the adults at the table. Plates means the person who sets out the plates and forks before dessert, which is the job that takes two minutes and saves a scramble. Server means the person who carries slices to the older relatives, which is the job that makes Grandma smile. Cleaner means the person who clears the dessert plates after, which is the job nobody volunteers for and everybody appreciates. Host rests means the host sits down, which is the right pick when the host cooked all day and deserves a slice in peace.

Spin once per job, or spin once and let the winner pick their job first. The wheel lands on cutter for Dad, coffee for Maya, and plates for Tom, which means dessert starts on time and the host actually eats sitting down. One rule helps: the jobs spin happens on Wednesday, not Thursday at 4pm. A job assigned early is a job done. A job assigned late is a favor, and favors at Thanksgiving come with sighs.

Try the Thanksgiving dessert wheel

The dessert table starts on Wednesday night, and Wednesday night is where the wheel earns its keep. Load the Decision Wheel with pumpkin, pecan, apple, sweet potato, cherry, and chocolate cream, and spin for the pie before anyone goes to the store. If the baker list turns into its own debate, settle the shortlist with the Random Name Picker first. If Friday dinner needs a call, put it on the What to Eat Wheel and let the leftovers have their moment.

Spin for the pie today. Bake one pie, buy one backup, set out one bowl of whipped cream, and send the leftovers home with Tom. The flour bag will be empty, the fridge will have room again, and nobody will remember who argued about pecan on Wednesday night.

Summer Farmers Market Wheel: Pick the Saturday Haul

Last Saturday I got to the market at 8:05 with $40 in cash, two canvas bags, and a list on the back of a receipt. By 8:20 the receipt was gone, and the wheel was picking our haul instead.

Last Saturday I got to the farmers market at 8:05 with $40 in cash, two canvas bags, and a list I had written on the back of a receipt. By 8:20 the receipt was gone. It had slipped out of my pocket somewhere between the parking lot and the tomato stall, and my neighbor Sam and I stood in the middle of the market with no list, $40, and a full hour before the crowds got thick. Sam buys too much zucchini when left alone. I buy bread at three stalls and then wonder why we have no money left for fruit. We both knew how this trip usually ends: two bags of good intentions and a third bag of stuff we already had at home.

So I opened the What to Eat Wheel on my phone and put the haul on wheels. One spin for the produce, one for the bread, one for the flowers, one for the snack we would eat while we walked. The wheel did not make us smarter shoppers. It made us stop debating in the aisle, which is the same thing on a Saturday morning when the sun is already up and the good peaches are going fast.

Spin for the produce first

The produce spin comes first because it sets the rest of the bag. A market haul built around a random main item cooks better than a haul built around a list of twelve things you sort of want. Our wheel held six: tomatoes, peaches, corn, zucchini, green beans, and eggplant. The wheel landed on peaches, and peaches were a kind draw for a hot Saturday, because peaches decide the week. You eat them fresh for two days, you slice the soft ones over yogurt on day three, and the last two go into a crisp on Sunday if you have butter and oats at home.

I’ll be honest, I wanted tomatoes. Tomatoes were the reason we came, and the tomato stall had a line already. But a lead item picked by a spin ends the lead item debate, and peaches sent us to the fruit stall first, while the tomato line was still long. By the time we circled back, the line was down to three people, and the tomato farmer had put out a second crate. The peaches rode home in the top of the bag, where nothing sat on them, because Sam carried them in his hands the whole way back to the car like a man carrying eggs. That is the produce rule worth writing down: the softest item rides on top, and the person who picks it carries it.

One rule makes the produce spin work: only put items on the wheel that are in season at your market that week. A wheel full of asparagus in August gives an answer you cannot buy, and then you are back to debating in the aisle. Check the stalls first, walk the row once, and then load the wheel with what you actually saw. Six items is plenty. Twelve items means the wheel lands on something you forgot you added, and you buy it out of loyalty to the spin, which is how Sam ended up with zucchini three weeks in a row.

Spin for the bread and cheese

The bread spin is the one that saves the budget, because bread is where a market trip quietly doubles in price. One loaf is a plan. Three loaves from three stalls is a pantry problem. Our wheel held six: sourdough, baguette, focaccia, rye, cornbread, and rolls. The wheel landed on sourdough, and sourdough was the right call for a peach week, because sourdough with goat cheese and sliced peaches makes a lunch that needs no cooking, and a no-cooking lunch matters on a day when the kitchen is already hot by noon.

The cheese rides on the bread spin, not on its own spin, because cheese follows bread. Sourdough gets goat cheese. Baguette gets brie. Cornbread gets sharp cheddar. Focaccia gets whatever soft cheese the cheese stall has on sample that day. This is a pairing rule, not a law, and it keeps the cheese choice to one minute instead of ten. Ten minutes at the cheese stall is how you spend $18 on cheese and forget the flowers, and the flowers were the one thing Sam had asked for before we left the house.

A note from a market trip I got wrong: buy the bread after the produce, not before. We once bought a warm loaf first, carried it through a crowded row, and sat on it in the car on the way home. A loaf that gets sat on is still bread, but it is flat bread, and flat bread with peaches is a sad lunch. Produce first, bread second, cheese third, and the bread goes in its own bag, on top of nothing and under nothing. A paper bread bag costs nothing at the stall, and it is the difference between a loaf that survives the ride and a loaf that does not.

Spin for the flowers

The flowers spin is the smallest spin and the one people skip, because flowers feel like a luxury next to food. That is the wrong way to see it. A $10 bunch on the kitchen table lasts a week, and it is the one market purchase you look at every single day. Our wheel held six: sunflowers, zinnias, dahlias, wildflowers, lavender, and peonies. The wheel landed on sunflowers, and sunflowers were a fair draw for a small kitchen, because one tall stem in a jar looks like a decision, and five short stems in a jar look like clutter.

Here’s the thing about the flower spin: it needs a price cap before the spin, not after. Our cap was $10. The sunflower bunch was $8, so we had $2 left for a honey stick at the snack stall, and the $2 mattered, because a spin without a cap is how a $10 flower plan becomes a $22 flower plan with a vase you did not need. Tell the flower seller your cap if you have to. They have heard it before, and they will point you to the bunch that fits it.

One more flower rule: the flowers ride home in the footwell of the car, not in the trunk with the bags. A bunch in the trunk arrives bent, and a bent bunch goes straight into the compost, which is $8 in the bin. The footwell is cooler, darker, and flatter than the trunk, and the person in the passenger seat can hold the stems on a bumpy road. Sam held ours. He complained about it for two blocks and then forgot, because sunflowers in a car smell like August, and August is a hard smell to argue with.

Spin for the snack while you walk

The snack spin is the one that keeps the trip fun, because a market trip without a snack turns into a chore by the third stall. Our wheel held six: lemonade, iced coffee, kettle corn, a fruit cup, a honey stick, and a pastry. The wheel landed on lemonade, and lemonade was a good draw for a hot morning, because you can drink it while you walk, it does not leave crumbs in the produce bag, and one cup shared between two people costs half of two cups bought separately.

The rule for the snack spin is one snack, eaten while walking, and no second snack stop. A second snack stop is how a $40 market trip becomes a $55 market trip with a pastry box you did not plan for. One snack also gives the trip a shape: you buy it at the middle stall, you drink it through the second half of the row, and the empty cup goes in the trash can by the exit, not in the produce bag. A cup in the produce bag leaks, and lemonade on peaches is a mess nobody wants to clean in a parking lot.

A note from a market trip I got wrong: do not spin for the snack before you arrive. We once picked kettle corn at home, walked straight to the kettle corn stall, and skipped the whole first row because we were eating and walking in the wrong direction. Spin for the snack at the market, after you have walked the row once and seen what is actually there. A snack picked from the stalls in front of you is a snack you can buy in two minutes. A snack picked from home is a detour.

Spin for the stall order

The stall order spin sounds fussy until you have stood in a tomato line for twenty minutes while the bread stall sold out. Order matters at a market, because the popular stalls sell out and the long lines eat your hour. Our wheel held six orders: back row first, front row first, bread stall first, flower stall first, produce first, and shortest line first. The wheel landed on back row first, and back row first turned out to be a smart draw, because the back row had the same tomatoes as the front row with a line of three people instead of twenty.

Shortest line first deserves a word, because it is the order people pick when they are in a hurry, and it is a trap. The shortest line is often the stall with the least to sell, and a trip built on shortest lines ends at stalls you did not want, with a bag of things you would not have picked. Back row first or front row first is a real plan, because it walks the whole market in one direction and you see every stall once. Shortest line first is a zigzag, and a zigzag with two canvas bags is tiring by stall four.

If your market has a stall you always buy from, put that stall on the wheel as its own order, not as a rule you follow before the spin. A rule you follow before the spin is a spin that does not matter, and a spin that does not matter is a debate with extra steps. Our always-stall was the bread stall, and it stayed on the wheel as “bread stall first” instead of a pre-trip stop, because some weeks the wheel sent us to the back row first, and the bread was still there when we got to it. Bread sells out last. Tomatoes sell out first. That fact alone is worth a spin.

Spin for who carries what

The carrying spin is the one that ends the walk-home argument, because two bags and two people always produce a negotiation about weight. Our wheel held six carriers for the heavy bag: Sam carries it, I carry it, split it in the car, swap halfway, Sam carries produce and I carry bread, and I carry produce and Sam carries bread. The wheel landed on swap halfway, and swap halfway was fair for a walk of six blocks, because the produce bag gets heavier as you add the bread, and the person who starts with it should not also end with it.

The Random Name Picker can settle this spin if your group is bigger than two. Put the names on the wheel, spin once per bag, and write the result on your phone before you leave the stall. A result written down is a plan. A result remembered is a debate at the halfway point, and the halfway point of a six-block walk is a bad place for a debate, because one person is holding a heavy bag and the other person is holding flowers and a lemonade cup.

Two rules make the carrying spin work. First, the flowers never count as a bag. The person carrying the flowers still carries a bag, because flowers weigh nothing and everyone knows it. Second, the snack cup does not count either, for the same reason. A carrying spin with loopholes is a spin that one person games, and a gamed spin is worse than no spin, because it ends with someone carrying two bags and saying the wheel said so, which the wheel did not.

Spin for next week’s skip

The skip spin is the spin for the ride home, and it is the one that saves next week’s trip. Every market haul has one item that comes home too often. Ours was zucchini. We bought it three weeks in a row, cooked it once, and watched the rest go soft in the fridge. So on the ride home, we put last week’s haul on the Decision Wheel and spun for the one item that stays off next week’s list. The wheel landed on zucchini, and zucchini stayed home the next Saturday, which meant we had $6 and a bag slot for green beans, and the green beans actually got cooked.

The skip spin works because it is a ban with an end date. A forever ban on zucchini starts an argument about zucchini. A one-week skip is a joke everyone agrees to, and a joke everyone agrees to is a rule that holds. Next week, zucchini goes back on the wheel, and if it lands again, it stays off again, and nobody has to defend a vegetable in the car. A skip list also keeps the haul honest: if an item lands on the skip wheel three weeks running, it is not a market item for your house right now, and that is useful to know, even if it stings a little when the item is peaches in a bad year.

One last skip rule: the skip spin picks one item, not three. Three skips is a new list, and a new list is a debate in the car, which is where this whole trip started. One skip, written on the same phone note as the carrying result, and next Saturday starts with a wheel that already knows what not to buy. That is the whole trick of a market wheel, really. It does not pick better food. It picks food faster, with fewer arguments, and leaves you the hour to walk the row twice and buy the thing you did not know you wanted until you saw it.

Try the summer farmers market wheel

A Saturday haul does not need a lost receipt and a debate in the aisle. It needs one lead item that sets the bag, one loaf the cook can actually use, one bunch under a $10 cap, one snack eaten while you walk, one row order that walks the market in a single direction, one carrying plan written on your phone, and one skip for next week. Put tomatoes, peaches, corn, zucchini, green beans, and eggplant on the What to Eat Wheel and spin for the produce first. Then spin for the bread, the flowers, and the snack, and let the wheel carry the arguments so you can carry the peaches.

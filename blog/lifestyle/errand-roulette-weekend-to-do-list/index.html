Errand Roulette: Tackle the Weekend To-Do List

One spin assigns the errands, nobody negotiates. A Saturday system for groceries, the post office, and the rest of the list nobody wants.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 23, 2026 Updated October 23, 2026

On this page

  1. Spin the assignments, don’t negotiate them
  2. Give everyone three spins, not one
  3. The one-trade rule
  4. Add the veto for the truly awful ones
  5. Run the errand race
  6. Spin for the order too
  7. What goes on the wheel and what doesn’t
  8. Give it one Saturday

Saturday, 9:14am. I’m at the kitchen table with a list of eleven errands and the full weekend draining out in front of me. Groceries, post office, oil change, drop the dry cleaning, refill the prescription, wash the car, pick up the kid’s soccer cleats from the store, returns to the mall, library books due Monday, car inspection, and one vague item that just says “Costco??” in my own handwriting.

My partner glances at the list and says, “I’ll do the Costco run,” which is the one fun errand on the whole page. I say, “Great, and the rest?” Silence. The dog is asleep. Nobody wants the post office.

That used to be the whole morning: ten minutes of negotiating who does what, everybody cherry-picking, and the post office still sitting there at noon. Now we play errand roulette. Eleven errands go on the Chore Wheel, we spin for assignments, and by 9:30 everybody’s in their car. Here’s how it works.

Spin the assignments, don’t negotiate them

The reason errand negotiation eats the morning is that it feels like bargaining and nobody wants to lose. A wheel fixes it because the loss isn’t personal. You didn’t pick the post office. The spin did.

Type every errand onto the wheel, one per segment. Spin once per person, in age order or reverse age order, whatever causes the least discussion. Each person gets the errand the pointer lands on. Spin through the list until every errand has a name. Takes about two minutes and it’s genuinely hard to argue with a thing you watched happen.

One rule worth stating out loud before the first spin: an errand you land on is yours. If the wheel gives you the DMV, the wheel gave you the DMV. Complaints to the wheel, which does not care.

Give everyone three spins, not one

One errand each doesn’t clear an eleven-item list in a two-person house. We spin three times per person and keep all three. That sounds like a lot until you realize most errands are twenty-minute jobs. Three spins is an hour of work, which is about right for a Saturday morning.

With kids in the house, spin separately. Adults get the full wheel. Kids get their own wheel with kid-sized errands: return library books, walk the dog, wipe down the car interior, sort the recycling, dust the living room. My nine-year-old landed on “clean out the car” last month and found eleven dollars in change under the seats. He considers errand day profitable now.

The one-trade rule

Someone will land on the one errand they truly hate, and forcing it breeds resentment, which defeats the point. So each person gets exactly one trade. You can swap with anyone, but only if both people agree, and only one trade per person per Saturday.

The one-trade limit matters. Unlimited trades turns it back into negotiation, just with extra steps. One trade is enough to feel fair without reopening the whole debate. In practice, about half our Saturdays have zero trades. The limit makes people take their spin seriously.

If you’re stuck in a multi-household or roommate situation where trades are getting complicated, the Decision Wheel handles straight either-or calls cleanly. But for a normal Saturday list, one trade each is plenty.

Add the veto for the truly awful ones

Some errands aren’t just boring, they’re bad. The smog check with a two-hour line. The returns desk at the mall the week before Christmas. These need a different treatment or one person eats a whole afternoon on a bad spin.

Write the three worst errands on a second wheel. Spin once. Whoever it lands on gets that errand, and they get to pick one bonus from the main list to skip. It keeps the terrible jobs on the wheel instead of quietly getting postponed every week until they’re emergencies. The Costco?? errand sat for three weeks in my house until it became a smog-check-style disaster. It would have been a Tuesday job if a spin had assigned it earlier.

Run the errand race

This one is for families with competitive kids, or competitive adults who won’t admit it. Everybody gets their errands, and the first person to finish all of theirs picks what’s for lunch. That’s it. That’s the whole rule.

The race changes the psychology completely. Suddenly the post office isn’t a chore, it’s a time trial. My kids leave the house like it’s a fire drill. We keep a running tally on the fridge: me 4 wins, partner 3, kids 6 combined. The prize costs me nothing, lunch was happening anyway, but people shave real minutes off their errands. The whole list is done by 11:30 most weeks, and the afternoon is actually free.

A small tip: the race only works if the errands are roughly equal effort. If someone spins the oil change and someone else spins the library drop-off, the finish was decided before anyone left the driveway. Balance the wheel before you spin. Pair one long errand with two short ones per person.

Spin for the order too

If two people need the one car, or the grocery run has to happen before noon, the order of errands matters. Spin for departure times. Whoever wins the first spin leaves first, second spin leaves second. Dead simple.

This also solves the “I’ll wait for you” trap, where everybody plans to go together and nobody leaves. One car, one person, one spin. The solo driver gets the podcast pick as a consolation prize. My partner chose a true-crime series last week and now volunteers for the solo runs.

For families that rotate who does what across weekends, load names onto the Random Name Picker and spin for the chore captain. The captain runs the wheel next Saturday. It rotates the admin work too, which is the part nobody thinks about until it’s always the same person doing it.

What goes on the wheel and what doesn’t

The wheel is for errands that are annoying, forgettable, or fighting-worthy. Groceries, returns, pickups, drop-offs, the car stuff, the yard stuff, the pharmacy run. It’s perfect for those because the stakes are low and the resistance is pure inertia.

Don’t put appointments on the wheel. The dentist appointment at 2pm doesn’t get spun. Anything with a fixed time, a deadline, or a legal consequence gets assigned directly by whoever manages the calendar. The wheel handles the flexible list. When a spin assigns “pick up the prescription” and the pharmacy closes at 1pm, the person who spun it does it first. Common sense still applies.

Also don’t put cooking on the wheel if someone genuinely can’t cook. A wheel is fair, not magical. My partner cannot make rice without it becoming a project. I make the rice. The wheel stays out of it.

Give it one Saturday

The first run takes a little setup: writing eleven errands onto the wheel, explaining the trade rule, somebody asking if they can veto the smog check. The second Saturday takes five minutes and the third takes three. By then it’s just what your family does.

Our record for the whole list, eleven errands, four people: done by 11:12, lunch at the taco truck, nobody mad. Compare that to the old days, where the list was still half-done at dinner and the post office was “a Monday problem.”

Load your Saturday list onto the Chore Wheel and spin it once. Worst case, you spent two minutes and went back to negotiating. Best case, errands stop being the argument and start being the game.

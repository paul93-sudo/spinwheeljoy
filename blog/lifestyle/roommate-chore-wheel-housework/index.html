Roommate Chore Wheel: Split Housework Without the Passive Aggression

Tired of tracking who did the dishes last? Load your chores and roommates' names onto a chore wheel and split housework in one spin, no charts, no fights, no sticky notes.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 18, 2026 Updated October 18, 2026

On this page

  1. Who does the dishes tonight
  2. Bathroom duty
  3. Trash and recycling day
  4. The monthly deep clean draft
  5. Who buys the shared stuff
  6. Shared laundry and linens
  7. The one rule that holds it all together

The fight started over a sponge. Not a bill, not rent, a three-dollar sponge. It had been sitting in the sink for four days, getting that smell, and my roommate Marcus looked at it the way you’d look at a crime scene. “Did you use this?” he asked. I had not used it. Neither had he, supposedly. We both stood there like detectives at a murder that was, in fairness, about dishwashing.

Every shared apartment has a version of this. The trash fills up while two adults walk past it. Someone’s dishes “soak” for 36 hours. The bathroom gets cleaned only when a parent visits. The problem isn’t that roommates are lazy. The problem is nobody wrote down whose turn it is, so everyone has a different version of whose turn it is.

I’ll be honest: we tried a chore chart. It lasted nine days. The chart was fair on paper, but nobody opened the fridge door to look at it, and the only entries were me initialing things Marcus did and Marcus initialing things I did. What finally worked was dumber and faster. We put our names and the chores on the Chore Wheel, spin it every Sunday night, and live with whatever comes out. Here’s the thing, the wheel doesn’t argue back, and that’s the whole point.

These are the splits that actually matter in a shared place.

Who does the dishes tonight

Dishes are the number-one roommate fight in America, and I’m not citing a study for that, I’m citing every group chat I’ve ever been in. Our rule now: after dinner, the wheel gets one spin with both our names. Winner, sorry, loser does the dishes. It takes five seconds, and the decision is made before anyone has a chance to say “I’ll get them in a minute.”

Here’s what surprised us. Once the wheel decides, the loser just does it. There’s no negotiation phase, no “well you cooked so I should…” lecture. The randomness removes the blame. You can’t be mad at a spin. You can be mad at a person who spun it, which is why we spin in front of each other. Public spins only. That’s a house rule, and it matters.

Bathroom duty

Nobody volunteers to clean the bathroom. Ever. In three years of shared apartments, I have never once heard someone say “I got the bathroom this week, it’s fine.” So this chore never gets volunteered for, which means it never gets done, which means the grout starts looking like a science project.

Load the names, spin once a week. The loser cleans the toilet, the sink, the mirror, and the shower floor. Ten minutes with a sponge and it’s done. Ten minutes is nothing. The hard part was never the cleaning, it was deciding whose ten minutes.

We added a bonus rule: whoever lands on bathroom duty gets to pick what’s for dinner that night. Sounds silly, works great. It turned the worst chore into a small bargaining chip, and Marcus now pretends to be disappointed when he loses the spin, but he picked tacos twice in a row.

Trash and recycling day

Our building’s trash day is Wednesday. The bins have to go to the curb Tuesday night. For months, this happened because one of us noticed the neighbors’ bins outside and panicked. It was a system. A bad one, but a system.

The wheel does this one weekly, same spin as dishes night, just a second spin. Two names, one job. The winner of the spin puts the bags out and brings the bins back in the next morning. Thirty seconds of work. The old way took thirty seconds of work too, but it also took a full evening of “were you going to take the trash out” texts, and I will take the bin over the texts every time.

If your city has separate recycling and compost, make it three spins. Same names, same rules. Don’t overthink it.

The monthly deep clean draft

Weekly chores keep a place livable. Monthly chores keep it from slowly becoming a health-code violation. The floors need mopping. The fridge needs the science experiments evicted. The microwave needs someone to deal with whatever exploded in there in March.

Once a month, we do a “draft.” I put the four big jobs on the wheel (mop floors, clean fridge, scrub microwave, dust the living room) and Marcus and I take turns spinning until each of us has two. Draft order matters, so we spin for that too, with our names. It’s a whole ceremony. It takes maybe three minutes and it has replaced the quarterly screaming match we used to have right before a landlord inspection.

Pro tip from experience: do the draft on the first of the month, and do the chores that same day. If you draft on Sunday and schedule for “sometime this week,” sometime never comes.

Who buys the shared stuff

Toilet paper, dish soap, trash bags, the sponges nobody wants to fight over. These things run out at the worst times, and whoever buys them ends up quietly resenting everyone else for the next six months.

Two spins solve this. Spin one picks who does the store run this month. Spin two happens at checkout: if the total is over twenty bucks, you text the other person a photo of the receipt and split it. Our threshold is twenty dollars because below that it’s not worth the paperwork. You can set yours at ten, or fifty, or zero if you’re both accountants. The number doesn’t matter. Having a number matters.

We keep a shared note on our phones with the running total. At the end of the month, the person who owes money sends it, no reminders needed. So far, in fourteen months, we’ve needed exactly one reminder. It was me. I forgot. The wheel didn’t care.

Shared laundry and linens

If you share a bathroom, you share hand towels. If you share a couch, you share the throw blankets. These things get gross in a way that’s nobody’s specific fault, so they belong to everyone, which means nobody washes them.

Add “wash the shared stuff” as a wheel segment. One spin a month, the loser throws the bath mats, hand towels, and couch blankets in the wash. It’s one load. It takes four minutes of actual effort, and the couch stops smelling like old pizza. I cannot stress enough how much nicer an apartment feels when the shared fabrics are clean. It’s the cheapest apartment upgrade that exists.

This one also works if you have a third roommate. Three names, same spin. The math of fairness gets weird with three people, but the wheel doesn’t do math, it does spins, and nobody can argue with a spin.

The one rule that holds it all together

Everything above falls apart without this: the spin is final. No redos, no “best of three,” no “that doesn’t count because it was still moving.” We learned this the hard way. The first week, Marcus lost the dishes spin and asked for a redo because the wheel “wobbled.” I gave it to him. He won. I did the dishes, and I was mad about it for two days, not because of the dishes but because the system had a loophole.

Now the rule is written on a sticky note next to the wheel’s bookmark on our phones: spin stands, no redos. It’s been fourteen months. We’ve argued about lots of things since then. Chores isn’t one of them.

If you need to settle something that isn’t a chore, like whose turn it is to pick the movie or which takeout place to order from, the Decision Wheel handles those, and the Random Name Picker is good for picking names when there are no chores attached at all.

Spin the Chore Wheel this Sunday. Five minutes of spinning buys you a week of not fighting about a sponge.

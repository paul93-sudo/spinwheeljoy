Grocery Run Wheel: Decide Who Does the Weekly Shop

The fridge is empty on Sunday night and four people are all sure it is someone else's turn. A grocery run wheel settles the weekly shop, the store, and the unload in a few spins.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published November 1, 2026 Updated November 1, 2026

On this page

  3. Check the pantry, then write the list

  5. Spin for who unloads and puts it away
  6. Put a number on the week

It is Sunday night at 6. The fridge holds half a carton of milk, one egg, and a jar of pickles nobody admits to buying. Maya says she shopped last week. Dan says he did the big shop two weeks ago and Maya only grabbed milk. Priya says she has a work deadline and Sam is already putting his shoes on, which in this house means he plans to be out the door before anyone can ask him to go.

I’ll be honest. Nobody in that kitchen is lying. They just remember different weeks. A shared house runs through a lot of small shops, and after a month nobody can say for sure whose turn it is. That is the whole job of a grocery run wheel. It keeps the turn visible so the argument never starts.

Load the Chore Wheel with the names of everyone who shops for the house. Spin once for this week’s shopper. Write the name on the fridge calendar before anyone leaves the kitchen. The wheel does not care who shopped last time. The calendar does, and that is the point.

Start with a four-week rotation

Four people, four weeks, one shop each. That is the simplest version and it works for most houses.

Put Maya, Dan, Priya, and Sam on the wheel. Spin once. Maya lands on week one. Spin again for week two, with Maya’s name off the wheel. Dan lands on week two. Spin twice more. Priya gets week three, Sam gets week four. Four spins, one month planned, no meeting needed.

Here’s the thing. The rotation only holds if the names come off the wheel after each spin. If Maya stays on the wheel for week two, she can land twice in a row, and then the house is right back to arguing about fairness. One spin, one name, name off. When all four weeks are filled, the wheel resets for next month.

Write the rotation where everyone can see it. A paper calendar on the fridge beats a note in a group chat. A group chat note scrolls away by Tuesday. The fridge calendar is still there on Sunday when the next shop comes due.

One more detail that saves trouble. Agree on what counts as the weekly shop. In this house, the weekly shop means the main trip that stocks the kitchen for the week: milk, eggs, bread, vegetables, rice, and whatever the house agreed to cook. A quick run for ice cream at 9 at night does not count. Neither does grabbing milk on the way home from work. If the house does not agree on this line, the wheel will settle the turn and the argument will just move to what counts.

Spin for the store before the list

The store matters more than people admit. The big supermarket is cheaper for a full shop but takes an hour. The corner shop is ten minutes away but costs more for the same basket. The bulk store is great for rice and oats and terrible for fresh vegetables. Sending someone to the wrong store wastes their whole evening.

Spin for the store on the same night you spin for the shopper. Put the real options on the wheel: the supermarket, the corner shop, the bulk store, and the market stall on Saturday. The shopper for the week spins the store wheel once. If the market stall lands and the shop is on a Tuesday, that pick does not work. So keep the store wheel to stores that are open on the shop day. A wheel only feels fair when every option on it can actually happen.

This spin also stops a quiet habit. In a lot of houses, one person always shops at the cheap supermarket because they know the aisles, and another person always shops at the corner shop because it is close. Over a month, the corner-shop person spends more money and more trips for the same food. One store spin per week spreads the long trips and the quick trips around.

If two shoppers share a week, spin the store twice. One store for the main shop, one for the top-up. The point stays the same. The wheel picks the store so no one person always gets the hour-long trip.

Check the pantry, then write the list

The shopper wastes the most money on food the house already owns. A second bag of rice when the cupboard already holds two. A third jar of pasta sauce. Another bunch of bananas when the fruit bowl is full.

So the rule is simple. Before the shop, the shopper for the week checks the pantry, the fridge, and the freezer. Ten minutes, three places, one list. Write down what is missing. Cross off what is already there. The list goes on the fridge so anyone can add to it during the week.

Spin for one extra job here if the house keeps forgetting this step. Put the list jobs on the wheel: check the pantry, check the fridge, check the freezer, write the list. One person can do all four in ten minutes, but splitting the check makes it harder to skip. In a house of four, the week’s shopper checks the pantry and writes the list. Someone else checks the fridge and freezer on Saturday and adds the missing items to the list before Sunday.

Sounds silly, works great. A list written from the pantry costs less than a list written from memory in the cereal aisle. The house in this story cut its weekly shop by about twenty dollars once the pantry check became a rule. Your number will differ. The point is that the check pays for the ten minutes it takes.

Keep the list to one page. If the list runs to two pages, the shop has turned into a stock-up trip, and that is a different job with a different budget. One page, one week, one shop.

Split the big shop from the top-up runs

A week of food never survives in one trip. Milk runs out on Wednesday. Bread goes stale on Thursday. Someone decides to cook soup on Friday and the house has no carrots. Top-up runs are normal. The mistake is treating them as free.

Spin for the top-up runner at the start of the week, separate from the main shopper. The top-up runner handles the small trips: milk, bread, fruit, anything that runs out midweek. One person, one week, small trips only. When the top-up runner is also the main shopper, that person shops twice and nobody notices until they complain.

Set a cap for top-ups. Twenty dollars works for a house of four in this story. Your house will have its own number. The cap stops a top-up run from turning into a second full shop. The runner texts the receipt to the group chat. No receipt, no payback from the shared pot. That rule sounds strict. It ends more arguments than any other rule in this article.

If the house cooks together on weekends, spin for the weekend cook shop too. The weekend cook picks the recipe and shops for it. That trip is not part of the weekly shop and not part of top-ups. It is its own job, with its own spin, and the cook eats the result of their own shopping. Funny how careful people get with the list when they cook the meal.

Spin for who unloads and puts it away

Shopping is half the job. The bags still sit on the counter when the shopper gets home, and in a lot of houses they sit there until someone hungry opens them. Unloading takes fifteen minutes and nobody wants to do it after a long shop.

Spin for the unloader before the shopper leaves. The unloader meets the bags at the door, puts cold food in the fridge and freezer first, then dry food in the pantry. Cold food first is a food safety rule, not a preference. Milk and meat sit in the fridge within half an hour of coming home. Everything else can wait ten minutes.

The unloader also does one small check. They look at the receipt and check that the bags hold what the list asked for. If the shopper forgot the eggs, the unloader says so that night, not on Sunday when the house tries to cook breakfast. A missed item found on shopping day is a quick top-up run. A missed item found on Sunday morning is a ruined breakfast.

Rotate the unloader with the shopper, but never give both jobs to the same person in the same week. The shopper shops. Someone else unloads. That split is what makes the weekly shop feel shared instead of dumped on one person.

Put a number on the week

A grocery wheel without a budget still works, but a budget makes it work better. Pick a weekly number together. Look at the last four weeks of receipts, add them up, divide by four. That average is your starting number. Round it to a clean figure. If the average is 143 dollars, call the budget 140.

Spin for the budget keeper each month. The budget keeper holds the shared pot or the shared card, collects receipts, and reports the total each Sunday. One text in the group chat: this week we spent 132, budget 140, eight left. No lecture, no spreadsheet. Just the number.

When the wheel lands on a week where the shop goes over budget, the house does not argue about who overspent. The budget keeper notes the overage and the next week starts fresh. If three weeks in a row go over, the house talks about the budget number itself. Maybe the number was too low. Maybe the list grew. Either way, the talk is about the number, not about the shopper.

The Decision Wheel can settle the small calls that come up around the budget, like whether the house buys the name-brand cereal or the store brand this week. One spin, one answer, no ten-minute debate in the aisle.

Keep a swap rule for sick weeks

Someone will get sick on their shopping week. Someone will work late. Someone will leave town for a weekend. A rotation without a swap rule breaks the first time life gets in the way.

The swap rule is this. The shopper who cannot shop trades with the next person on the rotation. Maya cannot shop week two, so Dan takes week two and Maya takes week three. The trade is a swap, not a skip. Maya still shops that month, just one week later. No one shops twice to cover someone else, and no one gets a free month.

If the swap happens less than a day before the shop, the person who cannot shop still writes the list and checks the pantry. The stand-in shopper only does the trip. That split keeps the swap fair. The stand-in gives an hour. The original shopper still does the ten-minute pantry check.

Keep a bench name too. The Random Name Picker can draw one backup name each month. The bench person only shops if two people cancel in the same week. Most months the bench person never shops. The months they do, they already knew they might, so the call feels normal instead of desperate.

Try the grocery run wheel

The weekly shop does not need a better shopper. It needs a turn everyone can see, a store picked before the list is written, a pantry check before the money is spent, and an unloader waiting at the door. A wheel does all of that in a few minutes on Sunday night.

Load the Chore Wheel with the names in your house. Spin for this week’s shopper, the store, and the unloader. Write the rotation on the fridge calendar. Set the weekly number and the top-up cap. Then go cook dinner with food that is actually in the fridge. The Decision Wheel can settle what to cook with it, if the house still has energy left for one more spin.

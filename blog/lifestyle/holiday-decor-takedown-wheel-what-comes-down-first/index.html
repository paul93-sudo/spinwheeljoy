Holiday Decor Takedown Wheel: What Comes Down First

On January 2 our tree was still up, the stockings were still on the mantel, and nobody in the house would start. A decor takedown wheel picks the order, the jobs, and the day, so the boxes get packed before February.

On January 2, our tree was still up. The stockings were still on the mantel. The string lights were still on the porch, on a timer nobody had changed since Christmas Eve, so they clicked on at five every evening and lit up an empty driveway. My wife looked at the living room and said, “We should take this down.” I said, “Yep.” Then we both sat there for another twenty minutes, because taking it down is a job nobody wants to start, and a job with no starting point is a job that waits until February.

Here is the problem with takedown. Putting the decorations up felt like an event. Taking them down feels like cleaning up after an event, and nobody volunteers for cleanup. So the tree stays up one more day, then one more week, and by the third week of January you are eating dinner next to a tree that has dropped half its needles on the rug and you have stopped seeing it entirely.

So I put the takedown on the Chore Wheel and let it decide. One spin per call. The order, the jobs, the rooms, the day. Nobody argues with the wheel, because the wheel did not pick favorites. It picked a segment.

Spin for what comes down first

Start with the order, because the order is the part everyone argues about. One person wants the tree down first. Another person wants the outside lights down before the next storm. A third person thinks the stockings should stay up because taking them down feels sad. All three of them are right, and all three of them will keep the whole job stuck if they have to agree first.

Put the big jobs on the wheel: tree, outside lights, stockings, wreath, table centerpiece, porch garland. Spin once. Whatever it lands on comes down first, no discussion. When the tree came up for us, I took the ornaments off while my wife held the box, and twenty minutes later the biggest job in the house was done. The rest of the list looked smaller after that, because it was smaller.

If the wheel lands on a job you dread, that is the point. The dreaded job is the one that keeps the whole list waiting. Do it first and the rest of the afternoon goes faster than you expect.

Spin for who takes down what

Once the order is set, spin for the people. Put the names on the wheel and spin for each job, one name per job. The tree goes to whoever the wheel picks. The lights go to the next name. The stockings go to the next. Nobody picks their own job, and nobody gets stuck with every job because they were the one standing in the room when someone said, “Somebody should take this down.”

In our house the rule is simple. The wheel picks the person, the person does the job, and the person who finishes first spins for the next job. That last part matters. The fast finisher does not get to sit down and watch everyone else work. The wheel hands them the next job, and the next job is usually the one nobody wanted, like untangling the light strand that got wound up wrong last year.

If you have kids, spin for them too, with jobs that fit. A ten-year-old can take the stockings down and fold the garland into a box. A six-year-old can carry the small ornaments to the box one at a time, which takes longer and keeps them busy longer, and both of those things are good. Spin for the room order

Takedown is easier room by room than job by job across the whole house. Put the rooms on the wheel: living room, porch, dining room, bedrooms, kitchen. Spin once, and that room gets finished completely before you move on. Ornaments off, boxes packed, surfaces wiped, done.

This room rule stops the most common takedown failure I know. You take the ornaments off the tree, then you go outside for the lights, then you come back in and take the stockings down, and at the end of the day every room is half done and the boxes are still open in the hallway. A room that is finished feels done. Five rooms that are half done feel like nothing happened.

Our living room took forty minutes from spin to done, because the tree was the big job and the tree was already out by then. The porch took ten minutes, because the lights came down in one strand and the garland went straight into a bag. The dining room took five minutes, because the centerpiece was one box and a wipe of the table. Forty plus ten plus five is an afternoon that ends with the house back to normal, instead of an afternoon that ends with boxes in the hallway.

Spin for keep, store, or toss

Every box you open during takedown has something in it that should not go back in the box. An ornament with a broken hook. A light strand where half the bulbs are dead. A stocking that has lost its loop. A garland that sheds every time you touch it. Put three options on the wheel: keep, store, or toss. Spin once per item, and do what the wheel says.

I will be honest, this is the section I resisted. I wanted to keep everything, because every ornament came from somewhere and throwing one away felt like throwing away the memory. But a box full of broken things is not a memory. It is a box you dread opening next December. The wheel made the calls I would not make, and three ornaments went in the trash, two light strands went in the trash, and the box that went back to the attic was lighter and easier to carry.

Here is a rule that helps. If an item broke this year and you did not fix it before takedown, it goes on the wheel. If the wheel says toss, it goes. If the wheel says keep, you fix the hook before it goes in the box, not next December when you open the box and find the same broken hook again.

Spin for the outdoor takedown day

Outside lights are the job that waits longest, because outside in January is cold and the lights are still working fine. They are also the job that turns into a problem, because a light strand left up through a February storm comes down in pieces, and a wreath left on the door through a wet month comes down soggy.

Put four days on the wheel: Saturday, Sunday, next Saturday, next Sunday. Spin once. That day is the outdoor day, and you write it on the calendar where you will see it. If the wheel lands on a day with rain in the forecast, spin again for the time of day, not the day itself. Morning is usually calmer than afternoon in January, and an hour of morning light is enough for lights and a wreath.

Our outdoor day landed on a Saturday with a clear morning. The lights came down in twenty minutes, the wreath came off the door, and the porch looked like a porch again instead of a porch waiting for a holiday that already happened. The timer went in the box with the lights, so next December we find the timer and the lights in the same box, which is a small thing that saves a real argument next year.

Spin for where each box goes

Takedown ends with boxes, and boxes end up wherever someone sets them down, which is usually the hallway, the garage floor, or the spare room. Put the storage spots on the wheel: attic, garage shelf, closet top, spare room. Spin once per box, and the box goes where the wheel says, the same day it gets packed.

This sounds fussy. It is not. A box that sits in the hallway for a week becomes a box that sits in the hallway for a month, and a box that sits in the hallway for a month becomes a box you trip over and swear at. A box that goes straight to the attic is a box you do not think about again until December, and in December you open the attic and find boxes with labels, not a hallway full of boxes you forgot.

Label each box before you spin for its spot. Tree ornaments, lights, stockings, porch. One word per box, written on tape with a marker. The label is what makes the attic work next year. Without labels, the attic is just a hallway with a ladder.

Spin for the last thing standing

Every house has one decoration that stays up longest. In our house it is the small wreath on the kitchen window, because nobody sees it from the street and my wife likes it. In other houses it is the porch lights, or the stockings, or the tree in the spare room nobody goes into. Picking the last thing standing on purpose beats finding it in March and feeling guilty about it.

Put the remaining decorations on the wheel after everything else is down. Spin once. That decoration stays up until a date you spin for next: the end of January, the first weekend of February, or the day the next holiday shows up. Write the date on the calendar, same as the outdoor day. When the date comes, the decoration comes down, no new spin needed, because you already made the call.

Sounds silly, works great. A house with one wreath left on purpose looks like a house that chose to keep a wreath. A house with a tree still up in February looks like a house that forgot. The difference is one spin and one date on the calendar.

Try the holiday decor takedown wheel

Put your jobs on the Chore Wheel, spin for the order, the people, the rooms, and the day, and take the first thing down tonight. One job, twenty minutes, box labeled, box stored. The rest of the list gets easier once the first job is done, because the hardest part of takedown was never the work. It was deciding where to start.

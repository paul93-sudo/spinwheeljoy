New Year's Resolution Wheel: Pick the One Habit You'll Keep

Last January you probably wrote down ten resolutions and kept none of them. Put six honest options on a wheel, spin once, and give that one habit a full month before you judge it.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 24, 2026 Updated October 24, 2026

On this page

  1. One habit, not twelve
  2. Put only real options on the wheel
  3. Shrink it until a bad day still works
  4. Tie it to something you already do
  5. Check in once a week, not every night
  6. The miss rule: spin again, don’t quit
  7. Hand the wheel to someone else
  8. Try the resolution wheel for January

Every January I write the same list. Exercise more. Save money. Read more books. Cook at home. Call my parents more. Learn Spanish, which I have now put on the list four years in a row without learning a single verb. By February, the list is still on the fridge, and I am doing none of it, because a list of ten things is really a list of zero things. Nobody keeps ten new habits at once. The people who change something in January change one thing.

Last year I tried a different approach. I put six options on a Decision Wheel, spun it in front of my wife, and it landed on walk. Thirty minutes, after dinner, most nights. That one I actually did, because one habit fits in a day and ten do not. Here is how to run the same pick for yourself, and how to keep the habit past the part where January enthusiasm runs out.

One habit, not twelve

The resolution list fails for a boring reason: each new habit costs attention, and attention is limited. You can white-knuckle your way through a week of five changes, and then a bad Tuesday arrives, you skip all five, and the whole list feels ruined. One habit survives a bad Tuesday. You miss one walk, you walk the next night, and nothing else in your life falls apart.

So the wheel gets one job: pick the single habit. Not the top three. Not a backup in case the first one gets boring. One segment, one habit, one month of honest effort before you decide whether it stays.

If you already know what your one habit is, you do not need a wheel. Most people do not know. They know they want “to be better.” A vague wish like that gives the wheel nothing to pick from. The wheel forces you to name six concrete things, and that naming is half the work.

Put only real options on the wheel

A wheel only works if every segment is something you would actually do. This is where most people cheat, and they cheat in both directions. They put “run a marathon” on the wheel next to “drink more water,” which is not a fair fight, and then they are shocked when the marathon wins and they quit by week two. Or they put six versions of the same habit on it, which is just picking the habit with extra steps.

Write six options that are all about the same size. Walk 30 minutes, read 20 pages, cook dinner four nights a week, save 50 dollars a week, call one friend a week, sleep by 11pm. Each one takes roughly the same effort. Each one fits in a normal day. If one option makes you wince when you read it, take it off before you spin. A wheel full of options you dread is a wheel that picks your next excuse.

And be honest about the season of your life. A new parent should not put “wake up at 5am to journal” on the wheel. A person working night shifts should not put “morning run” on it. The habit has to fit the life you have in January, not the life you wish you had.

Shrink it until a bad day still works

Here is the thing people get wrong about resolutions: they size the habit for their best day. On a good Saturday you can walk for an hour, cook a full dinner, and read fifty pages. On a bad Wednesday you get home late, you are tired, and you have twenty minutes before you fall asleep on the couch. The habit has to work on that Wednesday, or it is not a habit, it is a weekend project.

So shrink the winning habit until it survives a bad day. If the wheel lands on walk, set the rule as one trip around the block. Ten minutes, out the door, done. On a good day you will walk longer, because you are already outside. On a bad day you still did the thing, and the streak stays alive.

I use a five-minute floor for everything now. Read for five minutes. Tidy for five minutes. Stretch for five minutes. Five minutes sounds silly, and that is the point. A habit you can do when you are tired is a habit you can do every day, and every day is what makes it stick. If two sizes of the same habit are fighting in your head, a Yes or No Wheel can settle whether today counts as a bad day, but do not let it settle whether you do the habit at all. The floor is the floor.

Tie it to something you already do

A new habit floats around loose until you anchor it to an old one. “Walk more” has no time attached to it, so it slides to the end of every day and then off the day entirely. “Walk after dinner” has a time, a trigger, and a pair of shoes by the door.

Pick the anchor before January starts. Walk after dinner. Read in bed before the lights go out. Save money on payday, the same day the check lands, before the money finds other plans. Call your parents on Sunday afternoon, when the house is quiet. The old habit does the reminding, so willpower does not have to.

Cooking works the same way. If your wheel lands on cook at home, do not also decide what to cook every night, or you have added a second new habit on top of the first. On nights when the fridge stares back at you, spin the What to Eat Wheel and cook whatever it lands on. One decision, already made, and dinner is on the stove in ten minutes.

Check in once a week, not every night

Daily tracking sounds disciplined and feels like homework by day nine. A calendar with thirty empty boxes stares at you, and one missed day makes the whole month look broken. I stopped tracking daily years ago, and my habits got better, not worse.

Check in once a week, same day, same two minutes. Sunday night works for most people. Ask two questions: how many days did I do it this week, and what got in the way on the days I did not? Write the answers in one line. “Walked 5 of 7, missed Thursday, worked late.” That line tells you everything. If you missed two days because of work, the habit needs a different time, not more willpower. If you missed two days because you forgot, the anchor needs moving.

Do not grade the week. Five out of seven is a good week. Three out of seven is a week that taught you something. Zero out of seven is a signal to shrink the habit or change the anchor, not a reason to quit. The weekly check is a repair shop, not a report card.

The miss rule: spin again, don’t quit

Everyone misses. The difference between people who keep a habit and people who drop it is not that the keepers never miss. It is that the keepers have a rule for what happens after a miss, decided in advance, when they are not annoyed with themselves.

My rule is simple: miss one day, do it the next day, no comment. Miss a whole week, spin the wheel again for a fresh start, same habit, new anchor if the old one failed. The re-spin is a reset button you are allowed to press. Pressing it beats quietly quitting and pretending the resolution never happened.

Write the rule down before January. Say it out loud to someone. “If I miss a week, I spin again, I do not quit.” When February comes and the enthusiasm is gone, which it will be, the rule does the thinking for you. You do not have to feel motivated. You just have to follow the rule you wrote when you were motivated, and that is a much lower bar.

Hand the wheel to someone else

The last trick is the one I resisted longest, because it felt like asking for supervision. Tell one person what your habit is, and let them ask about it once a week. Not daily. Not with a spreadsheet. One text on Sunday: did you walk this week?

It works because a habit you keep private is easy to drop privately. Nobody knows, nobody asks, and by March you have forgotten the wheel entirely. A habit one other person knows about has a witness, and most of us will do an embarrassing amount of work to avoid telling a friend we quit.

Pick the right person. A spouse works, a friend works, a coworker works if the habit is a work habit. Do not pick someone who will lecture you. Pick someone who will just ask, accept the answer, and move on. And if they want a habit of their own, spin together. Two wheels, two habits, one Sunday text each way. That is a fair trade, and it is a lot harder to quit when someone else is counting on the text too.

Try the resolution wheel for January

Load six honest options onto the Decision Wheel, shrink each one until it fits a bad day, tie it to something you already do, and write down your miss rule before January starts. Spin once, in front of someone if you can, and give that one habit a full month.

My walk started as ten minutes around the block last January. By spring it was thirty minutes most nights, and by summer it was the part of the day I looked forward to, which is not something I ever expected to say about exercise. It started with one spin and one small habit, and it stayed because it was small enough to keep. Your one habit can do the same. Spin, pick one, and keep it.

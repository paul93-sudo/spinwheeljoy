Holiday Card List Wheel: Decide Who Gets a Christmas Card

A Christmas card list grows one name at a time until it costs a fortune. Sort the list into yes, maybe, and not this year, then let a wheel settle the maybe pile.

It was the first Saturday in December when I spread the cards out on the kitchen table and counted. Twenty-eight names. Not people I disliked, not people I had forgotten. Twenty-eight people I genuinely wanted to write to, and the math stopped me cold: cards, stamps, and the two evenings it would take to write anything warmer than a signature.

Last year I had sent eleven. The year before that, nine. Somewhere between then and now, the list had tripled without anyone deciding it should. A neighbor started sending us a card. A coworker moved to my team. My daughter’s teacher changed, and the new teacher got a card because the old one had. Each addition made sense on its own. Together, they added up to a project I dreaded.

I’ll be honest, the dread was not about money alone, though forty dollars in stamps is real money. It was about the guilt. Cutting a name felt like cutting a person. Keeping every name felt like signing up for a chore. What I needed was a way to cut the list without cutting people in anger, and without pretending the list could grow forever.

A wheel does that job better than willpower. Load the maybe names into the Decision Wheel, spin, and let chance make the calls you keep putting off. The rest of this post is about how to sort the list first, because a wheel can only settle a list you have already looked at honestly.

Count the list before you buy a single card

Do not buy cards first. Counting first changes everything, because a stack of pretty cards on the table makes every name feel mandatory.

Write every name down. Not from memory, from evidence: last year’s sent pile, the cards you got this year, your phone contacts, the class list, the office directory. My count came out at twenty-eight. Yours might be forty, or fifteen. The number itself is the point. Until you see it, the list is a feeling. Once you see it, it is a plan.

Then write two numbers next to the total. How many cards can you honestly write in one sitting without rushing? For most people, that is ten or twelve. And what can you spend on cards and stamps without wincing? That pair of numbers is your real limit. Everything after this section is about fitting the list inside it.

Sort into yes, maybe, and not this year

Three piles. No fourth pile, no “ask me later” pile. A name goes in one pile and stays there until the wheel says otherwise.

Yes means you would miss sending it. Your parents, your sister, the friend who called when your dad was in the hospital, the neighbor who brought soup when you were sick. These names do not need a spin. They need a pen.

Not this year means the card stopped being a real connection a while ago. The college friend you have not spoken to since 2019, the former coworker whose card you answer out of duty, the distant cousin whose name you only remember in December. Not this year is not never. It is a pause, and a pause is allowed.

Maybe is everyone left. The new coworker you like but barely know. The teacher from two years ago. The neighbor from the old street. The maybe pile is where the guilt lives, and it is also where the wheel earns its keep.

Spin the maybe pile, one card at a time

Here is where the wheel comes in. Put the maybe names on the wheel, and spin for each open slot you have left after the yes pile. If you have room for six more cards, spin six times, and take each name off the wheel as it lands. No re-spins because you do not like the result. That is the whole deal.

This works because it moves the decision out of your head. When I spun my maybe pile, the wheel picked my old neighbor Mrs. Alvarez and my cousin Dan, and it passed over two names I had been carrying out of pure habit. I felt relief, not guilt, because a spin I agreed to in advance is easier to accept than a cut I make while tired at midnight.

One rule keeps it honest: decide the number of open slots before the first spin, and write it down. Six slots, six spins. If you add slots mid-spin because a favorite name has not come up, you are not using a wheel. You are stalling.

Set a budget cap and let the wheel enforce it

Cards cost more than they used to, and stamps add up fast. A box of twenty cards plus stamps can pass fifty dollars before you write a word. A cap turns that from a surprise into a plan.

Pick the cap in dollars, then work backward. If the cap is forty dollars and a card plus stamp runs about two dollars, the list holds twenty names. Count the yes pile. If it holds fourteen, the maybe pile gets six spins. If the yes pile already holds twenty-two, the wheel cannot fix that, and the honest move is a smaller yes pile or a bigger cap. Say which one out loud before you spin.

The cap also settles the fancy card question. A twelve-dollar box of eight fancy cards looks lovely in the store and eats a third of a forty-dollar budget. The wheel does not care what the card looks like. The people on the yes list do not either. They care that you wrote more than your name.

The cards you get do not all need an answer

This is the trap that grew my list from eleven to twenty-eight. Someone sends you a card, and you feel you owe one back. So you add them. Next year they send one because you sent one. Nobody decided this. The mail did.

Break the loop with a simple test: if this person stopped sending cards tomorrow, would you call them? If the answer is yes, they belong on the list on their own merits, and the card they sent is beside the point. If the answer is no, their card was kind, and you can enjoy it without adding a permanent line to your December.

A short note in January works for the rest. “Loved your card, hope the new year treats you well.” That is a real reply. It costs a text, not a place on a list you dread.

One spin per group keeps it fair

Card lists go wrong group by group. You send to three coworkers and skip the fourth, and the fourth notices. You send to one teacher and not the aide who also helped your kid. Groups are where hurt feelings hide.

Spin per group instead of across the whole list. Put the four coworkers on the wheel and spin for the two slots you have. Put the neighbors on their own wheel and spin for three. Each group gets its own fair draw, and nobody can say the office got cards while the neighbors got nothing.

Teachers deserve their own spin, too. My daughter had a teacher, an aide, and a reading tutor in one year. Three cards felt right. The next year she had one teacher and a part-time aide, and two cards felt right. The wheel handles the change without a family debate, because the group size sets the slots, not last year’s list.

Keep the no list private and the yes list honest

Two habits make the list work next year. First, keep the no list to yourself. Nobody needs to hear they were cut, and nobody benefits from a speech about it. A quiet pause is kinder than an explained one.

Second, write the yes list by hand, even if the handwriting is bad. A printed label on a card you signed tells the person exactly how much thought went into it. Two lines in your own handwriting tell them something else. “Thinking of you and the snowstorm drive to your place last winter” beats a printed greeting every time, and it takes thirty seconds.

Store the sorted list with the leftover cards. Next December, you start from a list you already trust, not from a blank page and a fresh wave of guilt. The yes pile stays. The maybe pile gets a fresh spin. The no pile stays private, and most of it will stay no, which is fine.

Try the holiday card list wheel

Start tonight, before the cards go on sale and the list starts feeling urgent. Count the names. Sort them into yes, maybe, and not this year. Then load the maybe pile into the Decision Wheel and spin for the slots you have left.

If the list is still too long after that, two tools help with the edges. The Yes or No Wheel settles the single-name arguments, the ones where you keep going back and forth about one person. And if the real problem is picking who to write to first, the Random Name Picker can set the writing order so the yes pile gets done before the week runs out.

The point was never to send fewer cards out of stinginess. It was to send cards you mean, to people you would miss, without turning December into a guilt project. Spin the maybe pile, write the yes pile by hand, and put the pen down when the list is done.

Ski Day Pack Wheel: What Goes in the Bag

Last February we left the house at 6:40am for a ski day and got halfway down the road before my daughter asked where her gloves were. A wheel would have settled the layers, the gear, and the snacks the night before, before anyone was in a coat.

Last February we left the house at 6:40am for a ski day and got halfway down the road before my daughter asked where her gloves were. Her gloves were on the kitchen counter, next to the sunscreen we had also forgotten, next to a lunch bag that held exactly one granola bar for four people. We turned around. We lost the early parking lot, we lost the first hour on the snow, and we spent the rest of the drive listing everything else we might have left behind.

Here’s the thing. Packing for a ski day goes wrong for the same reason dinner plans go wrong. Six small decisions hide inside it, and each one gets made by a different person at a different time, usually while someone is already putting on a coat. Layers, gear, who carries what, food, the car, and the small stuff. Put those six decisions on the Decision Wheel the night before, spin them in order, and the bag gets packed while everyone is still calm.

I’ll be honest, the wheel does not pack the bag for you. It just stops the part where three people stand in the hallway and ask each other what they think they need. That part took us forty minutes last February. The wheel takes about ten.

Spin for the layers first

Layers go first because everything else fits around them. A ski jacket takes up half a bag on its own, and if you pack the jacket before you pack the base layer, you end up sitting on the bag to close it. So spin for layers first, and pack in the order the wheel gives you. Our wheel holds four layer picks: base, mid, shell, and socks. Four options. The wheel picks one, you pack it, and you spin again until all four are in the bag.

The shell is the layer people skip when the morning looks sunny. Don’t. A sunny morning at the base can turn into wind and wet snow by lunch, and a shell weighs almost nothing in the bag. Last year my son left his shell in the car because the sky was blue at 8am. By 11am he was wearing a borrowed windbreaker two sizes too big, and he spent the afternoon with wet sleeves. The shell goes in the bag even when the forecast looks friendly. That is a rule in our house now, and the wheel enforces it better than I do. When the wheel lands on shell, the shell goes in. Nobody argues with the wheel at 8pm the night before.

One rule that saves space: each person gets one base layer, one mid layer, one shell, and two pairs of socks. Two pairs because socks get wet, and wet socks end a ski day faster than cold air does. The second pair lives in a zip bag at the bottom of the pack, not loose in the car. Loose socks in a car disappear. Socks in a zip bag stay socks.

Spin for the gear check

Gear is the check that has to happen at home, not at the resort. Skis, boots, helmet, and goggles all look fine in the garage, and all four can fail in ways you only notice on the snow. So we spin for the gear check the night before, and each spin means one item gets checked by hand. Helmet first, because a helmet with a cracked strap or a missing clip is a helmet you cannot use, and you will not find that out until you try to put it on in the parking lot.

Our wheel holds four gear picks: helmet, boots, skis, and goggles. When the wheel lands on boots, someone actually puts the boots on and walks across the kitchen. That sounds silly. It catches the boot that will not buckle, the liner that slipped, and the boot that fits a foot that grew two sizes since last winter. My daughter’s boots failed that kitchen test twice in three years. Both times we found out at home, and both times we had time to fix it.

Goggles get the same treatment. Put them on, look at a light, and check for a scratch right in the middle of the view. A scratch at the edge is fine. A scratch in the middle will bother you all day, and you will think about it on every run. If the goggles fail, they go on the shopping list that night, not on the morning list when the shops are still closed.

Spin for who packs what

One person packing for four people is how things get forgotten. The packer remembers their own stuff and guesses at everyone else’s. So we spin for who packs what, and each person packs their own bag. Our wheel holds four names: Mia, Mom, Dad, and Sam. When the wheel lands on Mia, Mia packs her bag, and nobody packs it for her. That includes her gloves. Especially her gloves.

This works better than a list taped to the fridge, because a list still leaves one person in charge of checking the list. A wheel hands the job to the person whose name lands. Mia packs her layers and her gear. Dad packs the car bag. Mom packs the food. Sam packs the extras. If a bag is missing something, the person who packed it knows, because they packed it. There is no argument about who was supposed to remember the sunscreen. The wheel said Sam, and Sam will remember next time.

If you have a bigger group, put the names on the Random Name Picker and keep the packing jobs on the decision wheel. Two wheels sounds like a lot of setup for a bag. It takes about two minutes to set up both, and it settles a question that otherwise gets re-argued in the hallway every single trip.

Spin for the snacks and lunch

Lunch at a ski resort costs more than the gas to get there, and the line at noon is long enough to eat half your ski time. So we pack lunch, and we spin for it the same way we spin for layers. Our wheel holds four lunch picks: sandwich, cocoa, apples, and bars. When the wheel lands on cocoa, someone fills the thermos the night before and puts it in the fridge door, not on the counter where it gets left behind.

Cocoa is the pick that surprises people. A thermos of cocoa is the warm drink that gets a cold kid back on the snow after lunch. A thermos of cocoa at 2pm, shared on a bench, does more for morale than a second sandwich does. We learned that the hard way, on a day when we packed plenty of food and nothing warm to drink, and everyone was done by 1:30pm even though the snow was good.

One rule that keeps lunch simple: each person picks one sandwich filling, and the wheel picks the shared extras. If the wheel lands on apples, apples go in. If it lands on bars, bars go in. Nobody packs a second lunch just in case, because a second lunch is the bag that stays in the car and gets eaten on the drive home, when everyone is too tired to care.

Spin for the drive up

The drive up is its own packing job, and it gets forgotten because it is not snow gear. Chains, a phone charger, water, and a paper map all live in the car bag, and the car bag lives by the front door, not in the garage. We spin for the drive up after the bags are packed, and each spin means one car item gets checked. Chains go first in our house, because mountain roads can require them with very little warning, and a chain check at home takes five minutes. A chain check on the side of the road takes much longer, and it takes place in the snow.

Water goes in the car bag too, even though it sounds obvious. People drink less in the cold, and then they wonder why they have a headache at lunch. Two bottles per person in the car, plus one extra for the drive home. The extra bottle is the one that gets used. Every time.

The phone charger is the item that gets argued about most, because everyone assumes someone else packed one. Put the charger on the wheel. When the wheel lands on charger, one charger goes in the car bag, and it stays in the car bag all season. A charger that lives in the car bag does not get borrowed for the bedroom and forgotten there.

Spin for the one thing everyone forgets

Every ski family has one item they forget on every trip. Ours was sunscreen. Sunscreen on snow feels wrong, because it is cold and your face feels fine. Then the sun bounces off the snow all day, and by dinner your cheeks are red and your lips are cracked. So we made a wheel just for the forgettable stuff: sunscreen, lip balm, hand warmers, a phone charger, and dry socks. Five options, one spin per person, and whatever lands goes in a small zip bag that lives in the ski jacket pocket.

Lip balm is the pick that gets laughed at until it gets used. A tube of lip balm weighs nothing, costs very little, and saves a whole afternoon of cracked lips. Hand warmers are the same. One pair per person, in the jacket pocket, not in the car. Hand warmers in the car help nobody on the lift.

Here’s a trick that works with teenagers: let them spin for their own forgettable item, and let them pick the brand. A teenager who spins for lip balm and picks a flavor will actually carry it. A teenager who gets handed a tube will leave it in the car. That small bit of choice does more than any reminder from a parent.

Spin for the end of the day dry bag

The end of the day is when the wet stuff meets the dry stuff, and the dry stuff loses. So we pack a dry bag before we leave, and we spin for what goes in it. Our wheel holds four end-of-day picks: dry socks, a dry shirt, a plastic bag for wet gear, and a towel. When the wheel lands on the plastic bag, a big zip bag goes in the pack, and wet gloves go in the zip bag on the drive home, not loose on the back seat.

Dry socks at the end of the day are the closest thing to a reward that packing offers. You take off wet ski socks in the parking lot, you put on dry socks, and the drive home feels different. It is a small thing. It is also the thing everyone mentions on the way home, every single time. Pack the dry socks. Spin for them first if you have to.

The towel goes in for the same reason. A small towel dries a face, wipes goggles, and cleans a car seat that got snow on it. It takes up almost no space, and it gets used on almost every trip. If you have a Chore Wheel at home, this is the same idea applied to a ski bag: one spin, one job, nobody has to remember the whole list.

Try the ski day pack wheel

Packing for a ski day will never be fun. It can be short, though, and it can be done the night before, while everyone is still in regular clothes and nobody is in a hurry. Put the layers, the gear, the names, the lunch, the car items, and the forgettable stuff on the Decision Wheel, spin through them in order, and pack what lands. Ten minutes at the kitchen table beats forty minutes in the hallway, and it beats turning the car around for gloves.

Spin it tonight, before the next ski day. Your future self, the one standing in the parking lot at 8am with everything in the bag, will be glad you did.

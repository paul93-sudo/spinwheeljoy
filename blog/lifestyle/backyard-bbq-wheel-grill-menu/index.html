Backyard BBQ Wheel: Pick the Grill Menu

My neighbor announced a backyard BBQ on a Tuesday and by Thursday the group chat had 63 messages about burgers, ribs, and who was bringing the cooler. I put the menu on a wheel, and the grill got planned in one evening.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 29, 2026 Updated October 29, 2026

My neighbor Dan announced a backyard BBQ on a Tuesday, for that Saturday, and by Thursday the group chat had 63 messages and no menu. His wife wanted ribs. His brother wanted burgers and refused to discuss anything else. One cousin was vegetarian and had stopped replying, which is never a good sign. Someone suggested a potluck, and three people said yes to potluck while meaning three different things by it. The only thing everyone agreed on was that Saturday was the day, which is the one decision Dan had already made on his own.

Here’s the thing. A BBQ menu is not one decision. It is a protein, a side, a sauce, a drink, and a dessert, and each one has someone with a strong opinion and a story about the time it went wrong. That is why the chat goes in circles. So I opened the What to Eat Wheel and put the menu on wheels instead: the protein, the side, the sauce, the drink, the dessert, the grill master, and the start time. One evening, seven spins, and the BBQ had a menu. Nobody got everything they wanted, and nobody had to lose an argument either. The wheel took the blame, which is the whole point.

Spin for the protein

The protein comes first, because it decides the shopping list, the grill time, and how early someone has to start cooking. Ribs need hours and a patient cook. Burgers need thirty minutes and a hot grill. Chicken sits in the middle: forgiving, cheap, and easy to dry out if the cook walks away to argue about the playlist. Our wheel held six: burgers, brats, chicken, ribs, veggie burgers, and fish. The wheel landed on burgers, and burgers were a kind draw for twelve people, because they cook fast, they feed picky eaters, and the vegetarian cousin got veggie burgers from the same grill without a separate menu meeting.

I’ll be honest, I wanted ribs. Ribs feel like a real BBQ, and burgers feel like a Tuesday dinner with extra chairs. But a protein picked by a spin ends the protein debate, and an ended debate is worth more than a slightly fancier main dish. Once the protein was set, Dan could shop on Friday instead of texting the group about it until Saturday morning, and the cousin came back to the chat, which mattered more than the ribs ever would.

One rule keeps this spin fair: only put proteins on the wheel that the cook can actually grill. A protein nobody knows how to cook does not belong on the wheel, even if it sounds impressive. A wheel full of real options gives a real answer. A wheel full of options people will veto after the spin just starts the argument again, with extra steps.

Spin for the side

The side spin decides whether the table looks like a meal or a pile of meat with a bag of chips next to it. A good side does three jobs: it fills plates cheaply, it gives vegetarians something real to eat, and it holds up outside for two hours without turning sad. Our wheel held six: corn, slaw, beans, salad, chips, and potato salad. The wheel landed on corn, and corn was the right call for a backyard, because Dan could grill it on the same grill after the burgers came off, and butter and salt are the whole recipe.

Corn also fixed the budget without a fight. Twelve ears cost less than a deli tray of anything, and nobody argues about corn the way they argue about potato salad, which always has one person who says the store kind is wrong and another person who says the homemade kind is wrong. If your wheel lands on a side that needs the oven, check the oven first. Dan’s oven was already holding a tray of buns, and a side that needs oven space you do not have is a side that arrives late and cold.

A note from a BBQ I got wrong: spin for the side before you shop, not at the store. We once stood in the grocery aisle for twenty minutes debating slaw versus beans while the ice melted in the cart. The wheel takes ten seconds. The grocery aisle debate takes twenty minutes and still ends with someone saying “whatever is fine” and then complaining about it later.

Spin for the sauce

Sauce is the spin everyone thinks is small until the burgers come off the grill and there is nothing to put on them. A BBQ without sauce is a dry burger and a quiet table. A BBQ with five sauces is a condiment meeting. Our wheel held six: BBQ sauce, ketchup, mustard, hot sauce, mayo, and chimichurri. The wheel landed on BBQ sauce, and BBQ sauce was the easy winner for burgers, because it works on the bun, on the side, and on the one veggie burger that always needs a little help.

The rule for the sauce spin is to buy two bottles of whatever wins, not one. One bottle feeds six people. Twelve people and one bottle means the last four burgers go dry, and the last four people notice. Two bottles cost a few dollars and end the problem before it starts. If the wheel lands on a sauce half the guests hate, put the runner-up on the table too. One backup sauce is fair. Five backup sauces is just a condiment meeting with extra steps.

The Decision Wheel can settle the smaller sauce calls, like mild or spicy, once the main sauce is set. Put the two heat levels on it and spin once, instead of asking the group chat, which will give you nine answers and one person who says hot sauce is a personality trait.

Spin for the drink

The drink spin decides the cooler, and the cooler decides how many trips someone makes to the store during the party. A drink plan is one simple choice: what goes in the cooler, and how much of it. Our wheel held six: lemonade, iced tea, soda, water, punch, and sparkling water. The wheel landed on lemonade, and lemonade was the right call for a hot Saturday, because Dan could make a big batch on Friday night, and kids and adults drink the same thing, which means one cooler instead of three.

Two rules make the drink spin work. First, buy ice the morning of the party, not the night before. Ice bought Friday night melts in a warm garage by Saturday noon, and a cooler full of warm lemonade is a sad sight. Second, put water in the cooler no matter what the wheel says. A cooler with only lemonade and no water means someone ends up drinking from the garden hose, and nobody wants that story told at the next BBQ.

One more cooler note: label the cooler. A cooler that says “drinks” gets opened by everyone. A cooler that says “drinks, help yourself” gets opened by everyone and restocked by nobody. Put a second cooler by the grill for the cook, with water and one cold drink in it, because the person standing over a hot grill for an hour should not have to walk to the house for a sip.

Spin for the dessert

Dessert is the spin people skip, because by the time the burgers are planned everyone is tired of deciding. That is exactly why it belongs on a wheel. If you ask the group, you get nine dessert ideas and no dessert. If you spin, you get one dessert, and one person buys it on the way over. Our wheel held six: pie, cookies, ice cream, cake, fruit, and brownies. The wheel landed on ice cream, and ice cream was the best possible draw for a hot backyard, because Dan’s freezer did the work and the kids served themselves with a scoop each.

The dessert rule is simple: pick one dessert that needs no plates, no cutting, and no serving. Ice cream in cones passes. A sheet cake passes if someone brings a knife and plates, and fails if nobody does, which happens more often than anyone admits. Cookies pass every time, which is why cookies are the backup dessert for every BBQ I have ever planned. If the wheel lands on ice cream and the freezer is small, buy a bag of ice and a cheap foam cooler, and keep the tubs in the cooler until serving time. Melted ice cream is a puddle with good intentions.

The Random Name Picker can pick who brings the dessert, if your group would rather spin names than argue about it. Put the actual names on it, spin once, and text the winner that night. A name picked by a wheel is easier to accept than a name picked by a committee, because a committee feels like a judgment and a wheel feels like luck.

Spin for the grill master

The grill master spin is the one people avoid, because standing over a hot grill for an hour is work, and nobody wants to volunteer for work at a party. That is exactly why it belongs on a wheel. If you ask for a volunteer, you get silence, then Dan, then Dan grilling for twelve people while everyone else sits in the shade. If you spin, you get a name, and the name gets a shift, not the whole afternoon. Our wheel held six names from the guest list, and it landed on Dan’s brother, the burger purist, which felt fair, because the man who demanded burgers got to cook them.

Two rules make the grill master spin work. First, set the shift before the spin, not after. One hour at the grill is fair. The whole party is not. Second, give the grill master a helper for the first fifteen minutes, to carry plates out and bring the raw burgers to the grill, so the cook is not running back and forth to the kitchen while the first batch burns. A grill master with a helper is a cook. A grill master alone is a short-order kitchen with no roof.

A note from a BBQ I got wrong: spin for the grill master before the party, not at the party. We once spun for the cook after everyone arrived, and the winner had just changed into white shorts and spent the next hour guarding them instead of the grill. A name picked the night before shows up in old clothes. A name picked at the party shows up in whatever they wore to impress people.

Spin for the start time

The start time spin sounds silly until you have lived through a BBQ where half the guests arrive at noon and half at three, and the burgers are gone before the second half gets there. A start time is one answer to one question: when does the food come off the grill. Our wheel held six times: 11:30, 12:00, 12:30, 1:00, 4:00, and 5:00. The wheel landed on 12:30, and 12:30 worked because the morning people had time to get there and the afternoon people did not have to wait three hours for lunch.

The rule I use: tell people the grill time as well as the party time. A party that starts at noon with food at 12:30 is a plan. A party that starts at noon with food “whenever” is a crowd standing around a cold grill at 1:15 wondering where Dan went. Put the grill time in the invitation, in the group chat, and on the cooler, because someone will ask anyway, and the answer should be written down somewhere that is not your phone.

One more timing note worth naming: the cleanup spin, or rather the cleanup deal. Someone always leaves before the trash bags come out. Make the deal before the party: the grill master does not clean, because the grill master cooked. Everyone else takes one job on the way out: one bag of trash, one stack of plates, one cooler to the garage. A BBQ that ends with three people cleaning while nine people wave from their cars is a BBQ people stop coming to. A BBQ that ends with everyone carrying one thing is a BBQ people come back to.

Try the backyard BBQ wheel

A backyard BBQ does not need 63 messages about burgers, ribs, and who brings the cooler. It needs one protein the cook can grill, one side that holds up outside, one sauce with a backup bottle, one drink in a cooler with ice bought that morning, one dessert that needs no plates, one grill master with a one-hour shift, and one grill time written in the invitation. Put burgers, brats, chicken, ribs, veggie burgers, and fish on the What to Eat Wheel and spin for the protein first. Then spin for the side and the drink, text the dessert-bringer that night, and let the grill do the rest before the sun moves across the yard.

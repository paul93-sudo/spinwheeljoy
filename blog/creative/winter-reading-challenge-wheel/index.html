Winter Reading Challenge Wheel: Pick the Cold-Night Challenge

My to-read pile hit eleven books last January and I still spent twenty minutes every night picking nothing. A wheel that assigns one genre, one page target, one buddy read, and one wild card gave the pile a shape, and the shape got me reading again.

Last January my to-read pile hit eleven books, and I still spent twenty minutes most nights picking nothing. I would stand in front of the shelf, pull out a novel, read the first page, put it back, and then watch a show I had already seen. The pile was not the problem. The pile was fine. The nightly pick was the problem, because at 9pm, tired, with a cold wind rattling the window, every book looked like homework.

Here’s the thing. I did not need a reading list. I had three reading lists, one on paper, one in my phone, one in my head, and all three disagreed with each other. I needed a way to pick one book and start it before my brain could talk me out of it. So I built a winter reading challenge out of spins. One spin for genre, one for a page target, one for a buddy read, one for a reread, and one for a wild card. Five spins, one cold season, and the pile finally started shrinking.

Put the challenge on the Book Picker and spin in order: genre first, page target second, buddy third, reread fourth, wild card last. The order matters, and I will explain why in each section below.

Spin for the genre you never touch

Genre goes first because it narrows the shelf. If you spin for a page target first, you still have eleven books to choose from, and you are back at the shelf at 9pm doing the same dance. Spin genre first and ten books leave the running at once. Put six genres on the wheel, and make at least three of them genres you never touch. Ours were mystery, sci-fi, memoir, poetry, history, and fantasy. Fantasy was the safety net. I read fantasy the way some people eat toast: often, without thinking, and with no complaints. Poetry was the stretch.

When our wheel landed on mystery, I did something I never do. I picked the mystery on top of the pile instead of the third fantasy down. It took me four nights to finish, and I stayed up too late on the third night because the last forty pages would not let go. That had not happened since the summer, when reading was easy and nights were long. The lesson was not that mystery is magic. The lesson was that one unfamiliar genre per challenge is enough. Two unfamiliar genres at once is a syllabus, and nobody signed up for a syllabus in January.

One rule that keeps this honest: if the genre spin lands on a genre with zero books on your shelf, you get one re-spin. A challenge that sends you to a bookstore in a snowstorm is not a challenge. It is an errand, and errands kill reading streaks faster than a boring chapter does.

Spin for the page-count target

Page target goes second, and it goes second because a target turns a vague plan into a finish line. “Read more this winter” means nothing at 9pm. “Read 200 pages of this book by Sunday” means you open the book. Put six numbers on the wheel: 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500. Six is enough. A wheel with twenty numbers is a calculator, and nobody needs a calculator to pick a reading target.

Our wheel landed on 200, and I will be honest, 200 sounded small until I did the math. Two hundred pages across a week is about thirty pages a night, which is one chapter and a bit of the next one, which is exactly the amount I can read before my eyes start skipping lines. A 500-page target sounds brave on a Sunday and feels like a punishment by Wednesday. A 50-page target sounds lazy until you remember that fifty pages of a book you actually finish beats two hundred pages of a book you abandon on page forty.

Here is a trick that saved our challenge twice: count pages read, not days read. A streak that breaks because you missed one Tuesday is a streak that makes you quit. A page count forgives a missed night. You read zero on Tuesday, you read sixty on Wednesday, and the count still moves. The wheel gives you the number. The number gives you room.

Spin for the buddy read

Buddy read goes third, because a solo challenge stalls in week three and a buddy read does not. Put the names of people who actually read on the wheel. Ours were Maya, Dan, Priya, and Sam. Four names, four people who answer texts, four people who will tell you the truth about a slow middle chapter instead of pretending they loved it.

When the wheel landed on Maya, I texted her the title and the page target, and she replied with a photo of the same book on her nightstand. She had bought it in November and never opened it. We set one rule: no spoilers past the page we had both reached, and one text each night with the page number. That was it. Two people, one book, one number each night. We finished within two days of each other, and the night she finished first she texted me “page 200, no spoilers, just read faster,” which is the best review I have ever gotten.

If the buddy spin lands on someone who is in the middle of their own challenge, let them pass. A buddy who reads under pressure is a buddy who starts dreading your texts. Pass, re-spin, and put their name back on the next wheel. The challenge is supposed to make winter nights better, not add one more person you owe a reply.

Spin for the reread you keep skipping

Every reader has a book they loved and keep meaning to reread, and every reader keeps skipping it for something new. That is what the reread spin is for. Put four titles on the wheel, all books you have already read, all books you rated highly, all books you have not opened in at least two years. Four is enough. A reread wheel with twelve titles is just your shelf again, and your shelf is what got you into this mess.

Our wheel landed on a novel I first read at twenty-two, and I braced for disappointment. Books from your twenties can shrink when you meet them again. This one did not shrink. It changed. The parts I remembered as funny read as sad this time, and the parts I had forgotten entirely turned out to be the best parts. I read it in six nights, which is faster than I read it the first time, because I already trusted the author and I did not waste nights wondering whether the book would pay off.

One honest warning: a reread counts toward the page target. If it does not count, nobody picks the reread spin, and the reread spin becomes a chore you skip. Count it. A page is a page, and a reread that pulls you back into reading is worth more than a new book that sits on the nightstand for three weeks.

Spin for the wild-card pick

Wild card goes last, and it goes last because it works best when the rest of the challenge is already set. The wild card is one pick outside your habits: a poetry collection, a graphic novel, a book of essays, a play. Put four formats on the wheel. Ours were poetry, graphic, essays, and play. The wild card is not a punishment round. It is the spin that keeps the challenge from turning into a routine, and a routine is what killed your reading in the first place.

When our wheel landed on poetry, I picked a thin collection off the shelf and read it in two nights, a few poems at a time. I read most of them in the kitchen while the kettle boiled. Poetry turned out to be the perfect cold-night format, because a poem fits into the ten minutes between dinner and dishes, and ten minutes is sometimes all a winter night has to offer. I would never have picked it on purpose. That is the whole point of the wild card. It picks the thing you would never pick, and then you find out whether you were right to skip it all those years.

If the wild card lands on a format you truly dislike, swap the format, not the spin. Keep the rule that the wild card must be outside your habits, and pick a different outsider. A poetry hater who swaps poetry for essays still played the round. A poetry hater who swaps poetry for another fantasy novel did not.

Spin for the cold-night ritual

A challenge needs a ritual, or the spins become five more decisions and you are back where you started. The ritual is small on purpose: same chair, same mug, same start time, same phone in another room. Ours was 9pm, kitchen table, tea, phone on the fridge. The fridge part sounds silly, and it works great, because a phone on the fridge is a phone you have to stand up to check, and standing up is usually enough to remind you that you were reading.

Put the ritual on a wheel too, if your household argues about it. One spin for the drink, one for the spot, one for the start time, and the argument ends before it starts. My house argued about start time for a week before I put 8:30, 9:00, and 9:30 on a wheel. The wheel landed on 9:00, and nobody argued again, because arguing with a wheel is like arguing with a coin. You can do it, but the coin does not care, and everyone in the room knows it.

The ritual also protects the page target. Thirty pages a night does not happen in a house where the television is on and the phone is buzzing. Thirty pages a night happens in a chair, with a mug, at a set time, and the wheel is what picked all three, so nobody has to defend them.

Spin for the rule that keeps it from stalling

Every challenge stalls. Ours stalled in week three, when the mystery got slow in the middle and the buddy read hit a chapter neither of us liked. That is normal, and the challenge needs a stall rule before week three arrives, not after. Put three stall rules on the wheel and spin once at the start: one skip, one swap, or one pause week. One spin, one rule, and the rule is written down where everyone can see it.

Our wheel landed on one skip. One skip means any reader can skip one spin result, once, no questions, no guilt. Maya used her skip on the reread, because her reread was a book she had come to dislike, and skipping it saved her challenge. I saved my skip and never used it, which is also fine. A skip you never use is still doing its job, because knowing you can quit a bad pick makes it easier to start a risky one.

Write the stall rule on paper and stick it to the fridge, next to the phone. A rule in your head is a rule you renegotiate at 9pm when you are tired, and tired negotiators always vote for television. A rule on paper is a rule you follow, and following it is what gets you to the last page.

Try the winter reading challenge wheel

Five spins, one season, one pile that finally shrinks. Genre first, page target second, buddy third, reread fourth, wild card last, and a ritual and a stall rule holding the whole thing together. Put your own titles on the Book Picker, spin once tonight, and read thirty pages before bed. If the genre spin lands somewhere strange, good. Strange is where the challenge lives.

Christmas Cookie Decorating Wheel: Pick Tonight's Design

It is a Tuesday night in December, the sugar cookies are baked and cooling on racks, and four people want four different designs. A Christmas cookie decorating wheel settles the shape, the frosting color, the sprinkles, the piping tip, and the wild-card cookie before anyone squeezes a frosting bag.

It is a Tuesday night in December, and the kitchen smells like sugar cookies. Maya baked two trays on Sunday and froze half of them, so tonight there are 24 plain cookies on cooling racks, four frosting bags on the counter, and a box of sprinkles that Tom bought on sale in November. The kids are already at the table with aprons on. One wants a snowman. One wants a tree with so many sprinkles you cannot see the frosting. Tom wants candy canes, because he says a candy cane cookie looks like Christmas and takes two minutes. Maya wants stars, because stars are fast and they look good in the tin she plans to give Grandma.

Nobody is wrong, and 24 cookies decorated four different ways still leaves a table full of arguments about the next batch. Put it on the Color Wheel instead. One spin for the shape. One for the frosting color. One for the sprinkles. One for the piping tip. One for who decorates what. One for the wild-card cookie. One for the gift box. Seven spins, and the frosting bags get used with a plan instead of a debate about whose design counts as Christmas.

Spin for the shape

Start with the shape, because the shape decides the cookie cutter, the frosting area, and how long each cookie takes. A star takes a minute. A snowman takes three, because it needs a hat, buttons, and a face, and someone always pipes the face too low.

Load the wheel with star, tree, snowman, candy cane, wreath, and gingerbread man. Star means the five-point star cutter, which is the right pick when the table has little kids, because a star holds a thick line of frosting and a handful of sprinkles without falling apart. Tree means the tree cutter, which suits a table that wants a cookie to look like a tree and not like a green blob, so the frosting goes on in a zigzag and the sprinkles go on as ornaments. Snowman means the snowman cutter, which is the right pick when someone at the table has patience for a face and a scarf. Candy cane means the cane cutter, which suits a fast night, because a cane needs two stripes and it is done. Wreath means the round wreath cutter, which is the right pick when the table wants a cookie that looks fancy with a circle of green frosting and red dots. Gingerbread man means the man cutter, which suits a table that wants a cookie with a face and buttons and a story to tell about it.

Spin once, and that is the shape for the batch. The wheel lands on star, which suits Maya fine and gives the kids a shape they can finish before bedtime. One rule keeps this spin honest: the shape that wins is the shape for all 12 cookies in the batch, not the shape for the first two cookies plus whatever anyone felt like after that. A tin of matching stars looks like a gift. A tin of six random shapes looks like leftovers.

If the shape list is longer than the cutter box and the cutters turn into their own debate, settle who picks the shortlist with the Random Name Picker first. One draw for the six shapes on the wheel, one spin for the batch, and the table has a cutter.

Spin for the frosting color

The shape is picked, and now the frosting color decides what the batch looks like in the tin. A star with red frosting reads as Christmas from across the room. A star with blue frosting reads as a cookie that got lost on the way to a birthday party.

Load the wheel with red, green, white, gold, pink, and blue. Red means the classic red frosting, which is the right pick when the batch goes in a gift tin, because red reads as Christmas next to green ribbon. Green means the tree green frosting, which suits a tree batch, and it also suits a wreath batch, because a wreath needs green to look like a wreath. White means plain white frosting, which is the right pick when the sprinkles do the work, because white shows every sprinkle color. Gold means the gold-tinted frosting, which suits a star batch, because a gold star looks like an ornament. Pink means pink frosting, which is the right pick when the kids asked for pink and the tin goes to a neighbor who likes pink. Blue means the winter blue frosting, which suits a snowflake batch, because blue reads as cold and snowy next to white sprinkles.

Spin once, and that is the color for the batch. The wheel lands on red, which means the stars get red frosting and the kids get to argue about sprinkles instead of colors. One rule helps: one color per batch. Two colors per cookie sounds fun and ends with brown frosting, because red plus green in the same bag turns into mud by the third cookie.

Here is the thing. Frosting color is the spin adults care about most and kids care about least. Kids will eat a blue star and a red star with the same speed. Adults will photograph the red ones. Spin for the color, take the photo, and let the kids eat the blue test cookie that nobody photographed.

Spin for the sprinkles

The frosting is on, and now the sprinkles decide whether the cookie looks finished or looks like a plain cookie that someone got bored with. Sprinkles are also the part where one kid pours half the jar on one cookie and then asks why the jar is empty.

Load the wheel with stars, pearls, crushed candy, gold, rainbow, and none. Stars means small star sprinkles, which are the right pick when the frosting is red or white, because star sprinkles show up on both. Pearls means white pearl sprinkles, which suit a fancy tin, because pearls look like tiny ornaments and they do not bleed color into the frosting. Crushed candy means crushed candy cane pieces, which are the right pick when the batch smells like peppermint already and the cookies go with hot chocolate. Gold means gold sugar crystals, which suit a gold star batch, because gold on gold looks like an ornament and not like a mistake. Rainbow means the mixed rainbow sprinkles, which are the right pick when the kids are decorating, because kids like rainbow sprinkles and they do not care about matching the tin. None means no sprinkles at all, which suits a batch with piped details, because sprinkles cover up piping and the piping took ten minutes per cookie.

Spin once, and that is the sprinkle for the batch. The wheel lands on stars, which means each red star gets five small star sprinkles, one near each point, and the jar stays half full for the next batch. One rule helps: count the sprinkles per cookie before the first cookie. Five per cookie means 60 sprinkles for 12 cookies, and the jar has more than that. No count means the first cookie gets 40 sprinkles and the last cookie gets crumbs.

I’ll be honest, the sprinkles spin is the one that saves the most jars. Without it, the first kid decorates one cookie like a disco ball and the second kid gets a plain cookie and a lecture about sharing. With it, both kids get the same five stars, and the jar survives until the next batch.

Spin for the piping tip

The shape, the color, and the sprinkles are picked, and now the piping tip decides whether the frosting goes on as a line, a dot, or a blob that slides off the cookie. A tip argument sounds small until someone pipes a whole batch with a tip that makes a line as wide as the cookie.

Load the wheel with round tip, star tip, flat tip, writing tip, leaf tip, and squeeze bottle. Round tip means the plain round opening, which is the right pick when the batch needs outlines and dots, because a round tip makes a clean line. Star tip means the star-shaped opening, which suits a tree batch, because a star tip makes frosting look like branches with one squeeze. Flat tip means the flat ribbon opening, which is the right pick when the batch needs a wide stripe, because a flat tip covers a candy cane in two passes. Writing tip means the tiny writing opening, which suits names and dates on a gift cookie, because a writing tip makes small letters that stay readable. Leaf tip means the leaf-shaped opening, which is the right pick when the wreath batch needs leaves, because a leaf tip makes a leaf shape with one squeeze. Squeeze bottle means the plastic bottle with a thin tip, which suits little kids, because a bottle is easier to hold than a bag and it does not burst when a kid squeezes too hard.

Spin once, and that is the tip for the batch. The wheel lands on round tip, which means the stars get a red outline and a dot in the middle, and the kids learn one tip instead of switching tips every two cookies. One rule helps: one tip per batch, and the tip stays on the bag until the batch is done. A tip change mid-batch means frosting on hands, frosting on the table, and a bag that needs refilling.

Spin for who decorates what

The design is picked, and now the people part starts. Four people at a table with 12 cookies and one design still find a way to argue, because one person decorates fast, one decorates slow, and one wants to redo every cookie that looks less than perfect.

Load the wheel with names, plus one slot that says host picks. Maya decorates when she has a plan and a tin to fill. Tom decorates when someone hands him a bag and tells him the design. The older kid decorates when the design is simple and the sprinkles are counted. The younger kid decorates when an adult sits next to them and the cookie is a star. Host picks means Maya assigns the cookies, which is the right pick when the table has a guest who does not want to choose.

Spin once per cookie, or once per pair of cookies if the table is big. The wheel lands on Tom for the first pair, which means Tom pipes two red stars while Maya fills the next bag. One rule helps: a finished cookie stays finished. Nobody redoes a cookie that someone else already decorated, even if the star sprinkles are not straight. A table rule like that keeps the night short and the cookies in the tin.

If the name list turns into a debate about who decorated last time, settle the order with the Decision Wheel first. One spin for the order, and the table has a rotation instead of a memory contest about last December.

Spin for the wild-card cookie

Every decorating night needs one cookie that breaks the rules, because a tin of 12 matching stars is a gift, and a tin of 11 matching stars plus one wild cookie is a story. The wild-card spin picks one cookie per batch that gets a design nobody voted for.

Load the wheel with ugly sweater, reindeer, snow globe, ornament, kid picks, and freehand. Ugly sweater means a cookie shaped like a sweater with clashing colors, which is the right pick when the table wants a cookie that makes people laugh. Reindeer means a reindeer face with pretzel antlers, which suits a batch that already has a star theme, because a reindeer breaks the theme in a good way. Snow globe means a round cookie with a scene inside a white circle, which is the right pick when someone at the table has patience for a tiny scene. Ornament means a round cookie with a hook loop and stripes, which suits a tin that goes on a tree, because an ornament cookie hangs next to real ornaments. Kid picks means the younger kid chooses the design with no vote, which is the right pick when the kid sat through five spins and followed every rule. Freehand means no plan at all, which suits the last cookie in the batch, because the frosting bags are almost empty and the night is almost over.

Spin once, and that is the wild cookie. The wheel lands on kid picks, which means the younger kid draws a star with a face on it, and that cookie goes on top of the tin where everyone sees it first. One rule helps: one wild cookie per batch. Two wild cookies means the batch has no theme, and six wild cookies means the wheel spins were a waste of time.

Spin for the gift box

The cookies are decorated, and now the gift box spin decides who gets which cookies, because a tin of stars for Grandma and a tin of stars for the neighbor still causes a debate when one tin has the wild cookie and the other tin does not.

Load the wheel with Grandma, neighbor, teacher, coworker, host keeps, and freeze for later. Grandma means the tin that goes to Grandma, which is the right pick when Grandma baked with the family last year and the tin is a thank-you. Neighbor means the tin for the neighbor who brought soup in November, which suits a batch with a wild cookie, because neighbors like the wild cookie. Teacher means the tin for the teacher, which is the right pick when the class party is this week and the tin needs to arrive before Friday. Coworker means the tin for the office, which suits a batch with matching stars, because an office tin gets eaten fast and nobody photographs it. Host keeps means the tin stays home, which is the right pick when the family wants cookies for their own tree-trimming night. Freeze for later means the tin goes in the freezer, which suits a batch baked early in December, because frozen cookies thaw in an hour and taste fine on Christmas Eve.

Spin once per tin, and that is where the tin goes. The wheel lands on Grandma for the tin with the wild cookie on top, which suits the family fine, because Grandma will laugh at the star with a face and keep it until last. One rule helps: label the tin before the spin. A tin labeled Grandma with a ribbon on it does not get mixed up with the neighbor tin after the spins.

Try the Christmas cookie decorating wheel

Tonight the table has 24 cookies, four frosting bags, and one box of sprinkles. The wheel picks the shape, the color, the sprinkles, the tip, the decorator, the wild cookie, and the tin. The family still decorates every cookie by hand, and the cookies still look homemade, because they are. The wheel just stops the part where four people debate the design until the frosting hardens in the bag.

Try it on the Color Wheel before the next batch. Load the six shapes, spin for the batch, and write the result on a sticky note next to the cooling racks. Sounds silly, works great. The cookies get decorated, the tin gets filled, and the kitchen gets cleaned before bedtime, which is more than most December nights manage.

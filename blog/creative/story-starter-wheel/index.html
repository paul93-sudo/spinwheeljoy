Story Starter Wheel: Beat Writer's Block With Random Prompts

November, 11:47pm, day twelve of NaNoWriMo, 8,797 words behind, and a blank page. Then a spin gives me a washed-up magician, a motel ice machine, and one very bad decision.

It’s 11:47pm on a Tuesday. My NaNoWriMo dashboard says 14,203 words, which is 8,797 short of where it should be, and the cursor has been blinking at me for forty minutes. I have written and deleted the sentence “The rain was…” three times.

I open the Decision Wheel, type eight random words into it, and spin. “Dishwasher repairman. Underwater casino. Steals the wrong suitcase.”

Okay. Now I’m writing.

The 15-minute flash-fiction sprint

A blank page gets worse the longer you stare at it. So don’t stare. Set a timer for fifteen minutes, load a wheel with first lines, and spin. Write whatever lands as your first sentence, then keep going until the timer goes off.

The lines can be yours or borrowed from anywhere: “The last payphone in Ohio started ringing at 3am.” “Marta hid the ring in the birthday cake.” “Nobody remembered hiring the clown.” Load eight or ten. The weaker ones are fine, because weak first lines still do the one job that matters: they end the staring.

I did this in November with a friend over a video call, both of us with timers, both spinning at the same time. We traded prompts after. Mine was “The parade was supposed to be a surprise.” His was something about a parking garage. We both hit 400 words in fifteen minutes, which was 400 more than I’d written the night before.

Stop mid-sentence when the timer goes off. That’s allowed. A story that stops mid-sentence is still a story. A blank page is not.

Character plus one fatal flaw

The fastest way into a story is a person with a problem they made themselves. So I keep two wheels: one of names, one of flaws.

The names wheel is first names only, stranger the better. Maya. Gus. Priya. Old Man Hendricks. Zoya. Spin. Then the flaw wheel: “lies about small things,” “can’t say no to a dare,” “quotes her grandmother in every argument,” “steals office snacks and feels nothing.”

Maya plus “lies about small things.” Gus plus “can’t say no to a dare.” That’s a story engine in two spins. A character with no flaw is a biography. A character with a flaw is a plot waiting to happen.

If the name the wheel gives you feels wrong, don’t spin again. Ask yourself why it feels wrong. Usually your brain already had a name picked, which means you aren’t stuck anymore. The wheel did its job either way.

Still stuck on the name itself? The baby name picker is built for babies, but a wheel of first names is a wheel of first names. Steal from it.

Setting mashups that don’t belong together

Take two settings with no business being in the same story and bolt them together. One wheel: “moon colony,” “overnight laundromat,” “wedding at a vineyard,” “strip-mall dentist office.” Spin it twice.

Moon colony laundromat. The vending machines are older than the colony. A wedding where the strip-mall dentist is the best man. The fun part of a mashup is how ordinary behavior survives the move: a waitress at a moon diner still calls you “hon,” and the coffee machine still breaks. Familiar people in an unfamiliar place. That tension carries whole novels, and the wheel hands it to you in three seconds.

I once spun “grandma’s attic” and “drive-through car wash” and wrote 1,200 words before I remembered I had other things to do that afternoon. Sometimes the dumbest combination wins.

The stuck-in-chapter-three rescue

Every long draft dies the same way. Chapter three, the scene that was supposed to connect A to B, and nothing moves. The instinct is to outline harder. The wheel is faster.

Keep a disruption wheel for exactly this: “a character gets a phone call they refuse to answer,” “someone leaves, no explanation,” “the power goes out,” “the landlord knocks at 11pm,” “an old receipt in a coat pocket changes the math.”

Spin once, then apply whatever lands to the next scene. It doesn’t have to be elegant. A stuck scene needs energy more than logic. My chapter three got “the landlord knocks at 11pm.” I was annoyed at the wheel for it. Then I wrote 900 words in one sitting, because a protagonist dealing with someone at the door is easier to write than a protagonist rearranging backstory.

And when the spin hands you something you hate, keep the yes-or-no wheel open in the next tab. Ask it whether you have to commit to the hated prompt anyway. If it says yes, no takes-backsies. Half my best pages came from prompts I resented for a full minute first.

The bedtime story machine

My six-year-old asked for a story three nights in a row, and by night three my brain was running on fumes. So we made it a game. One wheel for who: “dragon,” “pirate,” “talking sandwich.” One wheel for where: “library,” “school roof,” “grandma’s attic.” She spins.

“The dragon was lost in the library” beat anything I could have invented that week. She corrects me when I get the story wrong, which is the entire point. She is co-writing. My version had the dragon borrowing ten books. Her version had the dragon as the librarian. Her version won, obviously.

Fifteen minutes, one new story a night, and you never repeat the same plot twice. The kid who spins the wheel takes it personally when the story is bad, which means you get instant editorial feedback no writing group could match.

Game night hooks for the tabletop

DMs know the look. It’s 7pm, the session starts at 8, and the tavern scene you prepped got skipped because the party decided to rob the blacksmith instead. You need a hook in six minutes.

Hook wheel: “a stranger pays their bar tab in foreign coins,” “someone’s grandmother shows up asking questions,” “the well water tastes like copper,” “a letter arrives addressed to a dead man,” “the innkeeper is selling the inn, tonight, cheap.”

Spin once and drop it in front of the players. The players will assume you planned it for weeks. I won’t tell them.

The same wheel works for the murder-mystery party, the improv night, the creative writing class that goes quiet when the teacher asks for volunteers. One spun hook, five seconds of reading, and the room starts talking again.

First-line roulette

Some days you just need one good sentence. Keep a wheel of nothing but first lines: some you love, some you’re borrowing, all fair game. Spin until one sticks. Then write the second sentence. That’s the whole section, really.

My wheel has a line I’ve been trying to earn for two years: “We buried the piano on the first day of the drought.” I have no idea what story that belongs to. One of these days a spin is going to land on it and I’ll find out.

The one hard rule

Honest warning. The wheel gives you a start. It does not give you a story. A spun prompt is a dare, and dares don’t write endings.

If you spin ten times looking for the perfect prompt, you’re doing the blank-page stare with extra steps. The tool can’t save you from that. So here’s my rule, and it’s the only one: spin three times. Pick the one that annoys you the most. That’s your prompt.

The annoying one is the one your brain can’t file away. A story that bugs you gets written. A story you like gets saved for later, which is writer for “never.”

Open the Decision Wheel, load your first lines or your names or your settings, and give it one spin. Fifteen minutes, one story, zero blinking cursors. And if it gives you a dishwasher repairman at an underwater casino, say hello to him from me.

Inktober Prompt Wheel: 31 Drawing Ideas for October

October ended and the ink pen is still in the drawer. A prompt wheel turns 31 ink drawings into one spin each morning, with a catch-up rule for the days you miss.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published November 1, 2026 Updated November 1, 2026

On this page

  1. Spin one prompt each morning
  2. Start with prompts that forgive a shaky hand
  3. Draw the room you sit in
  4. Draw outside before the light changes
  5. Draw people in pieces, not portraits
  6. Save the spooky prompts for days you have energy
  7. Missed days get a catch-up spin, not a quit
  8. Try the inktober prompt wheel

Yesterday was October 31. My bottle of black ink sat on the desk all month, and I used it four times. Four drawings. The rest of October, I opened the sketchbook, thought about what to draw, and closed it again. The pen was not the problem. The blank page plus 31 days of choices was the problem.

I’ll be honest. I like ink because it does not let me erase my way out of a bad line. That same quality makes starting hard. One wrong line stays on the page. So I stopped choosing the subject in the morning. I put 31 prompts on the Color Wheel and let one spin pick the day’s drawing. One spin, one prompt, fifteen minutes. The page did not have to be good. It had to be done.

This article gives you those 31 prompts, grouped so the easy ones come first and the harder ones land after your hand has warmed up. You can run them in October next year, or start today, November 1, and run a 31-day ink challenge on your own calendar. The month on the calendar matters less than the run of days. Thirty-one drawings in a row teaches your hand more than four drawings in a month ever will.

Spin one prompt each morning

Load all 31 prompts on the wheel before day one. Do not add prompts as you go. A wheel you edit each morning turns back into a choice, and choice is what stalled you in the first place.

Spin once, in the morning, before you sit down to draw. Write the prompt at the top of the page. Set a timer for fifteen minutes. Draw until the timer rings, then stop. The timer matters more than the prompt. Without it, a mug turns into an hour of shading and a page you hate. With it, a mug is a mug, drawn in fifteen minutes, and tomorrow you spin again.

Keep the tools small. One pen, one bottle of ink or one pen with black ink, one sketchbook. A brush pen counts. A fountain pen counts. A ballpoint pen counts too, if that is what you own. The challenge is the daily drawing, not the supply list. If your pen runs dry on day nine, finish the day with a pencil and spin again on day ten. A missed tool is not a missed day.

One more rule, and it is the only strict one. Draw the prompt you spin. Do not respin because you got a spider and you wanted a mug. The respin habit teaches your hand to avoid the hard prompts, and the hard prompts are the ones that move your drawing forward. Spin once. Draw what lands.

Start with prompts that forgive a shaky hand

The first seven prompts are shapes a rusty hand can finish in fifteen minutes. A mug is a rectangle with a handle. A boot is a long shape with a sole. A key is a circle, a stem, and teeth. None of them need a steady line. A wobble in the outline reads as ink texture, which is a kind way of saying the medium covers for you in week one.

The seven prompts are mug, boot, key, moon, fish, chair, and tree. Draw them in that order if you run the prompts straight through, or spin for one each morning if you prefer the wheel to set the order. Either way, start here. A moon is a circle and a shadow. A fish is an oval, a tail, and an eye. A chair is four legs and a seat, and nobody checks whether your chair would hold a real person.

Here’s the thing. Week one is not about making pages you frame. It is about making seven pages in a row. Seven pages in a row proves to you that the daily habit fits in your morning. After seven, the pen feels less strange in your hand, and the next prompts ask for a little more.

Draw big in week one. Fill half the page with the mug. A small mug in the middle of a big page looks lost, and a lost drawing invites you to keep adding lines to fill the space. A big mug leaves no room for that habit. One object, large, fifteen minutes, stop.

Draw the room you sit in

The next six prompts come from the room around you: lamp, window, plant, books, shoes, and kettle. You do not need to go anywhere for these. You need to look at one corner of your room for fifteen minutes, which is longer than most of us look at anything at home.

A lamp is a good second-week prompt because it has a shape and a shadow in one object. Draw the lamp, then draw the dark shape it throws on the wall. The shadow is half the drawing, and ink loves shadows. Black ink on white paper is already a shadow study. You just have to notice where the dark shapes sit.

A window teaches you to draw a frame inside a frame. The window shape, the panes, and whatever sits outside it. Keep the outside simple. A tree shape through glass, or a roofline. If you try to draw the whole street through the window, the fifteen minutes run out and the page stays half done. One view, simplified, beats a full street you never finish.

Books are a forgiving prompt too. A stack of books is rectangles at angles, and the titles do not need to be real words. A few lines that suggest letters are enough. Shoes are the hardest prompt in this group. Shoes bend, they crease, and they sit at odd angles by the door. Draw one shoe, not a pair. One shoe, large, from the side. The pair can wait for a day when your hand is steadier.

Draw these prompts where the object sits. Do not carry the kettle to a better table. The kettle on your stove, with the stove behind it, is the drawing. Moving objects to pretty spots is another way to spend the fifteen minutes without drawing.

Draw outside before the light changes

The next six prompts take you outside: fence, puddle, leaf, bird, bridge, and bench. Outside drawings have a deadline built in. The light moves, the bird leaves, the puddle dries. That deadline is useful. It stops you from fussing over one line for ten minutes.

A leaf is the safest outside prompt. Pick one up, bring it to a bench, draw it there. A leaf does not move, and its veins give you lines to follow. A bird is the opposite. A bird moves in three seconds, so draw the shape it makes when it perches, not the bird itself in detail. A dark blob with a tail and a beak reads as a bird at fifteen minutes. A half-drawn detailed bird reads as a mess.

A puddle is ink’s friend. A puddle is a dark shape with a light edge, and black ink draws dark shapes well. Draw the puddle shape first, then one reflection inside it. A tree shape, or a roofline, flipped upside down. One reflection. Two reflections turn the puddle into a mirror study, and the timer will ring before you finish.

A bench teaches you to draw something people sit on without drawing the people. Slats, legs, and the ground under it. A fence is lines at an angle, and a bridge is a curve over water or a path. Both are shapes first, details second. If you start with the details, the shape never lands right.

Dress for the outside prompts. A cold hand draws a shaky line, and a shaky line on an outside page is not charming, it is just cold. Gloves with the fingertips cut off work. So does drawing from a car window, if the prompt allows it. A fence seen through a car window still counts as drawn outside.

Draw people in pieces, not portraits

The next six prompts are hands, hat, coat, dog, bike, and stairs. Notice what is missing. No faces. Faces are where ink drawings fall apart for most people, because one eye a little high ruins the whole page. Hands, hats, and coats give you people without the face problem.

Hands are hard, and that is why they are here. Draw your own left hand with your right hand, or the other way around, for fifteen minutes. The hand will look wrong. Every hand drawing looks wrong at first. That holds for people who draw hands well, too. The point is not a correct hand. The point is fifteen minutes of looking at a hand, which teaches you more than fifteen minutes of avoiding hands.

A hat on a hook is a people prompt without a person in it. A coat on a chair is the same. Both tell you someone was there, and both are shapes you can finish. A dog is a moving prompt, like the bird, so draw the dog asleep. An asleep dog holds still, and a sleeping shape with ears and a tail reads as a dog even when the legs are approximate.

A bike is lines and circles, and circles are kind in ink. Two wheels, a frame, a seat. Draw the bike from the side, not from an angle. A side view keeps both wheels as circles. An angle view turns them into ellipses, and ellipses take longer than fifteen minutes to get right. Stairs are the same idea. Draw them from the side, one step at a time, and let the lines repeat. Repetition is restful in ink. It lets your hand settle into a rhythm.

Save the spooky prompts for days you have energy

The last six prompts are lantern, ghost, spider, pumpkin, mask, and candle. These are the fun ones, and fun prompts carry a trap. They tempt you to spend forty minutes on a pumpkin when the rule is fifteen. Save them for the end of the run, when fifteen minutes feels normal and your hand knows how to stop.

A lantern is a box with light lines inside it. Draw the box, then the glow as white space left blank. Ink drawings work by the white you leave. The black you put down matters, but the white does half the work. A ghost is white space with eyes. Draw the shape around it dark, leave the ghost white, and the page reads at once. If you shade the ghost gray, it stops being a ghost and starts being a cloud.

A spider is legs and a body, and the legs matter more than the body. Eight legs, drawn as curves from one center, read as a spider even when the body is a plain oval. A pumpkin is ribs and a stem. Draw the ribs as curves from the stem down, not as straight lines. A mask is a face shape with eye holes, and eye holes are dark shapes, which ink does well.

A candle is the quietest prompt in this group. A candle, a flame shape, and a dark room around it suggested by a few lines. Draw it on a night when you have ten minutes, not fifteen, and let the page stay simple. Simple is not a failure in ink. Simple is often the page you like a month later, when the busy pages look tired.

The Decision Wheel can settle the small calls around this group, like whether the spooky prompts go in a row at the end or get mixed through the month. One spin, one order, no debate. The Random Name Picker can also pick a friend to show each day’s page to, if showing the page helps you keep going.

Missed days get a catch-up spin, not a quit

You will miss a day. Everyone misses a day in a 31-day run. A sick kid, a late shift, a day where the pen is in the other room and the couch wins. The run does not end on a missed day. It ends when a missed day turns into three missed days, and three turn into a sketchbook back in the drawer.

So make a catch-up wheel before day one. Put six options on it: draw two small prompts, draw one big prompt, draw a small prompt, redo a prompt you liked, skip the day with a line through it, and free choice. When you miss a day, spin the catch-up wheel the next morning. Draw what lands. A skipped day with a line through it still counts as a handled day, because you decided it instead of drifting past it.

Draw two small prompts means two five-minute drawings on one page. A key and a leaf, for example, small, side by side. One big prompt means one prompt drawn large. It fills the page, and you give it the full fifteen minutes plus five more. Redo a prompt means picking a page from week one and drawing the same object again. The second version is almost always better, and seeing that jump is a clear reason to keep going.

Do not catch up by drawing three days of prompts in one sitting. Three prompts in one sitting feels like homework, and homework is what kills a daily habit. One catch-up spin, one catch-up drawing, back to the normal spin the next day. The run is 31 prompts, not 31 perfect days in a row.

Try the inktober prompt wheel

Thirty-one prompts, one spin a morning, fifteen minutes a page. Mug, boot, key, moon, fish, chair, tree. Lamp, window, plant, books, shoes, kettle. Fence, puddle, leaf, bird, bridge, bench. Hands, hat, coat, dog, bike, stairs. Lantern, ghost, spider, pumpkin, mask, candle. That is the whole list, and it fits on one wheel.

Load the Color Wheel with those prompts. Spin tomorrow morning. Draw what lands, stop at fifteen minutes, and spin again the day after. If you miss a day, spin the catch-up wheel instead of quitting. On day 31, you will have a sketchbook with 31 ink pages in it, and a hand that starts faster than it did on day one. That is the point of the wheel. It does not pick a good drawing for you. It picks a drawing, so you actually draw.

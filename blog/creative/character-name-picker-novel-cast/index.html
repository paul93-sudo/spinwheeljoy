Character Name Picker: Name Your Novel's Cast in One Sitting

Three of your characters are named some version of Ethan. Load a character name wheel with your shortlists and spin: one sitting, one named cast, zero 'Untitled Fantasy Guy #2' in the manuscript.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 19, 2026 Updated October 19, 2026

On this page

  1. The main character’s name has to carry 300 pages
  2. Background characters deserve names, not leftovers
  3. Villains whose names don’t sound like cartoons
  4. Fantasy and period names have to fit the world
  5. First name plus last name is where good names die
  6. When the name stops fitting, rename the character
  7. The whole cast, in one sitting

It was 2 in the morning, and I was 80,000 words into a draft when my beta reader sent it back with one comment circled in red. “You have three Ethans.”

She was right. There was Ethan, the love interest. Ethan, the bartender, who was supposed to be background. And Nathan, who apparently did not sound different enough from Ethan at 2am, because my brain had given up somewhere around chapter nineteen.

Naming characters is the smallest part of writing a novel and the one that eats the most hours. The main character needs a name that can survive 300 pages. The bartender needs a name that takes four seconds. Your brain will spend forty minutes on the bartender and name the villain “Malice Ravenblood” without irony, and I say this with love, because I have done both.

So here is the system I use now. I keep a shortlist of names for every character on the Baby Name Picker and I spin instead of stewing. One sitting. One named cast. Here is how it handles every naming problem a manuscript can throw at you.

The main character’s name has to carry 300 pages

Most names cannot do that. They sound fine in chapter one and by chapter twelve you are cringing every time you type them. The name that looked cool in your notes reads as silly in dialogue. “Kaelith, pass the potatoes” is a sentence nobody should have to write, but someone named their protagonist Kaelith and discovered it at chapter twelve, the hard way.

The wheel fix: load your top five or six candidate names and spin. Then test the winner before you commit. Say it out loud fifty times, fast. Write three lines of dialogue with it. Type it ten times in a row. If it still feels good after all that, it has passed the only audition that matters.

One thing the spin gives you that staring at a list never will: it breaks the stalemate. Every writer I know has a shortlist of four names they have been “deciding between” for six weeks. They were never going to decide. The wheel decided, and the name that landed stopped being a candidate and started being a person.

Background characters deserve names, not leftovers

Your novel has a bartender, a taxi driver, a nurse in one scene, and a neighbor who appears twice. Each of them needs a name for about four seconds of screen time, and your exhausted brain will name all of them Steve, or worse, some variant of your main character’s name. That is how you get three Ethans.

This is the wheel’s easiest win. Keep a running list of ordinary names, first and last, on a spare wheel: TOM REYES, ANA HALE, LEE VOSS, RON QUINN. When a minor character shows up, one spin and they have a full name in two seconds. Move on. The scene keeps moving, and your reader never has to wonder why every tradesman in the city is named Steve.

My one rule for background names: no two minor characters in the same chapter start with the same letter. I learned this one from a reader who could not tell my witness from my waiter. “Walt and Wren,” she said, “pick one.” The wheel will happily give you two W names in a row. You have to veto the second one.

Villains whose names don’t sound like cartoons

Nobody is scared of Malice Ravenblood. They are scared of someone named David. Real menace hides in ordinary names, and the scariest villains in fiction have names that would not look out of place on a mailbox: Annie Wilkes. Anton Chigurh. Even “Hannibal Lecter” is just two normal-sounding names put together.

The wheel fix works in reverse here. Load the villain shortlist and spin, but before you accept the winner, run the mailbox test. Would this name look normal on a package slip? If yes, it is scarier than anything with “Raven” or “Dark” in it. If your shortlist is all edgy on purpose, scrap it and start a new wheel with boring names. The most unsettling name in your manuscript should be the one that sounds like your neighbor.

And if you are writing for kids or a lighter tone, the reverse applies. A slightly ridiculous name like “Baron von Grump” does more work than a dark one. Match the name to the book you actually wrote, not the book you daydreamed about at sixteen.

Fantasy and period names have to fit the world

“Kaitlyn” did not exist in 1740. Nothing pulls a reader out of a period piece faster than a name from the wrong century, and nothing makes a fantasy world feel cheap faster than a cast where every name ends in “-eth” or starts with “X.” Readers notice patterns. They will not tell you nicely.

Load two wheels: one with names researched for your setting’s era or culture, one with your fantasy-invented names. Spin from the era wheel when you want the world to feel grounded, from the fantasy wheel when you want it to feel strange. The trick is the ratio. Most characters should sound like they belong to the same world. One or two odd names are flavor. Six is a pattern, and patterns read as the author showing off.

I’ll be honest, I once named an entire elven council and did not realize until a friend pointed out that all seven names ended in “-iel.” They were supposed to be distinct people. They sounded like a choir. One wheel of varied names would have caught it in a sitting.

First name plus last name is where good names die

A first name you love can fall apart the moment it meets a surname. “June Marlowe” has a ring to it. “June Okafor” is fine. “June June” is a thing that has happened to real drafts. The rhythm matters, the initials matter (check them, seriously), and the full name has to look right on a chapter heading.

This is the one place where two wheels beat one. Load first names on one wheel, surnames on another, and spin both. Try the pairing out loud. Write it the way it would appear in the book: “ELIAS VOSS walked into the room.” If it sounds like a real person, keep it. If it sounds like a password, spin again.

One detail that saves headaches later: search your manuscript for the surname before you lock it in. If you picked “Hale” and your novel already has a town called Hale, a horse named Hale, and a storm called the Hale, that is a problem you can avoid in thirty seconds.

When the name stops fitting, rename the character

Halfway through a draft, a character can outgrow their name. The timid sidekick becomes the comic relief. The minor love interest becomes the second lead. The name you picked in chapter one now belongs to a different person, and every time you type it, it feels like calling a grown man “Bubby.”

Renaming mid-draft is scary because of search-and-replace, but it is less scary than 40 more chapters of the wrong name. Spin a fresh shortlist, commit to the winner, and do the find-and-replace in one pass. Then read the last three chapters with the new name in place. If the character suddenly feels more like themselves, you just fixed a real problem.

One warning from experience: do this for one character at a time. I once renamed three characters in the same week and spent a month untangling who did what in chapters I had already “finished.” If more than one name needs changing, spin the Random Name Picker to decide the order, and finish one before you start the next.

The whole cast, in one sitting

Here is the actual workflow. Before your next writing session, open the Baby Name Picker and make one wheel per naming job: mains, background cast, villains, surnames. Work through your character list top to bottom. Spin, test, commit, move on.

You will be done in an hour. Compare that to the six weeks you spent not deciding between four names. The names will be good, because you already did the creative part when you built the shortlists. The wheel just did the part your brain kept postponing: choosing.

And for the deadlocks, the “I narrowed it to two and I am going in circles” cases, spin the Decision Wheel with just those two names on it. Two options, one spin, verdict. It works because the real relief is not getting the right name. It is getting out of the choosing.

Spin the Baby Name Picker and name your whole cast tonight. Your manuscript has enough real problems. The bartender’s name should not be one of them.

Palette Challenge Wheel: 12 Limited-Color Painting Exercises

A full tube set won't fix muddy colors. Spin a two- or three-color palette on the Color Wheel and run these 12 limited-palette painting exercises that teach mixing, value, and how to commit.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 18, 2026 Updated October 18, 2026

On this page

  1. 1. Two colors and a mug
  2. 2. The primaries round
  3. 3. One-color portraits
  4. 4. Complementary pairs, real landscapes
  5. 5. Warm skies and cool skies
  6. 6. The wild-card scramble

Last spring I bought the 24-tube acrylic set. Big mistake. For a month every painting I made came out brown and tired, like all the colors were apologizing to each other. A friend who actually went to art school looked at my shelf and said, “Stop buying paint.” She keeps a Color Wheel on her phone with palette sets loaded, and she won’t touch a canvas until the wheel picks two or three colors. I tried it that same weekend on a coffee mug.

Sounds silly. Works great.

A limited palette takes color-matching off your plate. You stop hunting for the perfect tube and start paying attention to value, edges, and mixing. That’s where paintings get good. The wheel part matters because your brain has favorites. Left alone, you’d pick blue and white every time. The spin forces the awkward combinations, and the awkward combinations are the lesson.

Here’s the setup. Load the wheel with palette sets, not single colors: two-color pairs (“burnt umber + white”, “ultramarine + white”), trios (“red, yellow, blue”), themed sets (“warm sunset”, “cool storm”). Spin before each exercise and commit to whatever lands. No re-spins. That’s the whole game. Twelve exercises, six rounds.

1. Two colors and a mug

Exercise 1: Spin a two-color pair plus white. Paint a white mug on a plain background. With only two colors, a white object stops being “white” and becomes a study of gray. You’ll mix ten shades before lunch and learn more about value than any tutorial can give you.

Exercise 2: Spin another pair and paint your shoe. Same rule. Shoes are forgiving subjects and brutally honest teachers: every wrong value shows up as a dent or a lump.

Keep these small. Six inches square is plenty. A big canvas with two colors turns into a chore; a small one turns into a finished painting.

2. The primaries round

Exercise 3: Spin until the wheel lands on “primaries plus white” and paint an apple. Red, yellow, blue, white. That’s it. Every green on the apple’s leaf, every orange in the highlight, every purple in the shadow comes from your knife, not a tube. I’ll be honest, the first attempt looks like a traffic light. The third one looks like fruit.

Exercise 4: Same palette, new subject: a teapot. The teapot adds metal and glass, which means reflections, which means mixing grays with actual color in them instead of tube gray. Big jump in skill for one extra object.

Don’t clean your brush between colors here. The muddy brush is part of the exercise. It teaches you to use what’s on the bristles instead of panicking and reaching for a new tube.

3. One-color portraits

Exercise 5: Spin one color. Any color. Paint yourself from a photo in that color plus white. Likeness without color is the purest drawing exercise there is. If the portrait reads as you in raw umber, you understand structure. If it doesn’t, more color wouldn’t have saved it.

Exercise 6: Same color, new subject: your pet. Fur in one color forces you to separate the animal from the background using value alone. This is the exercise that made me stop drawing outlines first. You learn to build form with patches of tone, because there’s nothing else to build it with.

Here’s the thing about monochrome: it looks impressive on a wall. Frame the good one. Nobody needs to know it was an exercise.

4. Complementary pairs, real landscapes

Exercise 7: Load the wheel with complementary pairs: orange and blue, purple and yellow, red and green. Spin one pair and paint a field at dusk. Complements vibrate against each other, so a two-color sunset can look brighter than a twelve-color one. Mix them together and you get grays that already belong in the painting. Nothing clashes.

Exercise 8: Spin another pair and paint an ocean horizon. Two colors, one horizon line, all the weather you can suggest. The discipline is restraint: when the whole sky wants to be one gray, you have to decide which gray, and that decision is painting.

Old masters did most of their underpainting in two colors for exactly this reason. You’re not doing a quirky challenge. You’re doing the same exercise they did, with a phone instead of a master.

5. Warm skies and cool skies

Exercise 9: Spin a warm set: red, orange, yellow, plus white. Paint a sunrise over water. Warm colors advance, which means the whole painting rushes toward the viewer. Your job is to push some of that warmth back with darker values so the sun stays the brightest thing in the frame.

Exercise 10: Spin a cool set: blue, teal, violet, plus white. Paint a storm sky. Cool colors recede, so the painting wants to flatten. The exercise is keeping the clouds in front of the sky with contrast alone. Do both skies in one session and you’ll feel the temperature difference in your hands. Warm sets paint fast and loud. Cool sets paint slow and careful.

If you and a painting partner can’t agree which set to attempt first, settle it on the Decision Wheel and get to work. The arguing eats more studio time than the painting does.

6. The wild-card scramble

Exercise 11: The loosest round. Load every color you own into the wheel and spin three, plus white. Set a twenty-minute timer. Paint the first object you see on your desk. No planning, no sketch. Ugly combinations are the point: teal, magenta, and raw sienna will teach you about harmony faster than any color-theory chapter, because you have to find the harmony instead of reading about it.

Exercise 12: Same spin, but someone else picks the subject. Can’t pick one? Put twenty household objects into the Random Name Picker and paint whatever lands. A hairbrush in purple and orange sounds like a joke. Painted well, it hangs in the hallway.

The scramble is the only round where you’re allowed to laugh at the spin. Every other round, the palette is the teacher. This round, the timer is.

Open the Color Wheel, load a palette set, and spin your first two colors. Twenty minutes, one small canvas, no brown. The 24-tube set can stay on the shelf. You’ll reach for it again soon enough, but this time you’ll know which three tubes actually matter.

Poetry Picker: Choose Tonight's Poem in One Spin

Poetry night stalls when nobody will pick the first poem. Load a Book Picker with the books on the table, the poets you keep skipping, or the poems themselves, and let one spin break the choosing deadlock.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 21, 2026 Updated October 21, 2026

On this page

  1. Put the books on the wheel, not the shelf
  2. Start a poem-a-night ritual
  3. Let the wheel pick what you read to the kids

  5. Discover one new poet a week
  6. Read one poem on date night
  7. Set the open mic order without the awkward pause
  8. One rule keeps the whole thing working

It’s 9:20 on a Thursday and poetry night is off to a bad start. Three friends, one couch, six poetry books fanned out on the coffee table like a hand of cards, and nobody has read a single poem yet. Everyone keeps saying “you pick.” Someone reaches for the Frost and puts it down. Someone else opens Mary Oliver, reads two lines to herself, and closes it.

Forty minutes of this and the poems stay in the books.

A Book Picker breaks the deadlock. Load it with the books on the table, or the poets, or the poems themselves, and one spin decides. The choosing is the whole bottleneck. Once it’s gone, the reading starts.

Put the books on the wheel, not the shelf

You own poetry books you have not opened in a year. Everyone does. Put each one on the wheel: the Neruda collection, the anthology from college, the slim volume a friend gave you for your birthday, the one you bought because the cover was nice.

Spin before anyone sits down. Whatever lands is tonight’s book, and nobody gets to veto it. That last part matters. The first time we did this, the wheel landed on a book of translated Polish poetry I had owned for three years and never opened. We read six poems out of it. I liked two of them. That was enough.

Start a poem-a-night ritual

Nine poems a week sounds ambitious until you realize most poems take ninety seconds to read. Load the wheel with twelve poems you already like, the ones you would read to someone to prove poetry isn’t boring, and spin one before bed. One poem. That’s the whole ritual.

Read it out loud. Poems read silently are a different animal, and usually a duller one. If you live with someone, read it to them whether they asked or not. My partner now expects it. Some nights we talk about the poem for five minutes. Some nights we just say “huh” and go to sleep. Both are fine.

Let the wheel pick what you read to the kids

Kids like poems more than adults expect, as long as nobody reads them in a serious voice. Funny rhymes, gross poems about bugs, anything with a dragon. The rule in my house: the kid spins, the adult reads, and the adult does the voices.

Load ten short poems on the wheel and let the kid spin every night. If it lands on the fart poem three nights in a row, that’s the kid’s favorite poem now and you should accept it. We read the same one about a frog for two weeks straight in March. I can still recite it. So can my daughter. That’s how rituals form.

When two kids fight over whose turn it is to spin, settle it with the Random Name Picker before the poetry wheel spins. One argument solved, one poem read.

Run your poetry group’s picking order

Poetry groups die on two questions: who reads first, and which poem. Put the names on one wheel and the poems on another. Spin a reader, spin a poem, and the whole evening runs itself.

This works because nobody has to volunteer. Volunteering is the hard part. Going second is easy. Nobody wants to be first, everybody is happy to be third, and the wheel doesn’t care about any of that.

I’ll be honest: the group I read with tried taking turns for months, and we spent half of every meeting negotiating the order. The wheel ended that in one night. We read more poems now and argue about less.

Discover one new poet a week

Put twelve poets you know nothing about on the wheel. Spin on Sunday. That poet is your week: find five poems online or at the library, read them through the week, and pick one to read out loud at the next poetry night.

This is the wheel doing what wheels do best. You would never have picked a 14th-century Persian poet yourself. You would have picked someone you already liked. The spin makes you read the thing you would have skipped, and once in a while the thing you skipped turns out to be the best part of the month.

Need a starting list? Any “100 essential poets” list works. Take twelve names you’ve never heard of and put them on.

Read one poem on date night

Sounds silly, works great. One poem, read out loud to each other, picked by a spin so neither of you can take the blame if it’s a bad one. Ten minutes, tops. It beats scrolling through your phones for forty minutes deciding whether to watch something.

Pick the list together: twenty poems you both half-remember from school, plus a few new ones. If the Decision Wheel already settled what to cook for dinner, let the poetry wheel settle what happens after. Fair is fair.

Set the open mic order without the awkward pause

Six friends, a living room, everyone reads one poem. The awkward part is always the same: the long silence after “so who wants to go first?” Spin the order and the silence never happens.

Put the readers’ names on the wheel. First spin opens, last spin closes. The middle of the order doesn’t matter much, but the first slot does, and nobody should have to volunteer for it. The wheel takes that bullet.

One rule keeps the whole thing working

Commit before the spin: whatever lands gets read out loud. The night someone says “let’s spin again” three times in a row, the ritual dies. One spin, one poem, no take-backs. If the poem is terrible, read it with feeling anyway and roast it together afterward. Some of our best poetry nights were the bad-poem nights.

Rebuild the wheel once a month. Take off the poems everyone groans at. I had an eleven-minute epic on mine for two months and the spin kept dodging it, which tells you everything. The wheel knows what you actually read.

Ready to try it? Load up the Book Picker with the poetry books on your shelf and spin once tonight. One poem, ninety seconds, read out loud. That’s the whole evening plan, and it’s a good one.

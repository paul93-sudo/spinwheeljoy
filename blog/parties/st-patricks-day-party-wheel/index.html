St. Patrick's Day Party Wheel: Plan the Green Night

Eight friends, one group chat, and zero decisions made by March 10. A St. Patrick's Day party wheel settles the food, drinks, dress code, games, and music in six spins.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 28, 2026 Updated October 28, 2026

It’s March 10, and the group chat for our St. Patrick’s Day party has 64 unread messages and no plan. Dana wants corned beef. Mike wants a potluck because corned beef for eight people costs real money. Priya wants games. Tom wants “just drinks and music,” which is his answer for every party and has never once produced a party. Someone suggested green beer at 9:14 pm on Tuesday. Someone else replied with a photo of a leprechaun hat. Then the chat went quiet for three days, the way group chats do when everyone is waiting for someone else to decide.

Last year we fixed this with one change. We put the whole night on the Decision Wheel and spun for each piece of the party, one spin at a time, in front of everyone. Food, drinks, dress code, games, music, who brings what, and the toast. Six spins, about ten minutes, and the night had a shape. Nobody got their first choice on everything, and that turned out to be the point. A wheel result is easy to accept in a way a group vote never is, because nobody at the table overruled you. The wheel did.

Here is how we run it, piece by piece, with the rules that kept eight opinionated friends from turning it back into a debate.

Spin for the food first

Food comes first because it decides the budget and the shopping list, and everything else can wait until those two are settled. Our food wheel holds six mains: beef stew, soda bread and soup, colcannon and sausage, shepherd’s pie, boxty and bacon, and fish and chips. Spin once. That dish is dinner. Sides stay fixed every year, soda bread and a big salad, so the spin only ever changes one thing. That limit matters. A wheel that decides the whole menu in one spin feels random in a bad way. A wheel that picks the main dish feels like a game.

Two years ago the wheel landed on beef stew, and I was relieved in a way I did not expect. Stew goes in the slow cooker at noon and minds itself until guests arrive, which meant I spent the afternoon setting up instead of standing over a stove. Last year it landed on shepherd’s pie, and Mike, who had argued for a potluck, ended up making the pie because he had a recipe from his grandmother and suddenly wanted to use it. That is the side effect nobody plans for. Once the wheel picks the dish, someone usually volunteers for it, because a picked dish feels like an assignment with a story, not a chore.

One rule matters here. If the wheel lands on a dish nobody can cook and nobody can afford to order, spin again, but only once. A party menu built on a dish nobody knows how to make is a menu built on takeout menus and panic. After the one re-spin, you take what lands and figure it out. That rule has saved us from exactly one argument, and one argument saved is enough.

Spin for one signature drink

A St. Patrick’s Day party does not need a full bar. It needs one drink that looks like the party, plus water and whatever people bring for themselves. Our drinks wheel holds six options: stout, cider, Irish coffee, whiskey sour, green lemonade, and hot tea. Spin once. That drink is the signature drink of the night, the one in the big jug or the pot on the counter with a little card next to it.

I will be honest. The first year we did this, I wanted the wheel to land on stout, because stout felt right for the night. It landed on Irish coffee, and I spent twenty minutes being annoyed about it before I admitted the coffee was the better pick. Half the guests do not drink beer, and coffee kept the night going past 10 pm instead of fading out at 9:30. The wheel was right and I was wrong, which is a useful thing for a host to learn once a year.

The wheel also solves the quiet problem of guests who do not drink alcohol. Lemonade and tea sit on the same wheel as stout and whiskey sour, so a non-alcoholic result is a normal result, not a special accommodation announced in front of everyone. When the wheel landed on green lemonade two years ago, nobody treated it as a lesser night. The lemonade went in the big glass dispenser with mint and lime, and it looked better in photos than the beer ever did. Put the signature drink where people can see it, with a card that says what it is. A drink without a label gets asked about all night.

Spin for the dress code a week early

Dress code is the one spin you do early, a full week before the party, because people need time to find a hat or dig a green shirt out of a drawer. Our dress code wheel holds six options: all green, gold accessories, shamrock pins, sports jerseys, funny hats, and tartan or plaid. Spin once, text the result to the group chat with a photo example, and that is the dress code. No second spin. A dress code that changes after people have shopped is worse than no dress code at all.

Last year the wheel landed on funny hats, and I braced for a party where nobody bothered. Then Dana showed up in a leprechaun hat taller than her head, Mike wore a foam shamrock hat, and Priya wore a green top hat she had owned since college and never had a reason to wear. The hats did more for the photos than any decoration we bought, and they cost nobody more than a few dollars. The year it landed on all green, the party looked like a team photo, which is a fine look for a group that spends most of the year in black and gray.

Text the result with one clear sentence, not a paragraph of rules. “Dress code: funny hats. Any hat counts.” That sentence gets a better turnout than a list of dos and don’ts, because people can picture it in five seconds. If someone shows up without following the code, let it go. The code is a game, not a door policy, and the fastest way to ruin a party game is to enforce it at the door.

Spin for the games at 8 pm

Games are where a St. Patrick’s Day party usually falls apart, because someone suggests a game nobody else wants to play and the next twenty minutes go to negotiating. Our games wheel holds six options: trivia, bingo, a pot-of-gold scavenger hunt, a card game, a dance-off, and charades. We spin at 8 pm, after dinner and before anyone settles too deep into the couch, and the result is the first game of the night. The winner of that game spins for the second game. That chain keeps the night moving without anyone having to announce “time for a game” like a camp counselor.

Trivia is the safest landing, and it is the one our wheel hits most often, which tells you something about how many segments it deserves. Keep the questions easy and local: name the green river city, name the Irish band, name the three-leaf plant. A trivia round where half the table knows every answer and the other half knows none is not a game, it is a quiz show with an audience. Bingo works for a bigger group, because people can play it while talking, and the scavenger hunt works best when you hide ten gold chocolate coins around the apartment before anyone arrives. Hide them in plain sight, on shelves and windowsills, not inside things. A coin hidden inside a drawer ends the hunt with someone opening your drawers.

For prizes, keep them small and silly. A bag of gold chocolate coins, a shamrock sticker, a green candle from the dollar store. If you want a wheel for the prizes themselves, the Prize Wheel handles that part, and small prizes keep the games friendly. A game with a big prize stops being a game and starts being a contest, and contests make people quiet in a bad way.

Spin for the music every hour

Music is the easiest part of the night to get wrong, because one person’s playlist is another person’s background noise. Our music wheel holds six options: trad Irish tunes, folk rock, pop hits, a ceilidh dance set, a singalong, and quiet background music. Spin once an hour, change the playlist to match, and nobody has to defend their taste for the whole night. An hour is long enough to enjoy a style and short enough that a style nobody likes ends on its own.

The ceilidh set is the landing people fear most and remember best. A ceilidh set means fast fiddle tunes and someone trying to teach a simple group dance in the living room, which sounds chaotic and mostly is. Last year Tom, who had voted for “just drinks and music,” ended up leading a circle dance with six people who had never danced together, and it was the loudest ten minutes of the night. The singalong landing works the same way. Pick songs everyone knows half the words to, not songs one person loves. A singalong where one person sings and seven people mumble is a performance, not a party.

If building the playlists feels like work, the Song Picker picks the songs for you, one spin per playlist. Build the six playlists in advance, one per wheel segment, and label them clearly on your phone. The hour changes should take ten seconds, not ten minutes of scrolling while the room goes quiet. A quiet room at a party is fine for about thirty seconds. After that, someone starts checking their phone, and the night starts leaking out the door.

Spin for who brings what

The potluck argument is the oldest argument in party planning, and it has a simple cause. When people choose what to bring, three people bring dessert and nobody brings plates. Our fix is a wheel with six jobs: soda bread, dessert, salad, drinks, plates and napkins, and ice. Spin once per guest, cross the job off after it lands, and that guest brings that job. No repeats, no swaps after the spin, unless two guests agree to swap with each other directly. The wheel assigns the job, but the guests own the swap, which keeps the host out of the middle.

Use the Random Name Picker to draw the order if the group cannot agree on who spins first. The order matters less than people think, but a drawn order ends the “who goes first” debate before it starts, and that debate is somehow always longer than the potluck debate itself. Text each guest their job right after the spin, in the same message as the dress code, so the whole plan lives in one message they can find later. A plan spread across twelve chat messages is a plan nobody can find on party day.

One job deserves a note. Plates and napkins looks like the boring job, and it is the job everyone hopes not to land on. Make it a good job anyway. The guest who brings plates can also bring the gold chocolate coins for the scavenger hunt, which turns the boring job into the fun job. Small fixes like that keep the wheel feeling fair, and a wheel that feels fair gets used again next year. A wheel that feels rigged gets one use and a story about it.

Spin for the toast at 10 pm

Every party needs an ending, and a St. Patrick’s Day party needs a toast. Our toast wheel holds six topics: friendship, luck, home, work, travel, and health. At 10 pm, the host spins, and the person standing closest to the kitchen counter gives a toast on that topic, sixty seconds or less. The time limit is the whole trick. A toast without a time limit is a speech, and a speech at 10 pm is how a party ends with people looking at their shoes.

The toast topic matters less than the act of toasting, but the wheel still earns its place. Left to free choice, the same two people give the toast every year, and they give the same toast every year. A spun topic forces a fresh toast out of someone who would never volunteer, and fresh toasts are almost always better than rehearsed ones. Last year the wheel landed on travel, and Priya, who never gives toasts, talked for forty seconds about a train ride through Ireland she took at twenty-two. The room went quiet in a good way, the way rooms do when someone says something real for once. Then we drank, and the party wound down on its own, which is the best ending a party can have.

If the spun guest truly does not want to toast, let them pass the spin to the next person. A forced toast is worse than no toast, and the pass keeps the game kind. The wheel is a tool for a good night, not a test of courage, and the night is better when the rules bend for the shy guest.

Try the St. Patrick’s Day party wheel

That is the whole system. Six spins, spread across the week and the night, and a party that plans itself in pieces instead of collapsing under one big group decision. Food first, one signature drink, a dress code spun a week early, games at 8 pm, music every hour, a potluck wheel with no repeats, and a sixty-second toast at 10 pm. Try it on the Decision Wheel before your next March 17, and text the results to the group chat the moment they land. A plan that arrives as a spin result gets accepted faster than a plan that arrives as a suggestion, because nobody has to agree with it. They just have to show up in a funny hat.

Thanksgiving Dish Draft: Who Brings What Without the Drama

Fourteen people, three green bean casseroles, one sad pie. A simple draft order fixes the Thanksgiving potluck before the first 'what should I bring?' text arrives.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 20, 2026 Updated October 20, 2026

It’s the second week of November, and my phone has started doing that thing where it lights up every twenty minutes with the same question from a different relative. “What should I bring?” Grandma texts it. Then Uncle Joe texts it. Then my cousin texts it, but hers says “what should I bring” like she already knows and wants me to talk her out of store-bought rolls.

I used to answer each one by hand, and I was terrible at it. I told three different people “a side,” and last year we sat down to fourteen people, three green bean casseroles, zero rolls, and one lopsided pumpkin pie with a cracked filling that nobody wanted to be the first to cut. We laughed about it. Then we ate a lot of green beans.

This year I’m doing it differently. One Random Name Picker, one draft order, one public dish list. Everyone picks their dish in order, and nobody texts me again until they’re asking for the Wi-Fi password.

Three casseroles and no dessert is a planning failure, not bad luck

The thing about the three-casserole Thanksgiving is that nobody did anything wrong. I said “bring a side” to three people, and three people made the side they always make. That is exactly what you asked for. The problem was never the casseroles. The problem was that the whole meal got planned by group text, which is the worst coordination tool ever invented for groups over four people.

A draft fixes it because the draft is a plan with an order. Nobody brings the same dish twice because the dish leaves the board the moment someone claims it. Nobody gets stuck with dessert as an afterthought because dessert is a tier, not an accident. The embarrassment of the cracked pie is preventable. The pie just needs a slot on the board.

How the draft works

Type every guest’s name into the Random Name Picker. Spin it. The wheel sets the draft order for the whole holiday, first pick to last, and you paste that order into the family group chat where everyone can see it.

Then comes the dish board: every dish the meal needs, listed before anyone picks. People claim dishes in draft order, one dish per person per round. When a dish is claimed, it comes off the board. Nobody can bring green bean casserole because Mia already took it, and Mia cannot pretend she didn’t see it, because the board is in the group chat.

One practical detail. Put the draft order and the dish board in the same message. If the order lives in one text and the board lives in your notes app, somebody will claim a dish out of turn and swear they didn’t see the rules. One screenshot, everything in it, no excuses.

Tier the dishes before anyone picks

A flat list of twenty dishes is just chaos with lines. Tier the board so the picks make sense.

Tier 1 is the dishes the meal lives or dies on. The turkey, the gravy, the mashed potatoes. Big effort, big payoff.

Tier 2 is the reliable classics. Stuffing, sweet potato casserole, cranberry sauce, the rolls people will fight over.

Tier 3 is the supporting cast. Salad, green beans, a second pie, appetizers, the relish tray that makes Grandma happy.

Tiering does two jobs. First, it stops someone from drafting the relish tray in round one while the turkey sits unclaimed. Second, it gives the late drafters a fair read: if you’re picking ninth, you want to know the tiers so you can grab the best remaining tier-2 dish instead of panic-claiming in tier 3.

The host keeps the turkey

I’ll be honest about the one rule I’d push back on in my own system. Some guides say the host should spin too and take whatever they get. No. The host keeps the turkey. A fourteen-pound bird that needs a thaw schedule, a brining bucket, and an oven cleared by 8am is not a fair burden to put on whoever draws pick one.

Keeping the turkey doesn’t wreck the fairness of the draft. It protects it. The person whose kitchen the whole party happens in gets the dish their kitchen can handle, and everyone else drafts from a board that starts at stuffing. Nobody has complained about this rule in the three years my family has run the draft, possibly because the complainer would have to volunteer for the bird.

Let dietary needs claim first

Before the draft starts, anyone with a real dietary restriction gets a first claim on one dish slot. My sister’s kid is gluten-free, my aunt is vegan, and both of them have sat through Thanksgiving dinners where their entire plate was cranberry sauce and hope.

This isn’t an extra dish. It’s a claim. The vegan aunt drafts the roasted vegetable platter in the pre-draft round, and that dish is marked as the vegan-safe option on the board so nobody “improves” it with bacon after she claims it. It costs everyone else nothing, and it means two people get a real dinner instead of a garnish plate.

Snake the rounds so the last picker doesn’t suffer

First round goes pick one to pick six. Second round goes pick six to pick one. That’s the whole rule, and it’s the reason fantasy leagues have used it for decades: the person who picks last in round one picks first in round two.

Without the snake, whoever draws the last slot gets the dregs twice, and that person remembers it all dinner. With it, the last picker in round one gets the first choice of every tier-2 dish, which is arguably the better seat. In my family the snake has killed exactly one genre of complaint: the “I always get stuck bringing the bad dish” speech.

Run a separate round for drinks, appetizers, and the kid table

The main draft covers the meal. Run a second, shorter round for everything else: drinks, ice, appetizers, the kid-table mac and cheese, the paper plates if you’re a paper-plate family. These are cheap, low-effort, and nobody cares who brings them, which is exactly why you want them in a list instead of a group text. The person who grabs “soda and ice” in the second round is done for the whole holiday in one decision.

If you’re stuck on what to offer a picky guest in this round, the decision wheel settles it in one spin.

Have a bail-out rule before you need one

Every Thanksgiving has one: the cousin who texts Tuesday night that their oven is broken, the aunt with the sudden stomach bug. Decide the rule now, while everyone is calm and full of goodwill.

Ours is simple. If someone bails, their dishes go back on the board in the group chat, and anyone can claim one on a first-reply basis. If nothing is claimed by Wednesday morning, the host spins the Random Name Picker among the confirmed guests for an emergency pick, and the winner gets one veto. That veto has never been used. People are generous when the ask is specific and the whole family is watching.

What you cannot do is nothing. An unclaimed stuffing just sits there while everyone assumes someone else handled it, and then Thursday arrives and you’re stuffing a bird with optimism.

Run the draft tonight

Open the Random Name Picker, type in the family names, and spin the order. Then make the dish board in three tiers, paste both into the group chat in one screenshot, and watch the “what should I bring?” texts stop. If you’re still arguing about what side to make once your turn comes, spin the what-to-eat wheel and let it decide for you.

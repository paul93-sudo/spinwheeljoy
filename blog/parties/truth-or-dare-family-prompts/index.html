Truth or Dare Wheel: 20 Family-Friendly Prompts

Twenty all-ages truth-or-dare prompts for game night, plus a wheel that picks the prompt so nobody has to be the one who asks.

Last Thanksgiving, my brother-in-law suggested truth or dare after pie. My mother-in-law said “absolutely not” before he’d even finished the sentence. Fair enough. The last time someone in this family played, a cousin dared an uncle to eat a whole raw onion and the night went sideways from there.

That’s the problem with truth or dare. The game itself is fine. The prompts are the problem.

So here’s the version that actually works with a mixed-age crowd: twenty prompts, all safe for a ten-year-old and still fun for a grown-up. And instead of anyone having to invent the question, the Truth or Dare Wheel picks it. The spin made the call, not your uncle. Nobody gets put on the spot by another player, and nobody has to be the person who asks the weird one.

How it works: the wheel picks, the group plays

Load the twenty prompts below onto the Truth or Dare Wheel. Spin it to pick the prompt. Then spin a name wheel with everyone’s names to pick the player. Two spins, zero arguments.

Before the first spin, agree on two house rules. One: every player gets one pass for the whole night, no questions asked. Two: a dare has to be safe for the youngest person in the room. If grandma wouldn’t survive it, it’s out. Those two rules are the difference between a game people remember fondly and a story that gets told at Thanksgiving forever, in the bad way.

Warm-up truths: keep it gentle at first

The first round sets the temperature for the whole night, so start with questions anyone can answer in ten seconds. Nobody should have to think hard on turn one.

  1. “What food could you eat every single day?”
  2. “What is your earliest memory?”
  3. “What was the first job you ever had?”
  4. “What song is stuck in your head right now?”
  5. “If you had to wear one color for a year, which one?”

Sounds boring? That’s the point. These are warm-up laps. They get people talking, they take no courage, and they usually surface something worth following up on. “You drove a forklift at sixteen?” is a whole conversation.

Dares that get laughs, not tears

The best family dares involve performing, not risking. Laughter is the goal. A quick test I use: would I be happy doing this dare myself? If yes, it goes on the list.

  1. Do your best animal impression, and the group guesses the animal.
  2. Sing the chorus of your favorite song, full drama, no half-singing.
  3. Talk in a movie-trailer voice for the next three turns.
  4. Do ten jumping jacks while spelling your name out loud.
  5. Freeze dance: dance until the music stops, then hold the pose for five seconds.

Number 8 deserves a mention, because it leaks into everything after. Every time that player speaks for the next three turns, the whole table loses it. That’s the multiplier effect you’re after.

Truths that turn into real stories

Once the room is loose, ask for stories. One good story prompts three more, and this is where game night gets memorable.

  1. “What is the funniest thing that ever happened to you at school or work?”
  2. “What is something you’re good at that almost nobody knows?”
  3. “What was the best trip you ever took?”
  4. “What did you want to be when you were eight?”
  5. “If you could learn any skill overnight, what would you pick?”

Number 12 is the one that consistently surprises. The quiet uncle who turns out to juggle. The grandmother who won a pie contest in 1974. Give the storyteller a full minute. Rushing ruins this round.

Dares the whole room joins

Solo dares are great, but group dares break the performer-audience split and pull in anyone who’s been checking out. Use these when the energy dips. Nothing resets a room like everyone looking silly together.

  1. Get the whole room to do the wave, three times, no half-heartedness.
  2. Teach the group a five-second handshake, then everyone does it in pairs.
  3. Everyone draws a portrait of the person to their left in thirty seconds, then holds them up at once.

Keep paper and pens near the table before you start. The drawings from number 18 become souvenirs. Tape the best one to the fridge.

The lightning round: end fast, end laughing

Save the last ten minutes for speed. One spin, one-word answers, five-second dares, no thinking allowed. Ask “favorite color?” and “worst haircut you ever had?” back to back. Shorten the prompts from earlier rounds instead of inventing new ones. Speed works because it removes overthinking, which is the enemy of every party game. End the night on a laugh, not on a slow wind-down.

Make the list yours

These twenty are a starter set, not a constitution. After one game you’ll know which ones landed and which ones your group skipped. Edit the wheel. Add the family-specific ones: “Who burnt the Thanksgiving turkey in 2019?” will get a bigger reaction than any generic prompt. Keep a short veto list too, the two or three topics your family genuinely doesn’t find funny, and honor it.

Want more question-style prompts to mix in? The Would You Rather Wheel runs on the same idea, choice questions that spark arguments worth having. Swap a few in and your list feels fresh next time.

Spin your first prompt

Load the list onto the Truth or Dare Wheel, add everyone’s names to the spin, and let the wheel do the choosing. Your game night just planned itself.

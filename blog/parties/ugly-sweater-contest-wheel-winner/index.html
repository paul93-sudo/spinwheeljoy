Ugly Sweater Contest Wheel: Pick the Winner Fairly

Last December our office ugly sweater contest took forty minutes to judge, and the winner was the boss's sweater. An ugly sweater contest wheel picks the judging order, the categories, and the tiebreak in front of everyone, so the loudest sweater does not always win.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published November 2, 2026 Updated November 2, 2026

Last December, our office ugly sweater contest took forty minutes to judge. Twenty-three sweaters. Three judges who could not agree on anything. And at the end of it, the winner was the boss’s sweater, which was fine, except the boss’s sweater was not ugly. It was a nice navy sweater with one small reindeer on it. Someone had nominated it as a joke, and then nobody wanted to be the person who voted against the boss in a room full of coworkers.

I’ll be honest. I went home that night thinking the contest was rigged, and I was one of the judges. Nobody rigged it. We just had no system. We looked at all twenty-three sweaters at once, argued about what “ugly” even meant, and picked the safest choice when we got tired. That is how most sweater contests end, and it is why the same kind of sweater wins every year: the big, loud, light-up one, or the boss’s.

An ugly sweater contest wheel fixes the judging instead of the sweaters. Load the Prize Wheel with your categories, your judges, and your finalists, and let the spins do the parts people argue about. The wheel does not get tired. It does not know who the boss is. And everyone watches it happen, which turns out to matter more than the result.

Spin for the judging order first

Do not judge the sweaters in the order people walk in. The first sweater sets the bar, and every sweater after it gets compared to that one instead of judged on its own. If the first sweater is a masterpiece of blinking lights and jingle bells, the quiet handmade sweater in slot fourteen never stands a chance. If the first sweater is a plain red one with a snowflake, everything after it looks amazing.

Spin the judging order before anyone puts a sweater on. Load the Prize Wheel with the entry numbers, spin once per entry, and write the order on a card by the door. Entry 7 goes first this year. Entry 12 goes last. Next year the wheel will say something different, and nobody can claim the order helped anyone.

This takes about five minutes for twenty entries, and it changes the whole room. A contestant who knows she goes seventeenth can relax, watch the first few sweaters, and figure out how the judges are scoring. A contestant who finds out she goes first thirty seconds before she walks up cannot do any of that. She just walks up cold, and the judges score her nerves instead of her sweater.

One more rule for the order. Spin it once and leave it alone. Do not re-spin because someone is stuck in traffic or still changing in the bathroom. If entry 4 is not ready when her turn comes, she goes to the end of the line. The order stays the order, and the contest starts on time, which is rarer than it sounds at a holiday party.

Draw the categories before anyone votes

Here is the argument that eats the most time at every sweater contest. What counts as ugly? One judge thinks ugly means the most lights. Another thinks it means the worst color combination. A third thinks a store-bought sweater with a lot of stuff glued on should not beat a sweater someone knitted badly on purpose. They are all right, and they will argue about it for twenty minutes if you let them.

Draw the categories before the first sweater walks up. Load the wheel with five or six categories and spin for the ones you will actually award: ugliest, funniest, most creative, best do-it-yourself, best group theme, and one wild card like most lights. Write the drawn categories on the board. Now every judge scores the same five things, and the sweater with the most lights competes for most lights instead of competing against a hand-knitted disaster for a single “ugliest” title.

Here’s the thing. Categories also save the quiet sweaters. A sweater with one perfect detail, like a hand-sewn cat in a Santa hat, will never win a straight ugliest vote against a sweater covered in tinsel and battery packs. But it can win most creative, and most creative is a real prize with a real ribbon. Without categories, that sweater goes home with nothing, and its owner does not come back next year.

Keep the category list short. Six is plenty. More than that and you are handing out ribbons until midnight, and the last three categories feel like consolation prizes. Spin for six, post them, and judge only those six.

Let the wheel pick the finalists

Twenty-three sweaters is too many to vote on fairly. By sweater fifteen, the judges have forgotten sweater three. By sweater twenty, they are picking from the last five they saw, because those are the ones they remember. That is not judging. That is recency bias with tinsel on it.

Narrow the field with the wheel before the final vote. After the parade round, where every sweater gets its walk, spin for five finalists. Load the entry numbers, spin five times, and write the five numbers on the board. Those five go to the final vote. Everyone else gets a round of applause and a candy cane, and nobody has to sit through a vote on twenty-three options.

This also fixes the shy-sweater problem. Some of the best sweaters belong to people who hate walking in front of a room. They shuffle through the parade round fast, head down, and the judges barely see the sweater. A wheel draw does not care how fast someone walked. Entry 12 is a finalist because the wheel said so, and now the judges have to actually look at entry 12.

If the same entry gets drawn twice, spin again. If a drawn finalist left early, spin for a replacement. Both rules go on the board before the parade starts, so nobody has to invent them in front of a crowd.

Break the tie with one spin, not a revote

Ties happen in sweater contests, and the tie is where friendships get tested. Two sweaters, nine votes each, and someone suggests a revote. The revote goes the same way, because nobody changed their mind. Then someone suggests the judges decide, and the judges are the ones who tied it in the first place. Then someone suggests rock paper scissors, and half the room thinks that is a joke and the other half thinks it is the only fair idea left.

Spin once. Two entries on the wheel, one spin, and the winner takes the ribbon. It takes fifteen seconds. It also gives the tie its own moment, which the room likes. Everyone goes quiet for the spin, and then the contest has an ending instead of a slow argument about voting procedure.

The Random Name Picker works for this too if you want the tiebreak drawn from names instead of entry numbers, but the prize wheel is simpler when the entries already have numbers. Either way, the point is the same. The tiebreak comes from a spin the whole room watched, not from a choice the host made in the moment.

Settle one more tie question before the final vote starts. What happens if the wheel draw itself ties, like two finalists with the same number of votes for the same category? Same answer. Spin again. A second spin is not indecision. It is the system working the way it is supposed to.

Pick the people’s choice separately

Most contests have two winners hiding inside one vote: the sweater the judges liked and the sweater the crowd liked. They are rarely the same sweater. Judges reward craft. Crowds reward the sweater that made them laugh the hardest, which is usually the one with the most going on. When you force both groups into one vote, the crowd’s favorite loses, because the judges out-argue everyone, or the judges’ favorite loses, because the crowd outnumbers them.

Run two votes. The judges vote for the category ribbons. The crowd votes for people’s choice, one vote per person, on a slip of paper in a jar by the punch bowl. Then spin the Prize Wheel to draw the people’s choice winner from the slips, in front of the room, after the judges’ ribbons are announced.

Separating the votes does one more useful thing. It gives the host a clean answer when someone complains. Your sweater won people’s choice, and the judges gave ugliest to entry 7. Both statements are true, both winners get a ribbon, and nobody has to argue about which vote counted more. The jar and the wheel counted both, in public.

Keep the people’s choice vote simple. Name or entry number on a slip, one slip per person, jar closes when the parade round ends. If someone stuffs the jar, that is a different problem, and the fix is one slip per person checked at the door, not a new voting system.

Count a group entry as one entry

Group sweaters cause the messiest argument at sweater contests, and it is always the same one. A couple shows up in matching sweaters, or a whole family shows up as a reindeer team, and someone says the group should count as one entry, and someone else says each person should count separately, and then the group wins three ribbons and the room goes quiet in a bad way.

Decide before the party, and put it on the invitation if you can. A group entry counts as one entry. One number, one spin, one ribbon if it wins. The group can split the prize however they like, but the contest counts them once. This rule goes on the board next to the categories, in the same marker, before the first sweater walks up.

The Decision Wheel helps here if the group cannot agree on what counts as a group. Load it with the options, spin once, and the answer stands for the night. But the default should be simple. Matching sweaters, one entry. A family of four in one theme, one entry. A couple who coordinated, one entry. The rule is easy to explain, and easy explanations survive holiday parties.

One exception worth writing down. Kids under ten can enter separately even if they match their parents, because a kid in a matching sweater is not a strategy. She is a kid who wanted to match her mom. Let her have her own number. The adults will understand, and the kid will remember it.

Draw the prize order, too

The contest does not end when the winner is announced. It ends when the prizes are gone, and the prize table is where the last argument lives. Winner picks first, obviously. But who picks second? The runner-up, or the people’s choice winner, or the funniest sweater? If you have not decided, the host decides in the moment, and the host’s decision looks like favoritism even when it is not.

Spin for the prize order after the ribbons are announced. Load the wheel with the winning entries, spin once per prize, and let each winner pick from the table in the order the wheel gives. First pick takes the gift card. Second pick takes the mug. Third pick takes the candy tin. Nobody argues with the order, because the order came from the wheel, and everyone watched it.

This also fixes the prize table itself. Most prize tables have one good prize and a lot of filler, and everyone knows it. A drawn pick order makes the filler prizes feel fair, because the person who got the candy tin picked third by the wheel, not because the host ran out of good prizes before her turn. She can still be disappointed, but she cannot be suspicious, and at a holiday party that difference matters.

Put a small card on the table with the pick order as it is drawn, so people do not have to remember it. Cross each prize off as it is picked. The table clears in five minutes, the party goes back to being a party, and nobody is still talking about the prizes at midnight.

Try the ugly sweater contest wheel

A sweater contest is supposed to be the fun part of the party. The judging should not take forty minutes, the winner should not be the safest choice, and nobody should go home thinking the boss won because he is the boss. Spin for the judging order, draw the categories before anyone votes, let the wheel pick the finalists, break the tie with one spin, run people’s choice separately, count a group as one entry, and draw the prize order after the ribbons.

Load the Prize Wheel with your entry numbers. Spin the order in front of the room, write it on the board, and run the contest. The sweaters will still be ugly. That part is the point. But the judging will be fair, the room will see it happen, and the sweater that wins will win because the wheel and the votes said so, not because it was the loudest one in the room.

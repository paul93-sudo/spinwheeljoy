Costume Contest Wheel: Pick Halloween Winners Fairly

How to run a Halloween costume contest without the argument: spin for the categories, spin for the judges, spin the tie-breaker. Fair, in front of everyone.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 23, 2026 Updated October 23, 2026

On this page

  1. Spin the categories, don’t pre-pick them
  2. Let the wheel pick the judges
  3. Run the people’s choice as a draw, not a straight vote
  4. Settle ties with one spin
  5. Spin consolation prizes so nobody leaves mad
  6. The kids’ version runs simpler
  7. Three rules that keep it honest

Two Octobers ago I went to a costume party where the host’s girlfriend won Best Costume. Her costume was good. A solid vampire, cape and fangs, drugstore makeup done well. But Marcus had built a full Iron Man suit out of foam and hot glue, with lights in the chest, and the room knew it. When the host announced his girlfriend as the winner, the applause was the polite kind. The kind where people clap for exactly four seconds and then look at their drinks.

Nobody said anything that night. Everybody said something in the group chat the next day.

Here’s the thing about costume contests: the costumes are never the problem. The judging is. Every contest blows up in one of three ways. The host picks the winners, and everyone suspects favoritism even when there isn’t any. The room votes by shouting, and the loudest table wins. Or it’s a popularity contest, and the funniest person in the room takes the trophy home no matter what they wore.

A prize wheel fixes all three because the fairness happens in front of everyone. Nobody can argue with a spin they watched.

Load up the Prize Wheel before the party starts. Here’s how to run the whole contest on it.

Spin the categories, don’t pre-pick them

The mistake most hosts make is announcing the categories in the invitation: scariest, funniest, most creative. Then six people show up as witches, nobody qualifies for funniest, and the most creative award goes to the only person who dressed as something weird.

Wait until you see the costumes. Load ten category ideas onto the wheel: scariest, funniest, best DIY, best store-bought glow-up, best group, best couple, most obscure reference, best makeup, laziest costume (said with love), judges’ favorite. Spin four or five times to set the night’s categories. The categories fit the room instead of the other way around.

This also kills the lobbying. Nobody can campaign for best couple for three weeks if the categories don’t exist until 9pm.

Let the wheel pick the judges

If the host judges, the host’s girlfriend wins. If the room votes by applause, the biggest friend group wins. The cleanest fix is three judges picked by the wheel, and the picking happens in front of everyone.

Type every guest’s name into the wheel and spin three times. Those three are the judges. One rule: a judge can’t win a category they’re judging. Say it out loud, spin, done.

I watched this work at a party of fourteen. The wheel picked a guy who’d shown up in a bedsheet ghost, and he turned out to be the strictest judge of the night. He took it personally. Nobody argued with his calls because nobody had chosen him.

If you’d rather pick judges quietly ahead of time, the random name picker does the same draw without the ceremony.

Run the people’s choice as a draw, not a straight vote

Let the room vote for one favorite costume. Count the votes where everyone can see you do it: a napkin tally on the kitchen counter, in the open. Take the top three vote-getters and put them on the wheel. Spin once in front of everyone.

Handing it straight to the top vote-getter feels obvious, but it has a flaw everyone feels and nobody says: second place by one vote feels robbed. The wheel stage says the top three all earned a shot, and the spin picks among equals. It turns a complaint into a finale.

Nothing happens in a back room. That’s the whole point.

Settle ties with one spin

Ties happen. Two vampires, both good, judges split down the middle. The worst response is a re-vote, because a re-vote just gives the loudest table a second chance.

Put the two names on the wheel and spin once. One spin, witnessed, final. It takes eleven seconds and nobody holds a grudge about it, because a grudge needs someone to blame and there’s nobody to blame.

A two-name spin like this is also exactly what the decision wheel is for, if you want the tie-breaker living on its own wheel.

Spin consolation prizes so nobody leaves mad

Every contest has a Marcus. The guy who spent nine hours on foam armor and loses to a bedsheet ghost with good stage presence. He’ll say he’s fine. He is not fine.

I’ll be honest, I used to skip this part. Then keep a second wheel loaded with small prizes: a king-size candy bar, a horror movie rental code, a rubber rat, a tiny trophy that says almost. Everyone who entered but didn’t win gets one spin. The prizes are silly on purpose. Nobody remembers the candy bar. They remember that the night noticed they tried.

This is the section hosts skip, and it’s the section people mention the next day. Losing stings less when the night hands you a rubber rat and the room laughs with you.

The kids’ version runs simpler

If kids are in the mix, shrink everything. Three categories max: silliest, scariest (age-adjusted), and best effort. Every kid who enters gets a spin on the prize wheel at the end, no exceptions. The prizes sit in one tier: stickers, glow bracelets, candy. A six-year-old does not understand why her pumpkin fairy lost to a zombie, and she shouldn’t have to.

Run the kids’ contest before the adult one. Kids go to bed, adults get petty, and never the reverse.

Three rules that keep it honest

One: every spin happens in front of the room. A spin nobody saw is a rumor.

Two: one entry per person per category. Say it once at the start. The guy who enters all five categories is why rule two exists.

Three: the wheel’s answer stands. No re-spins because someone blinked. The moment you allow one re-spin, you’ve allowed all of them, and you’re back to arguing.

Open the Prize Wheel now and load your categories before the first guest rings the bell. Ten minutes of setup, zero arguments, and Marcus gets his foam armor judged by a wheel instead of his host’s girlfriend. That’s the fairest deal in Halloween.

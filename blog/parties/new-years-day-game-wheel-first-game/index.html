New Year's Day Game Wheel: Pick the First Game of the Year

It is 2pm on New Year's Day, the house is full of cousins, and someone has suggested a game. A game wheel picks the first game, whether the kids play with the adults, a quick game or a long one, who goes first, and the rematch rule, so the afternoon starts with playing instead of twenty minutes of debate.

It is 2pm on New Year’s Day. The house is full of cousins, the leftovers are in the fridge, and someone has just said the words that start the same argument every year: “Let’s play a game.” Within two minutes, four people have named four different games. My uncle wants cards. My cousin wants a board game that takes three hours. The kids want something they can win. Nobody wants to be the person who picks, because the person who picks gets blamed when the game drags.

Here is the thing. New Year’s Day does not need a better game. It needs a faster pick. Put the whole afternoon on the Board Game Picker and let spins settle it. One spin for the first game. One for whether the kids play with the adults. One for a quick game or a long one. One for who goes first. One for a new game or an old favorite. One for the rematch rule. Six spins, and the table has a plan before anyone can start a second debate.

Spin for the first game of the year

Start here, because the first game sets the mood for the whole afternoon. A first game on New Year’s Day has to pass one test: everyone at the table can join within five minutes of sitting down. Anything with a forty-minute setup, a rule book, or pieces that need sorting fails that test at 2pm, when half the table is still full from lunch and nobody has patience for instructions. Save the big box game for later, when the group has warmed up.

Load the wheel with charades, Monopoly, Uno, trivia, Scrabble, and cards. Charades means no board and no setup, which fits a house with a big mixed group, because anyone can act out a word and the kids laugh even when the guesses go wrong. Monopoly means a long game with money and property, which fits a smaller group that wants to settle in for hours, because the game gives everyone a role and the table stays busy. Uno means a fast card game with simple rules, which fits a table with kids and grandparents together, because a seven-year-old can play it and a seventy-year-old can too. Trivia means questions in rounds, which fits a group that likes to argue about answers, because every wrong answer starts a story and the stories are half the fun. Scrabble means a word game on a board, which fits a quieter group that likes to think, because the turns move slow and the talk moves easy. Cards means a plain deck and a game everyone knows, which fits a house where the board games are still in a closet somewhere, because a deck of cards is already on the shelf.

Spin once, and that is the first game. The wheel lands on charades, which means someone stands up, acts out a movie title, and the afternoon starts with laughing instead of reading rules. One rule keeps this spin honest: only put games on the wheel that you can actually start right now. A wheel that lands on a game with missing pieces hands you a search through closets, and no closet search is worth it on New Year’s Day.

Spin for kids together or a kids table

The first game is picked, and now one spin decides whether the kids play with the adults or get their own table. This sounds small until a ten-year-old sits through a three-hour adult game and quits halfway, or a grandparent sits through a kids game and checks a phone. Both sides get bored for the same reason: the game was picked for someone else.

Load the wheel with together, kids table, adults, teams, pairs, and free play. Together means one table for everyone, which fits a game like charades or Uno, because the rules work for all ages and nobody needs a separate setup. Kids table means the kids get their own game at their own table, which fits a long adult game, because the kids play at their speed and the adults play at theirs. Adults means the grown-ups play while the kids do something else, which fits a late afternoon when the kids have a movie or a craft waiting, because a game night does not have to hold every person in the house. Teams means mixed teams of kids and adults, which fits a trivia game, because a kid on each team answers the questions the adults miss and the adults answer the ones the kids miss. Pairs means each kid pairs with one adult, which fits a card game, because the adult can coach without taking over and the kid still plays real turns. Free play means no table at all and everyone picks their own game, which fits a house where the group is too big for one table, because three small games beat one big argument.

Spin once, and that is the seating. The wheel lands on together, which means one table, one game, and the kids sit next to an adult who can help with the rules. If two kids both want different tables and cannot agree, settle it with the Random Name Picker and let one draw pick the table. One rule helps: the kids get a say in the next spin even when this spin goes to the adults. A kid who picked nothing all afternoon stops playing by 4pm, and a kid who picked one thing stays at the table.

Spin for a quick game or a long one

The table is set, and now one spin decides how long the game runs. This matters more than it sounds, because a quick game after lunch keeps the afternoon light, and a long game after lunch eats the whole afternoon. Someone always has to leave at 5pm. Someone always gets hungry at 4pm. A game without a length drifts, and a drifting game loses players one by one until only two people sit at the table.

Load the wheel with quick, long, medium, one round, marathon, and two games. Quick means a game that ends in under thirty minutes, which fits a house with people coming and going, because a short game ends before anyone has to leave mid-turn. Long means a game that runs two hours or more, which fits a small group with nowhere to be, because a long game gives the afternoon a shape and the talk a rhythm. Medium means a game that runs about an hour, which fits most New Year’s Day tables, because it fills the gap between lunch and dinner without swallowing either. One round means a single round of anything, which fits a group that just wants to try a game, because one round tells you if the game works for this table. Marathon means the table commits to the longest game in the house, which fits a group of cousins who only meet once a year, because a marathon game becomes the story they tell next year. Two games means two short games back to back, which fits a table split between two favorites, because each side gets its pick and nobody loses the vote.

Spin once, and that is the length. The wheel lands on quick, which means a thirty-minute game, a clear end, and time left for a second game if the table wants one. One rule keeps this spin fair: announce the length before the game starts. A quick game that turns into a two-hour game feels like a trick, and a table that feels tricked does not spin again.

Spin for who goes first

The length is set, and now one spin decides who goes first. Skip this spin and the table spends five minutes on “you go” and “no, you go” while the game sits unopened. A first-player spin takes ten seconds and ends the politeness loop before it starts.

Load the wheel with grandma, youngest, host, winner, guest, and dad. Grandma means the oldest player at the table goes first, which fits a house where grandma came for the holiday, because going first is a small honor and it costs nothing to give it. Youngest means the youngest player goes first, which fits a table with kids who are losing patience, because a first turn keeps a kid at the table for the whole game. Host means the person whose house it is goes first, which fits a house where the host set up the game, because the host already did the work and a first turn is a fair trade. Winner means the winner of the last game goes first, which fits a table that played earlier in the day, because a streak is fun to defend and fun to break. Guest means the guest who traveled farthest goes first, which fits a house full of out-of-town cousins, because a guest who goes first feels welcome fast. Dad means dad goes first, which fits a table where dad always goes last and jokes about it, because one year of going first ends the joke.

Spin once, and that is the first player. The wheel lands on grandma, which means grandma takes the first turn and the game starts before anyone can argue about it. One rule helps: the first player also reads the first rule out loud. A game where everyone knows the first rule starts clean, and a game where nobody read the rules starts with three arguments in the first round.

Spin for a new game or an old favorite

The game has started, and now one spin decides what happens after it ends: a game nobody has played before, or the favorite the table always comes back to. This is the argument that kills the second half of the afternoon. Half the table wants the new game someone got for Christmas. Half the table wants the game they know by heart. Both sides have a point, and neither side will give in on their own, so the spin decides and everyone accepts it.

Load the wheel with a new game, the old favorite, and a coin flip between the two. A new game means the unopened box from Christmas morning, which fits a table with a gift still in shrink wrap, because New Year’s Day is the best day to learn a game with a full house to learn it with. The old favorite means the game the table has played ten times, which fits a tired afternoon, because nobody has to learn rules and the game starts in two minutes. A coin flip between the two means one spin to pick the new game or the favorite, which fits a table split right down the middle, because a tie broken by a spin feels fair and a tie broken by a vote feels like a loss.

Spin once, and that is the second game. If the table splits between two new games and cannot agree, settle it with the Decision Wheel and let one spin pick between the two finalists. One rule keeps this spin honest: a new game gets one full round before anyone can quit it. A new game judged after five minutes never had a chance, and a table that quits every new game ends up playing the same favorite every year.

Spin for the rematch rule

The second game is picked, and now one spin decides the rematch rule before anyone wins or loses. Set this rule early, because a rematch argued after a loss sounds like sour grapes, and a rematch refused after a win sounds like gloating. A rule set before the game starts sounds like nothing at all, just part of the plan, and that is why it works.

Load the wheel with rematch, new game, stop, snacks, movie, and bed. Rematch means the same game again with the same players, which fits a close game, because a one-point loss deserves a second chance and a one-point win deserves a defense. New game means a different game next, which fits a blowout, because nobody wants to replay a game they lost by fifty points. Stop means game night ends after this game, which fits a table with an early dinner, because a planned stop beats a slow fade where players leave one by one. Snacks means a snack break before anything else, which fits a table that has played for two hours, because hungry players play badly and blame the game for it. Movie means the table switches to a film, which fits a late afternoon when the light goes and the eyes get tired, because a movie keeps the group together without asking anyone to think. Bed means the kids head to bed and the adults play one quiet game, which fits a house with small kids who hit a wall at 8pm, because a kids bedtime is not the end of the night for the adults.

Spin once, and that is the rule. The wheel lands on rematch, which means the loser gets one more game, same players, same rules, no changes. One rule helps: a rematch happens right away, not after a snack break and three side conversations. A rematch delayed by an hour is a rematch that never happens, and a table that never rematches remembers the loss all year.

Spin for who puts it all away

One last spin, and it happens at the end of the night. Games left on the table stay on the table until someone trips over them, and the person who puts them away is usually the host, alone, at midnight. A cleanup spin takes one minute and saves the host from feeling like the only adult in the house.

Load the wheel with host, winner, loser, kids, and everyone. Host means the host puts the games away, which fits a quick night when the host prefers to tidy alone, because some people clean faster solo than with helpers underfoot. Winner means the winner of the last game puts it away, which fits a table that likes a little payback, because winning feels a bit less sweet with a cleanup job attached. Loser means the loser puts it away, which fits a friendly table, because the loser already lost once and one more small job will not sting. Kids means the kids box up the games, which fits a house with kids old enough to sort pieces, because boxing a game teaches them the pieces do not sort themselves. Everyone means the whole table cleans for five minutes together, which fits a house full of people, because five minutes with eight people clears a table faster than one person in thirty.

Spin once, and that is the crew. Set a timer, clean until it rings, and whatever is left waits until morning. I will be honest: this is the spin most tables skip, and it is the one the host misses most. A night that ends with a clean table feels like a good day, and a night that ends with a mess feels like a chore waiting in the next room.

Try it this year. Put the first game, the seating, the length, the first player, the new game or favorite, the rematch rule, and the cleanup on the Board Game Picker and let the spins run the afternoon. The cousins will still be there at 5pm. The dice will still roll. And nobody will have to argue about what to play before the first turn.

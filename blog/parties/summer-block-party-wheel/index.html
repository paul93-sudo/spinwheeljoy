Summer Block Party Wheel: Plan the Street Party

Mara from number 12 showed up on my porch in May with a clipboard, a sign-up sheet, and a plan for a block party that nobody had agreed to yet. By June, the wheel was running the whole street.

Mara from number 12 showed up on my porch in May with a clipboard, a sign-up sheet, and a plan for a block party that nobody had agreed to yet. The sheet had twelve lines on it. Three were filled in. One said “hot dogs??” with two question marks, which told me everything about where this party stood. Mara wanted a street party for Maple Court before summer got away from us. Everyone on the street liked the idea in theory. Nobody wanted to be the person who picked the date, filed the form, and told Dan his grill plan was too much grill for one block.

So we put the party on the Decision Wheel. One spin for the date, one for the permit, one for the food, one for the games, one for the music, one for the crews, and one for the rain call. The wheel did not make us better neighbors. It made the decisions in public, in about ten seconds each, and nobody could say the same three people decided everything again. That last part mattered more than I expected. A block party run by three people feels like their party. A block party run by a wheel feels like the street’s party, and the street showed up for it.

Spin for the date first

The date spin comes first because every other choice hangs off it. A date picked by a group text never sticks, because someone is always on vacation, someone has a graduation, and someone replies three days late with a conflict that reopens the whole thread. Our wheel held six Saturdays: June 14, June 21, June 28, July 12, July 19, and August 2. The wheel landed on June 21, and June 21 was a fair draw for a street with kids, because school was out, the graduation parties were done, and the longest day of the year gave us light until almost nine.

I’ll be honest, I wanted July 12. July felt safer, further out, more time to plan. But a date picked by a spin ends the date thread, and the date thread was the thing killing the party. Once June 21 was on the flyer, people started signing up for real jobs instead of debating Saturdays. Dan took the grill. Priya took the drinks. Luis took the tables from his garage. A fixed date turns “we should do something” into “I am bringing the cooler on the 21st,” and that sentence is the whole party, really.

One rule makes the date spin work: only put Saturdays on the wheel that the street can actually use. We checked the school calendar and the church picnic first, and we left the graduation weekend off entirely. A wheel with a bad date on it gives an answer you have to overrule, and an overruled spin is worse than no spin, because now the next spin looks rigged too. Six Saturdays, all checked, one spin, and the date goes on the flyer the same day. A flyer with a date is a party. A group text about dates is a hobby.

Spin for who files the permit

The permit spin is the one adults avoid, because a city form with your name on it feels like responsibility with paperwork attached. Our city wanted one contact name, one date, and the request two weeks out, plus a note about barricades at both ends of the street. That is a small form, but it sat blank for a week, because everyone assumed someone else would file it. So we put the street on the wheel: Mara, Dan, Priya, Luis, me, and the Hendersons as one entry, since they share a house and a printer.

The wheel landed on Luis, and Luis was a good draw for a form, because Luis files things the day he gets them and keeps the confirmation email in a folder. He filed it that night, got the barricade note back two days later, and posted the approval in the group text with a photo of the form. That photo did more for the party than any flyer, because a filed form is proof the party is real. People who were “maybe” on the sign-up sheet started writing their names in full after that photo went up.

If your street has more than ten houses, the Random Name Picker can run this spin with every house on it, and the result goes on the flyer next to the date. One name, one form, one deadline. And here is the rule worth writing down: the person who files the permit does not also run the food table. Filing the form is a full job, and a full job plus a food table is how one neighbor ends up doing the whole party while everyone else eats. The wheel picks the filer. The wheel picks the food lead separately. Two spins, two people, no heroics.

Spin for the food table

The food table spin is the one that saves the budget, because a block party where everyone brings “whatever” ends up with nine bags of chips and no main dish. Our wheel held six formats: tacos, burgers, potluck, pizza, barbecue, and sandwiches. The wheel landed on potluck, and potluck was the right call for a street where half the houses cook and half the houses buy, because potluck lets both kinds of neighbor show up with something real.

Potluck still needs assignments, or it turns into the chip problem with better intentions. So we spun again inside the format: one spin per house for a slot, mains, sides, drinks, desserts, paper goods, or ice. Mara drew mains and made pulled pork in a slow cooker. Dan drew sides and brought corn and beans. Priya drew drinks and brought two coolers of lemonade and water. The Hendersons drew paper goods, which sounds small until you picture forty people eating pulled pork with no plates. Paper goods is a real job, and the wheel saying so kept anyone from treating it as an afterthought.

A note from a party I got wrong: do not let one house take two slots because they offered. We once let Dan take mains and drinks because his grill was big and his cooler was bigger, and Dan spent the whole party behind the grill refilling a cooler while his kids asked where he was. One house, one slot, and the grill cook eats with everyone else. The What to Eat Wheel can run the format spin if your street wants more choices on it, but the rule stays the same either way. Six formats, one spin, and the food table fills itself from the sign-up sheet instead of from one person’s kitchen.

Spin for the games

The games spin is the one the kids care about, and kids who care about the games will drag their parents to the party, which is the actual attendance plan. Our wheel held six: cornhole, balloons, parade, tug of war, bingo, and scavenger. The wheel landed on cornhole, and cornhole was a fair draw for a mixed-age street, because a seven-year-old can throw a bean bag, a grandparent can throw a bean bag, and nobody needs a rulebook to start.

Cornhole still needs a second spin, or it turns into four adults playing while the kids watch. We spun for the tournament format: pairs drawn from a hat, first to 21, losers ref the next game. Pairs from a hat mixed the street in a way assigned teams never do. Luis, who is sixty-eight and throws underhand, got paired with a twelve-year-old who throws overhand and trash-talks like a pro, and they made the final. That pair is still a story on the street, and it happened because a hat said so, not because someone arranged it.

One more games rule: the water game goes last, not first. We put balloons on the wheel as a full game, and if balloons had won, the plan was balloons at four, after the food and before the cleanup. A water game at noon means wet kids, wet chairs, and a food table nobody wants to sit near. A water game at four means the party ends on a high note and the wet kids go home to change while the adults fold tables. Order matters as much as the game, and the wheel can pick the order too, if your street wants to spin for it.

Spin for the music

The music spin is the one that sets the volume of the whole afternoon, and volume is a neighbor issue, not a taste issue. Our wheel held six: 90s, country, rock, pop, oldies, and kids. The wheel landed on 90s, and 90s was a good draw for a street with parents in their thirties and forties, because everyone knew half the words and nobody had to explain the playlist to anyone.

Here’s the thing about the music spin: a genre alone is not enough. It needs a DJ rotation with it. One person with the speaker all day becomes the party’s DJ by default, and a default DJ plays their own favorites by hour two. We spun for the DJ in one-hour slots: Luis took four to five, Priya took five to six, Dan took six to seven. Each DJ played inside the 90s genre, but each hour sounded different, because Luis plays 90s rock, Priya plays 90s pop, and Dan plays 90s hip-hop and apologizes for none of it. Three hours, three sounds, one genre, and nobody’s phone stayed plugged in all day.

A note on volume, because this is where block parties lose neighbors: agree on the speaker spot before the spin, not after. Our speaker sat at the middle of the street, pointed down the block, not at any one house. A speaker pointed at one house all afternoon is a complaint, even at a reasonable volume. A speaker in the middle, pointed down the street, is background for everyone and foreground for no one. The genre spin picks the songs. The speaker spot picks whether the neighbors still like you on Sunday.

Spin for the setup and cleanup crews

The crew spins are the ones nobody volunteers for, which is exactly why they need a wheel. Setup at two, party at four, cleanup at eight. We spun for four setup jobs: tables, chairs, coolers, and barricades. We spun for four cleanup jobs: trash bags, leftover sorting, table return, and barricade return. Eight jobs, eight spins, and every job had a name on it before the flyer went out.

The cleanup spin deserves a word, because cleanup is where a block party either ends well or ends with three tired people and a street full of cups. Our rule was simple: the cleanup crew spins at the party, not before, and the spin happens at seven, one hour before the end. A crew picked at seven is a crew that is still there. A crew picked in May is a crew that forgot, or moved, or left early with a tired toddler. Seven o’clock, wheel out, four names, and the four names get the first pick of the leftover desserts, which is a small payment that works better than thanks.

Two rules make the crew spins fair. First, the permit filer does not take a cleanup job. Luis filed the form, so Luis was off the cleanup wheel, and nobody argued with that, because everyone saw the form photo. Second, the grill cook does not take a setup job. Dan cooked, so Dan was off the setup wheel, and Dan showed up at four rested instead of at two sweaty. A wheel with exemptions written on it before the spin is a fair wheel. A wheel with exemptions added after the spin is an argument.

Spin for the rain call

The rain call spin sounds odd until you have watched twelve adults check twelve weather apps and reach twelve conclusions. Our rule was this: one spin picks the call time, and one person makes the call at that time, whatever the sky looks like. The wheel held six times: 8am, 10am, noon, 2pm, 3pm, and “morning of, no call.” The wheel landed on 10am, and 10am was a good draw for a four o’clock party, because it left time to send the text, move the tables under the Hendersons’ carport, and still start on time.

The call itself was one text from Luis, since Luis was the permit name and already the street’s contact. The text said the party was on, tables under the carport, games on the driveway, same time. One text, one voice, no thread. A rain call made by a thread is a thread, and a thread at noon on party day is twelve people typing “what do we think” while the sky does whatever it wants. One person, one time, one text, and the party happens under a carport if it has to. Our June 21 stayed dry, but the carport plan was written on the flyer anyway, and that line on the flyer kept three families from staying home over a cloudy forecast.

A last rain rule: the backup date goes on the flyer with the real date, not in someone’s head. Ours was the next Saturday, June 28, same time, same street. A backup date on the flyer is a plan. A backup date in someone’s head is a rumor, and a rumor does not get forty people to the same street twice.

Try the summer block party wheel

A street party does not need a clipboard hero and a sign-up sheet with three names on it. It needs one Saturday everyone can see on a flyer, one name on the city form, one food format with slots for every house, one game the kids will drag their parents to, one genre with a DJ rotation, two crews picked before the party and at seven, and one rain call made by one person at one time. Put June 14, June 21, June 28, July 12, July 19, and August 2 on the Decision Wheel and spin for the date first. Then spin for the permit, the food, the games, the music, the crews, and the rain call, and let the wheel carry the decisions so the street can carry the party.

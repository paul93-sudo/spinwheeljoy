Would You Rather: 25 Conversation Starters That Actually Work

Small talk dies fast. These 25 would-you-rather questions (dinner parties, road trips, team lunches, classrooms, kids) keep the conversation going long after the snacks run out.

Last July, my cousin showed up to the family barbecue with nothing but a phone and a Would You Rather Wheel. No board games, no playlist wars. By dessert, my uncle (a man of about eleven words per evening) was arguing that telepathy is clearly worse than teleporting, and the whole table had picked sides. I had been to a dozen of these cookouts. I had never heard him talk that much.

That is what the format does. Every question forces a choice, and choices show who people are. Answering is also permission: saying something about yourself inside a game feels safer than volunteering it cold. Load the questions onto a wheel, spin, and the conversation runs itself.

Dinner party openers

The goal of round one is simple: get every voice in the room. One-sentence answers, no defending yet. Once someone has spoken once, speaking again feels easy. These are warm-ups: low stakes, high opinions.

  1. Fly or be invisible?
  2. Always know the time or always know the date?
  3. Have a personal chef or a personal driver?
  4. Visit any place in history for a day or read minds for a day?
  5. Never wait in line again or never hit a red light again?

The debate starters

The table is talking. Now escalate. The best debate questions have no clear winner; both options cost something real. Watch for the moment someone switches sides mid-argument. That is how you know the question landed.

  1. Give up your phone for a month or give up dessert for a year?
  2. Have unlimited money but only four hours of sleep a night, or normal money with perfect sleep?
  3. Be the funniest person in every room or the smartest?
  4. Live 200 years in the past or 200 years in the future?
  5. Know every language on Earth or be able to play every instrument?

Keep it work-safe: team lunch questions

Colleagues learn more about each other from one round of these than from any icebreaker worksheet, and nobody has to do trust falls. The rule at work: questions about people, never about work. For a brand-new team, warm up with an Icebreaker Wheel first, then let would-you-rather do the rest.

  1. Work from a beach cabin or a city penthouse?
  2. Unlimited vacation but no promotions, or fast promotions but no vacation?
  3. Take every Monday off or every Friday off?
  4. Have every meeting last 15 minutes or have your inbox always be empty?

Road trip fuel

A long drive needs a steady supply of questions. The rhythm matters: silly questions keep the energy up, thoughtful ones fill the quiet stretches. Alternate them. And spin the Random Name Picker for who answers next; it keeps the back seat involved and stops the same two people from carrying the whole game.

  1. Talk like a pirate forever or sing everything you say?
  2. Relive one day of your life or see one day of your future?
  3. Stop at every roadside attraction or drive straight through with no stops?
  4. Have the perfect playlist or the perfect snacks for the trip?

Classroom-safe picks

Teachers, this format was made for morning meetings and brain breaks. No wrong answers, no preparation, and the quiet kids often give the best ones. Ask students to defend their choice in two sentences and you have taught a speaking standard without anyone noticing.

  1. Read minds or teleport?
  2. Have a pet dragon or be able to breathe underwater?
  3. Live in a treehouse or a castle?

Kid-friendly versions

For younger players, both options have to be concrete and visual. Kids argue hardest when they can picture both choices, and their answers are usually the funniest at the table. Skip abstract trade-offs with kids under eight. Fame or fortune means nothing to them, but this next set starts a real debate.

  1. Have a pet dragon or a pet unicorn?
  2. Eat ice cream every day or pizza every day?
  3. Be able to fly or turn invisible?
  4. Live in a treehouse on the beach or a castle in the mountains?

The follow-up rule

The secret to a great round has nothing to do with the questions. Always ask why. The first answer is a warm-up; the reason behind it is the conversation. “Mountains, because my grandfather took me hiking every summer” beats “mountains” every time. Make why a house rule and the game turns into genuine connection. The best hosts interview the answers instead of just reading the questions.

Let the wheel referee

Arguments about whose turn it is kill momentum. Spin the wheel for the question, then spin a name picker for who answers. The game runs itself and nobody plays favorites. This matters more than it sounds: the moment someone feels the loudest person is getting every turn, they check out. The wheel is neutral, and neutrality keeps everyone in. When the group is up for something spicier later, the Truth or Dare Wheel uses the same referee logic.

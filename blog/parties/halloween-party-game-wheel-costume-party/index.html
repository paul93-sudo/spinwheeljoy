Halloween Party Game Wheel: 8 Games for the Costume Party

Eight Halloween party games that actually run on a prize wheel: fair costume judging, mummy-wrap drafts, trivia twists, and a midnight prize draw that ends the night right.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 21, 2026 Updated October 21, 2026

  2. Draft the mummy-wrap teams by spin
  3. Use the wheel to pick who carves first, and what they carve

  7. Assign the haunted-house tour groups fairly
  8. Close the night with the midnight prize draw
  9. Load the wheel before the first guest arrives

Two years ago I hosted a Halloween party where the costume contest judging took forty minutes. Forty. Minutes. Ten of us stood in a circle arguing about whether Derek’s werewolf counted as “homemade” or “store-bought with accessories,” and by the time we handed him the candy crown, half the party had drifted outside and nobody clapped. The games after that went about as well: a pumpkin carve-off where the same two people volunteered first every time, trivia that died when someone shouted the answer, and a pinata that nobody wanted to start.

Here’s the thing: the games weren’t the problem. The running of them was. Every party game stalls at the same three spots: who goes first, how you keep it fair, and how you stop the loudest guest from running the whole night. A prize wheel solves all three because nobody can argue with a spin.

Load up the Prize Wheel and use it as the engine behind every game below. Eight games, one wheel, zero debates.

Spin for the costume contest winner, not for the debate

The costume contest is the main event at a costume party, so it gets the worst judging. Either the host decides everything (and everyone suspects favoritism), or the whole room votes by shouting (and the loudest table wins). Both versions end with someone muttering about it in the car home.

Here is the version that ended the muttering at my house. Put every costume on the wheel: each guest’s name and costume in one segment. Spin once, winner takes it. If that feels too random for the grand prize, spin for the judge instead. The wheel picks three impartial judges from the guest list, and those three vote. Either way the fairness is visible to the room, which is the part people actually care about. When Derek’s werewolf loses to a paper-bag ghost picked by the wheel, nobody argues, because nobody ran the pick.

Draft the mummy-wrap teams by spin

Mummy wrap is the classic: two volunteers per team, one gets wrapped head to toe in toilet paper, fastest clean wrap wins. It is also the game where the host points at four people and one of them visibly hates being picked.

Type every guest’s name into the wheel and spin four times. Spins one and two are Team A, spins three and four are Team B. Nobody was chosen, nobody was skipped, and the two wrappers plus two wrappies get sorted by one more round of spins if you want. The team that wins gets to pick their prize first from the candy table, and the losing wrappie gets unwrapped by the group, which is half the fun anyway.

I have run this three years straight. The spin-drafted teams always mix friend groups better than any host picking could, because the wheel doesn’t know who just got out of a breakup.

Use the wheel to pick who carves first, and what they carve

Pumpkin carve-off order sounds trivial until three people hover over the best pumpkin and the rest wait around with plastic knives. Spin for order. Then spin a second wheel with carve themes: classic jack-o-lantern, haunted house, your pet, horror movie villain, freestyle.

The theme spin matters more than the order spin. Without it, four of six pumpkins become the same triangle-eyes triangle-mouth face, and the judging is boring. With it, you get somebody carving their pug and somebody else attempting Pennywise from memory, and those are the pumpkins people photograph.

If you need a tie-breaker at the end, the decision wheel settles the last two pumpkins in one spin.

Run the candy-count guess with a wheel, not a shout

Fill a jar with candy corn, count it yourself, and seal the number in your phone. Guests guess the count on slips of paper during the first hour. The five closest guesses go on the wheel. Spin for the winner.

Why the wheel instead of just taking the closest guess? Because the closest-guess system has a known flaw: the person who guesses 312 when the answer is 309 always feels robbed when someone guesses 308. Both were one away. The wheel stage says the top five all earned a shot, and the spin picks among equals. It turns a complaint into a finale. Put the jar on the prize table and spin it in front of everyone right before the costume contest, so the night has a rhythm.

Turn horror trivia into a game of twists

Straight trivia is a quiz, and a quiz at a party is homework. The wheel makes it a game. Load it with trivia twists instead of questions: SKIP (pass your turn to anyone), STEAL (answer for double points), CALL THE WITCH (ask the room, lose a point if the room is wrong), PHONE A GHOST (ask one specific guest, no take-backs), DOUBLE OR NOTHING (your answer counts double or zero).

Each guest’s turn starts with a spin for their twist, then a question from your list. A guest who knows nothing about horror movies can still win with two STEALs and a lucky DOUBLE OR NOTHING, which means the non-horror people stay in the game instead of checking their phones. That is the whole trick of this format: it keeps the knowledge gap from ending the fun early.

Pick the dance-off bracket order with the wheel

At some point in the night somebody puts on Monster Mash and the dance floor becomes a contest. Lean into it. Anyone who wants in puts their name on the wheel, and the wheel sets the bracket order. Pair the spins: spin one vs spin two, spin three vs spin four, and so on.

The crowd judges each pair by applause, which takes thirty seconds per round instead of the forty-minute costume-contest disaster I described above. Winners advance, one more spin for the final order, done. The prize is the candy crown from the costume contest, which Derek has to hand over, and he will be fine about it.

Assign the haunted-house tour groups fairly

If your party has a walkthrough (a decorated garage, a backyard with fog machines), the group order matters more than you think. The first group gets the scares at full power while the actors are fresh. The last group gets tired actors and half-melted fog.

Spin for tour order. First spin goes first, everyone sees it happen, and the last group gets a consolation: they go through together with the lights up, which is actually the funniest version. Two years ago our last group filmed the whole thing like a documentary and it was the best video of the night.

The key detail: spin the tour order at the START of the party, before the first game. If you wait until the haunted house is ready, the people who lost the earlier games will assume the tour order is rigged against them too. Early spin, posted where everyone can see it, kills the conspiracy.

Close the night with the midnight prize draw

Every Halloween party ends with a table of leftover prizes and the host saying “just take stuff.” It is a sad ending. Instead, save the prize table for a closing draw at midnight.

Throughout the night, guests earn draw entries: one for showing up in costume, one per game won, one for the best scream during the haunted walkthrough, one for helping clean up a spill. Write each earned entry as a segment on the wheel, so the frequent winners have more segments and the shy guests have at least one. Spin three times for three prize tiers: the big candy box, the horror movie bundle, the gag prize (a rubber rat, obviously).

This is why the wheel earns its place at a costume party. It ties the whole night together. Every game fed the draw, the draw closes the night, and the rubber rat gets the biggest laugh of the evening, which is exactly how a Halloween party should end.

If your party has kids in the mix, spin the truth-or-dare wheel for the family-friendly round earlier in the evening, then switch to the prize draw for the grown-ups after bedtime.

Load the wheel before the first guest arrives

Open the Prize Wheel now and type in your segments: guest names for the drafts and draws, twist labels for trivia, themes for the pumpkin carve-off. Eight games, one wheel, no forty-minute costume debates. Your party deserves better than Derek’s werewolf controversy.

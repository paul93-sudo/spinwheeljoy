Midnight Toast Wheel: Who Gives the New Year's Eve Toast

It's 11:40 on New Year's Eve, the glasses are full, and everyone is suddenly looking at their shoes. Put the names on a wheel, spin for the toast-giver, and let chance do the awkward part.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 24, 2026 Updated October 24, 2026

On this page

  1. Spin for the toast-giver before the party starts
  2. Let the wheel set the order for more than one toast
  3. Spin for a prompt when someone freezes

It’s 11:40 on New Year’s Eve. The glasses are full, the countdown show is on mute in the background, and someone says, “So, who wants to give the toast?” Every single person in the room suddenly finds their shoes very interesting. Your uncle, who has a story for every occasion, goes quiet. Your sister, who gave a lovely toast last year, shakes her head: she did it last year, and the year before that, and she is done being the default.

Here’s the thing. The toast is a nice moment, but picking the toast-giver is the awkward part. Someone has to volunteer, or the host has to appoint someone, and appointing someone feels like putting them on the spot. A wheel skips all of that. Load the names onto a Random Name Picker, spin it, and the wheel picks. Nobody volunteered, nobody got cornered, and the person it lands on has a job for the next twenty minutes: think of something short and kind to say at midnight.

Spin for the toast-giver before the party starts

Don’t wait until 11:40. Spin early, while people are still arriving and the chips are still full. Put every adult who is willing on the wheel, spin once, and announce the result like it’s a raffle: “Marcus, you’re on toast duty tonight.” That gives Marcus an hour or two to think, instead of thirty seconds of panic while twelve people stare at him.

Spinning early also fixes the repeat problem. In most families, the same confident person gives the toast every year, because they are the only one who will say yes on the spot. When the wheel picks, the quiet cousin gets a turn, the new boyfriend gets a turn, and the person who gave last year’s toast gets a year off. If your group wants a rule, make it one line: nobody gives two years in a row unless the wheel lands on them after a full round of everyone else.

One more detail that matters: only put willing names on the wheel. A toast from someone who truly hates speaking in front of people is not a fun surprise, it’s a punishment. Ask first, “Who’s up for it if the wheel picks you?” and load only the yeses. The wheel is supposed to remove the awkwardness, not add a new kind.

Let the wheel set the order for more than one toast

One toast is fine for a small group. A bigger party usually needs three: one for the family, one for the friends, and one from the host. And the order matters, because nobody wants to follow the person who just gave a tearjerker about Grandma with a joke about the neighbor’s karaoke.

Spin a second wheel for the order. Put the toast slots on it: family, friends, host, kids. Spin until every slot has come up once, and that is the running order. Family first works well, because it sets a warm tone and gets the sentimental part out of the way. Friends second keeps the energy up. The host goes last, because the host’s toast is really the “thanks for coming, happy new year” closer, and closers should close.

If your party has a theme, say a 1980s night or a pajama party, spin the order the same way and let the toasts match the theme. A pajama-party toast order sounds silly, and it is, but the order still stops the same two loud people from giving all three toasts while everyone else sips in silence.

Spin for a prompt when someone freezes

Even a willing toast-giver can freeze. Marcus has the glass in his hand, twelve people are watching, and his mind goes completely blank. This is the moment a prompt wheel saves the night. Instead of staring at Marcus, spin for a prompt: gratitude, funniest moment of the year, a hope for next year, thanks to one person in the room.

Give him the prompt and thirty seconds. “Funniest moment” is the easiest one, because every family has one: the dog eating the Thanksgiving turkey, Dad falling asleep during the fireworks, the year the power went out at 11:58 and everyone counted down by phone flashlight. “Gratitude” is the one that makes people tear up, so save it for the family toast, not the friends toast at 11:55 when everyone is already emotional.

I’ll be honest, the prompt wheel also rescues the host. When you are running a party, your brain is full of ice, glasses, and whether the dip ran out. A prompt means you don’t have to write a speech in your head while refilling the chip bowl. Spin, read the prompt, say two honest sentences about it, done.

Give the shy toast-giver one pass, no questions asked

The one-pass rule is what makes the wheel fair instead of scary. If the wheel lands on you and you genuinely don’t want to do it, you say “pass,” and the wheel spins again. No teasing, no “come on, just do it,” no guilt. One pass per person per night, and the pass costs nothing.

Why does this work? Because the fear of being trapped is what makes people refuse to go on the wheel at all. Take the trap away and more people say yes. In our house, the pass has been used exactly once in three years, by an uncle who had a terrible cold and could barely talk. He passed, the wheel picked someone else, and he still stayed on the wheel the next year, because he knew the pass was real.

If someone passes twice in one night, they come off the wheel for that night, and that’s fine too. The point was never to force a speech out of anyone. The point was to spread a nice job around, and a wheel with a pass does that better than a wheel without one.

Run a kids’ toast at nine, not midnight

Kids under ten are not making it to midnight, and they know it, and they hate missing the big moment. So give them their own toast at nine o’clock, with juice in plastic champagne flutes, and let the wheel pick the kids’ toast-giver too. Put the kids’ names on a small wheel, spin it after dinner, and the winner gives the nine o’clock toast.

This does three things at once. It gives the kids a real moment instead of a consolation prize. It gives the adults a practice round, because watching a seven-year-old toast to “my dog and pizza” loosens the whole room up. And it gets the kids to bed happy instead of overtired and crying at 11:30 because they are still awake and nobody is paying attention to them.

Keep the kids’ rules simple: one spin, one toast, thirty seconds max, and everyone clinks glasses, even the adults with their juice boxes. If a kid freezes, the prompt wheel works for them too. “Funniest moment” from a seven-year-old is usually the best toast of the entire night, and it costs you nothing but a plastic flute.

The host should not always go first

Hosts love to go first, because going first gets it over with. But the host going first every year turns the toast into a host speech, and then nobody else’s toast feels like it counts. The wheel fixes this without a debate about it. If the order wheel puts the host last, the host goes last, and the host’s toast becomes the closer it should be.

There is a practical reason too. The host is the busiest person in the room at 11:40. They are checking the countdown, finding the good glasses, and making sure the front door is unlocked for the neighbors. A host who has to give the first toast at 11:40 is a host who is still holding a bag of ice when the glasses go up. Let someone else go first, let the host finish their jobs, and let the host’s toast actually land, instead of being rushed between chores.

If your group insists the host must speak, spin for when the host speaks, not whether. First, middle, or last, the wheel decides, and the host can plan around it.

Keep every toast under sixty seconds

The sixty-second rule is the one rule every toast wheel needs, and it’s the easiest to enforce. When the wheel picks you, you get sixty seconds. Not a speech, not a life story, not a recap of the whole year. Two or three sentences, glass up, drink, done. Someone holds up a hand at forty-five seconds, and at sixty the room clinks glasses whether the toast is finished or not.

This rule protects the toast-giver more than the audience. Sixty seconds is short enough that a shy person can survive it. It’s long enough for something real: “This year was hard, and I’m glad we all made it here tonight, and I hope next year is kinder to all of us.” That’s about fifteen seconds, and it’s a better toast than most five-minute speeches.

Put the rule on the wheel’s announcement, not in the moment. “Marcus, you’re up, sixty seconds, go.” When everyone knows the limit in advance, nobody feels cut off, and nobody dreads being picked, because the worst case is one minute of mild awkwardness and then champagne.

Try the midnight toast wheel

Load the willing names onto the Random Name Picker, spin for the toast-giver before the party starts, and spin again for the order and the prompt if you need them. Add the one-pass rule, run a kids’ toast at nine, and keep every toast under sixty seconds. Midnight will still be loud, the countdown will still be chaotic, and someone will still spill champagne on the rug. But nobody will be staring at their shoes at 11:40, because the wheel already decided, and all that’s left is the toast itself.

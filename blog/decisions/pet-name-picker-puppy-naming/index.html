Pet Name Picker: Naming the New Puppy Without a Family Feud

Four people, twelve name ideas, one confused puppy. Load the finalists onto a pet name picker wheel and let the spin settle it before the vet appointment on Thursday.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 16, 2026 Updated October 16, 2026

On this page

  1. Make the shortlist before you spin
  2. Give every kid an equal segment
  3. Vetoes before the spin, silence after
  4. Keep the shelter name or start fresh?
  5. The two-day trial
  6. Naming two at once
  7. One re-spin, agreed up front

It’s 4pm Saturday and the puppy is home. By 4:15, my eight-year-old has decided: Captain Thunderpaws. By 4:20, my wife has countered with Milo. My eleven-year-old, deep in a video-game phase, wants something I won’t repeat here. And my mother-in-law, on the phone from Ohio, announces the dog is called Biscuit, as if the paperwork is done. The vet appointment is Thursday. The puppy, for the record, answers to nothing.

We argued about it for two days. Not a real fight, just the kind where everyone is smiling and nobody is backing down. Then we made a rule: nobody gets to pick. The wheel picks. Here is how a Pet Name Picker ended the debate in about ten minutes, and what we learned about naming a dog without hurting anyone’s feelings.

Make the shortlist before you spin

A wheel can’t fix a bad list. Load it with thirty names and the spin feels meaningless, and nobody trusts it. Twelve is the most I’d put on. Eight is better.

So before anyone touches the wheel: every person in the house submits three names. That’s it. Three. Then run the list through three filters, out loud, together:

  1. Say it three times fast. “Captain Thunderpaws, Captain Thunderpaws, Captain Thunderpaws.” If you trip over it, it’s off the list. You’ll say this name a thousand times at the dog park.
  2. Shout it like you’re calling the dog back from traffic. “BISCUIT, COME!” If it feels silly, it’s off the list.
  3. Picture it at the vet’s office. “We’re here for Milo’s booster.” That one passes. “We’re here for Captain Thunderpaws’ booster” gets you a look from the receptionist, and you’ll get tired of it by March.

My mother-in-law’s Biscuit survived all three filters, by the way. Thunderpaws didn’t survive the first.

Give every kid an equal segment

Here’s where families actually fight. It isn’t about the names. It’s about the process. The loudest kid wins by repetition. They say their name forty times and everyone else’s twice, and by Sunday the dog is named whatever the loudest kid said. The other kids remember this. For years.

The wheel fixes the process, not the names. Load the finalists with equal segments and spin it in front of everyone. Equal segments is the part that matters. Nobody’s name gets a bigger slice because they cried louder or negotiated better at dinner. This is the same reason a Decision Wheel settles family votes: the process is what everyone ends up trusting.

We put six names on ours. My daughter got her three. My son got his three. My wife and I shared three. Equal. When the wheel landed, nobody could say mom rigged it, because mom’s finger never touched it.

Vetoes before the spin, silence after

One rule saved us: you can veto a name, but only before it goes on the wheel. If someone genuinely can’t live with a name, take it off. No mocking, no vote, just off.

What you can’t do is veto after the spin. The wheel lands on Biscuit and suddenly everyone hates Biscuit? Too late. A veto after the spin is a veto with extra steps, and the kids learn that the spin only counts when it agrees with the adults. Agree on the veto rule up front, out loud, and the wheel keeps its authority.

I’ll be honest: my wife vetoed one of my son’s picks before it went on. He pouted for an hour. Then the spin happened and he forgot about it, because the process was fair and the veto wasn’t aimed at him.

Keep the shelter name or start fresh?

Rescue dogs come with names, and opinions about those names. Ours came as “Buddy,” which the shelter had given him a week earlier. Some shelter names stick because they fit. Some are just labels from a clipboard.

Here’s a test that worked for us: use the old name for two days. Not one. Two. If it starts feeling like the dog’s name, keep it. If it still feels like a label, it goes on the wheel against your favorites, no special treatment.

There is one real consideration. If the dog clearly knows its old name (head turns, ears up, comes when called), think twice before changing it. A name a dog already knows is worth something. That’s a genuinely hard call, and for those, a Yes or No Wheel beats arguing about it at 11pm. We spun ours: keep Buddy or start fresh. The wheel said fresh, and nobody argued, because we’d already agreed the wheel decides.

The two-day trial

The spin picked a winner. Don’t carve it in the dog tag yet.

Use the winning name for two full days. The puppy will ignore it completely, like they all do at first. That’s not the test. You’re the test. Say it across the yard. Say it to the neighbor. Introduce the dog to someone: “This is Milo.” If it feels natural by day two, that’s the name. If you catch yourself hesitating or explaining it (“It’s Biscuit, like, after the…”), it isn’t.

We almost learned this the hard way. The spin landed on my daughter’s second choice, and for one evening I kept calling the dog by the other finalist’s name. My daughter caught me. We kept the trial honest and the name stuck by day two. The tag got engraved on day three.

Naming two at once

Some families bring home a pair: siblings, or a dog and a cat in the same weekend. Naming two animals at once doubles the arguments, because now you’re also arguing about whether the names “go together.”

They don’t have to go together. Nobody at the dog park cares that your dogs are named Milo and Kevin. What matters is that the names sound different from each other. “Milo” and “Nilo” will confuse you, the dogs, and everyone at the vet’s office. Spin one wheel twice, or load two wheels and spin once each. Just check that the two winners don’t rhyme before you commit.

One re-spin, agreed up front

Sometimes the wheel lands and the room goes quiet in a bad way. Not the fair kind of quiet: the “none of us actually like this” kind. That’s fine. It’s allowed. But agree on the re-spin rule before the first spin: one re-spin, everyone agrees, and then the second result stands no matter what.

What you don’t do is spin twelve times until someone’s favorite comes up. Everyone sees through that, especially the kids. One re-spin is honesty. Twelve is theater.

We didn’t need ours. Milo won on the first spin, survived the two-day trial, and my mother-in-law still calls him Biscuit on the phone. Some battles you let the wheel win, and some you just let grandma win.

Saturday morning, write down everyone’s three names, run the filters, load the finalists onto the Pet Name Picker, and spin it in the living room with the whole family watching. Ten minutes. Vet appointment Thursday. Done.

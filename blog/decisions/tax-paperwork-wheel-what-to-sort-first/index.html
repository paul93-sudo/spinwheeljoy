Tax Paperwork Wheel: What to Sort First

April arrives, the envelopes are still in a pile on the kitchen table, and nobody wants to open the first one. A tax paperwork wheel picks the first folder, the next pile, and the shred box so the sorting actually starts.

It is a Saturday in April, and the tax pile has been sitting on the kitchen table for three weeks. There is a white envelope from your employer, two thin envelopes you have not opened, a shoebox of receipts, and a folder labeled “tax stuff” that contains exactly one sheet of paper and a grocery list from November. You keep walking past the pile and telling yourself you will deal with it after lunch. Lunch ends. The pile stays.

Here is the thing. Nobody stalls on taxes because the work is hard. They stall because the first step is unclear. Open the wrong envelope first and you find a form you do not understand, and the whole pile goes back on the table for another week. A wheel fixes the first step by making it for you. Load the Decision Wheel with the piles you actually have, spin once, and do only what the wheel says. One spin, one pile, thirty minutes. Then you can stop, and the pile will be smaller than it was.

A wheel sorts paper. It does not file taxes for you, and it does not replace a preparer. What it does is get the envelopes open and the papers into folders, which is the part most people avoid.

Spin for the first envelope to open

Every tax pile has one envelope that matters more than the rest, and it is usually the one from your employer. Load the wheel with W-2, 1099, receipts, deductions, shred, and keep. W-2 means the wage form from your employer, the one that lists what you earned and what was withheld. 1099 means the thin forms from banks, clients, or gig work. Receipts means the shoebox. Deductions means the folder where you meant to keep donation slips and medical bills. Shred means the pile of old statements you do not need. Keep means the folder for papers you file away for next year.

Spin once, and that is the envelope you open first. The wheel lands on W-2, which means you open the employer envelope, check that your name and the numbers look right, and put it in a folder marked “income.” One rule keeps this spin honest: you open only the envelope the wheel picks. If you open three envelopes “just to peek,” you are back to staring at the pile, and the pile wins again.

Spin for the 1099 pile

The 1099 pile is the one people lose. A 1099 form is thin, it arrives in a plain envelope, and it looks like junk mail. Last year my neighbor tossed one in the recycling and spent a weekend in April digging through the bin. Do not be that neighbor. Load the wheel with bank, client, gig work, interest, dividends, and other. Bank means the 1099 from your bank. Client means a 1099 from a client who paid you for freelance work. Gig work means a 1099 from a platform. Interest means the form that lists interest your savings earned. Dividends means the form from a brokerage. Other means any 1099 you cannot place at first glance.

Spin once, and that is the 1099 you hunt down first. The wheel lands on 1099, which means you go through the mail pile, the kitchen drawer, and the email inbox for that one form, and you put it in the income folder next to the W-2. One rule helps: every 1099 you find goes in the same folder the same day. A 1099 on the fridge is a 1099 you will lose.

Spin for the receipt shoebox

The receipt shoebox is where good intentions go to fade. You saved every receipt in January, and by April the ink on half of them is gone. Load the wheel with medical, donations, work, home, car, and toss. Medical means receipts for doctor visits, prescriptions, and dental work. Donations means slips from charities and thrift store drop-offs. Work means receipts for supplies you bought for a job. Home means receipts for repairs and improvements. Car means gas and repair receipts tied to work driving. Toss means receipts that are blank, unreadable, or for groceries, which do not belong in the tax folder at all.

Spin once, and that is the receipt group you sort first. The wheel lands on receipts, which means you pull every medical receipt out of the shoebox, paper-clip them together, and write the total on a sticky note. Then you spin again for the next group. One rule matters: a receipt with no date and no amount goes in the toss pile. You cannot use a blank slip, and keeping it only makes the shoebox heavier.

Spin for the deduction check

A deduction check is just a pass through the folders you already made, to look for slips you forgot. Load the wheel with donations, medical, work, education, home, and none. Donations means you check the charity slips one more time. Medical means you add up the prescriptions you forgot in the shoebox. Work means you check for a supply receipt stuck in a coat pocket. Education means a tuition form or a course receipt. Home means a repair bill from last summer. None means you are done checking, and you close the folders.

Spin once, and that is the category you check. The wheel lands on deductions, which means you spend ten minutes on that one category and then stop. Ten minutes is enough to find a forgotten slip. Two hours is enough to talk yourself into keeping papers you should shred, and that helps nobody. If the spin says none, believe it. A finished pile is a finished pile, and you do not need to keep digging for a receipt that may not exist.

Spin for what goes to the preparer first

If you use a preparer, the handoff is its own stall. People wait until every paper is perfect, and perfect never arrives, so the folder sits on the table until the deadline week. Load the wheel with income, deductions, receipts, questions, ID, and done. Income means the W-2 and 1099 folder goes first. Deductions means the donation and medical folder goes first. Receipts means the clipped receipt groups go first. Questions means you write down the two questions you actually have and send those first. ID means the ID and prior-year return go first, since most preparers ask for those before anything else. Done means the folder is ready and you send it as is.

Spin once, and that is what you hand over first. The wheel lands on preparer, which means you put the income folder in an envelope, write your two questions on a sheet on top, and drop it off or send it that day. The rest can follow in a second envelope. A preparer would rather get half the papers in March than all of them in a panic in April, and you know it. One rule helps: the questions sheet goes on top, not buried at the bottom. A question nobody sees is a question nobody answers.

Spin for the shred pile

The shred pile is the most satisfying spin on the wheel, and it is also the one people skip. Old statements, duplicate forms, and pay stubs from three years ago take up drawer space and make the current pile look bigger than it is. Load the wheel with statements, duplicates, old pay stubs, junk mail, expired, and keep. Statements means old bank and credit card statements you no longer need. Duplicates means the second copy of a form you already filed. Old pay stubs means stubs from a job you left years ago. Junk mail means the pre-approved offers mixed into the tax envelopes. Expired means anything past the years you need to keep. Keep means a paper stays in the folder for next year.

Spin once, and that is the group you shred or recycle first. The wheel lands on shred, which means you take the old statements to the shredder, or tear them up by hand if you have no shredder, and the drawer gets one inch emptier. One rule matters: shred only the group the wheel picks, and check each sheet for a form number before it goes in. A 1099 mixed into the junk mail is the one paper you will miss, and you already know how that story ends.

Spin for the 30-minute timer

The last spin is about time, not paper. Tax sorting expands to fill the whole day if you let it, and a whole day of sorting ends with a person on the couch at 9 pm, surrounded by folders, with nothing finished. Load the wheel with 30 minutes, one folder, one pile, timer, break, and done. 30 minutes means you set a timer and sort until it rings. One folder means you finish a single folder and stop. One pile means you clear one pile off the table and stop. Timer means you sort in short bursts with a break between each one. Break means you stop now and come back tomorrow. Done means the sorting is finished for the day, and the table is clear enough to eat dinner on.

Spin once, and that is your session. The wheel lands on 30 minutes, which means you set the timer on your phone, sort until it rings, and then put the folders away, even if a pile is left. A half-sorted table you return to tomorrow beats a full-day marathon you quit at noon. Do one spin a day, and the pile that scared you in early April is gone before the deadline week starts.

Try it tonight. Open the Decision Wheel, type in the piles sitting on your table right now, and spin for the first envelope. If the wheel says W-2, open the W-2. If it says shred, shred the old statements. And if you need a second pair of hands for the small calls along the way, the Yes or No Wheel settles the keep-or-toss arguments, and the Random Name Picker picks who in the house takes the shred bag out. One spin is all it takes to start, and starting is the whole job.

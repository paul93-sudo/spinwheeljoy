Holiday Travel Pack Wheel: What Goes in the Carry-On

It is December 18, the flight leaves at 6:15 on Saturday morning, and the bed is covered with wrapped gifts, a coat, snacks, and three chargers. A holiday travel pack wheel settles wrapped gifts or ship ahead, the snack bag, layers or one big coat, plane entertainment, the security-ready pocket, who carries the shared stuff, and what sits on top for the first night.

It is 9:40 on the night of December 18, and Sam and Rosa are staring at their bed. Their flight to Chicago leaves at 6:15 on Saturday morning. Rosa’s parents are waiting at the other end, and the plan was simple: one carry-on each, no checked bag to wait for at midnight in Chicago. The bed does not look simple. Three wrapped gifts sit next to a heavy coat, a bag of snacks, a paperback, two sweaters, and three chargers, and Sam has already said “we can’t take all of this” twice. Rosa has already said “we need all of this” once, and she is right about half of it.

Here’s the thing. Packing for a holiday flight is not one decision. It is ten small ones, and each one turns into a debate because both people have a point. Sam wants the coat on the plane so it takes no bag space. Rosa says a coat on a crowded 6:15 flight ends up stuffed in the overhead bin with someone else’s bag on top of it. Sam wants to wrap the gifts now. Rosa says wrapped gifts in a carry-on get opened by security and rewrapped badly at the airport. Both of them are right, and that is why the bed is still covered at 9:40.

Put it on the Decision Wheel instead. One spin for the gifts. One for the snacks. One for layers. One for the plane entertainment. One for the security pocket. One for who carries the shared stuff. One for what sits on top for the first night. Seven spins, and the bags get zipped before 11:00, because the wheel picks and the packing starts.

Spin for wrapped gifts or ship ahead

Start here, because the gifts decide how much bag space is left for everything else. A wrapped box is bulky, fragile, and awkward in a carry-on. A shipped box costs money and needs a trip to the post office. Both are fine. The argument starts when nobody picks one.

Load the wheel with wrap, ship, bag, wrap there, gift card, and no gifts. Wrap means wrap the gifts at home and carry them on, which is the right pick when the gifts are small and flat, like books and scarves, because flat gifts slide between sweaters and do not eat much space. Ship means send the big box ahead, which suits a gift like a board game or a toy set, because a big box in a carry-on leaves no room for clothes. Bag means gift bags instead of wrapped boxes, which is the right pick when the gifts are odd shapes, because a bag folds flat on the way home and a wrapped box does not. Wrap there means carry the gifts unwrapped and buy paper in Chicago, which suits a trip where the hosts have scissors and tape already, because wrapping at the kitchen table takes twenty minutes and saves a whole bag compartment. Gift card means skip the box entirely, which is the right pick when the gift is for an adult who asked for one thing from one store. No gifts means no gifts on this trip at all, which suits a family that already agreed to skip gifts this year, because a rule everyone agreed to beats a bag full of last-minute boxes.

Spin once, and that is the gift plan. The wheel lands on ship, which means Rosa drops the big box at the post office Friday morning, and the two small gifts ride in the carry-on in gift bags. One rule keeps this spin honest: what ships, ships. Nobody unpacks the shipped box at the last minute because a cousin asked for one more gift. A shipped box that gets reopened is a shipped box that misses Friday.

Spin for the snack bag

The gifts are settled, and now the snack bag needs a call. A 6:15 flight means breakfast at the airport or no breakfast at all, and airport food at 5:30 in the morning is a short menu at a high price. A snack bag packed at home costs little and keeps two hungry travelers from snapping at each other before takeoff.

Load the wheel with nuts, sandwich, fruit, candy, crackers, and coffee. Nuts means a bag of mixed nuts, which is the right pick when the flight is short, because nuts pack small and do not spill. Sandwich means sandwiches made the night before, which suits a flight over two hours, because a sandwich is a real meal and a bag of nuts is not. Fruit means apples and bananas, which is the right pick when the bag already has heavy stuff, because fruit adds no crumbs and no smell. Candy means a small bag of candy, which suits a trip where the travelers want a treat and not a meal, because candy is light and everyone likes a sweet on a plane. Crackers means crackers and cheese, which is the right pick when the flight has no meal service, because crackers travel well and cheese holds up for a few hours. Coffee means skip the food and buy coffee at the gate, which suits a flight under an hour, because a short flight ends before hunger starts.

Spin once, and that is the snack plan. The wheel lands on sandwich, which means Sam makes two sandwiches Friday night, Rosa adds two apples, and the snack bag goes on top of Sam’s carry-on where it is easy to reach. One rule helps: the snack bag is separate from the gift bag. A sandwich next to a gift bag ends with crumbs on a scarf, and nobody wants to hand over a crumb-covered scarf on Christmas Eve.

If the snack debate turns into a dinner debate once you land, the What to Eat Wheel settles that one too. One spin for the first meal in Chicago, and the car ride from the airport has an answer.

Spin for layers or one big coat

Chicago in December is cold. That part is not up for debate. The debate is how to dress for it without wearing half the bag onto the plane. One big coat is warm and takes up a whole overhead bin. Layers are flexible and take up bag space one sweater at a time.

Load the wheel with coat, sweater, hoodie, scarf, socks, and blanket. Coat means the big coat goes on the plane, worn, which is the right pick when the coat is the warmest thing you own, because a worn coat takes zero bag space. Sweater means a sweater plus a light jacket, which suits a trip where the days run from a cold walk to a warm house, because a sweater comes off indoors and a big coat does not. Hoodie means a hoodie and a scarf, which is the right pick when the trip is mostly indoors, because a hoodie is enough for short walks between cars and doors. Scarf means a scarf, gloves, and a hat over a light jacket, which suits a traveler who runs warm, because small warm pieces pack flat and a big coat does not. Socks means warm socks and a second pair, which is the right pick when the shoes are thin, because cold feet ruin a walk faster than a cold jacket. Blanket means a travel blanket for the plane, which suits a traveler who gets cold on flights, because a plane blanket doubles as a couch blanket at the parents’ house.

Spin once, and that is the layer plan. The wheel lands on sweater, which means Rosa packs two sweaters and a light jacket, Sam wears his coat onto the plane, and the heavy coat stays home. One rule helps: wear the bulkiest thing you take. A coat that rides in the bag is a coat that eats the space the sweaters needed.

Spin for what keeps you busy on the plane

The bags are nearly packed, and now someone asks what to do for three hours in the air. This sounds small until one traveler has a book and the other has nothing, and then the one with nothing talks for three hours and the one with the book gets no reading done. I’ll be honest, this is the spin Sam cares about most, because last year he forgot his book and spent the flight reading the safety card twice.

Load the wheel with book, podcast, cards, tablet, crossword, and sleep. Book means a paperback, which is the right pick when the traveler wants no battery and no noise, because a paperback works from takeoff to landing with no charging. Podcast means downloaded episodes, which suits a traveler who gets motion sick reading, because listening needs no eyes on a page. Cards means a deck of cards, which is the right pick when two people travel together, because a card game fills a flight faster than two people staring out the window. Tablet means a tablet with shows downloaded, which suits a traveler who wants a screen and a choice, because a tablet holds a book, shows, and music in one flat case. Crossword means a crossword book and a pen, which is the right pick when the traveler wants quiet and a puzzle, because a crossword takes no battery and no signal. Sleep means an eye mask and earplugs, which suits a 6:15 flight, because a traveler who slept badly the night before packing will sleep on the plane if the light and noise stay out.

Spin once, and that is the plane plan. The wheel lands on book, which means Rosa’s paperback goes in the seat pocket, Sam downloads two podcast episodes as backup, and the cards go in Sam’s coat pocket for the wait at the gate. One rule helps: the backup counts. A book plus a podcast beats a book alone, because a book alone ends when the reader hits a dull chapter at 30,000 feet.

Spin for the security-ready pocket

Now the spin that saves the most time at the airport. Everyone has been through security. Everyone knows the drill. And every single trip, someone in line is digging through a bag for a liquids bag while the line waits. Sam did it last year. He left his phone charger in his checked bag on a different trip, and spent the first day in Chicago borrowing Rosa’s charger every hour. That is the thing everyone forgets: the charger goes in the carry-on, not the checked bag, and not on the kitchen counter at home.

Load the wheel with liquids, laptop, shoes, pockets, charger, and ID. Liquids means the liquids bag goes on top of the carry-on, which is the right pick when the bag is packed tight, because a liquids bag buried at the bottom means unpacking half the bag in line. Laptop means the laptop goes in its own sleeve on top, which suits a traveler who works on the plane, because a laptop at the bottom of a bag is a laptop that holds up the line. Shoes means slip-on shoes for the airport, which is the right pick when the traveler hates untying boots in line, because slip-on shoes go back on in ten seconds. Pockets means empty pockets before the line, which suits a traveler with keys and coins, because empty pockets mean no beeps and no second walk through. Charger means the phone charger goes in the carry-on side pocket, which is the right pick for every trip, because a phone with no charge at landing is a phone that cannot call for a ride. ID means the ID goes in the coat pocket, which suits a traveler who keeps losing the ID inside the bag, because a coat pocket is one place and a bag has six.

Spin once, and that is the security plan. The wheel lands on charger, which means Sam’s charger goes in the side pocket of his carry-on tonight, Rosa’s charger goes in hers, and both chargers get checked before the bags zip. One rule helps: the side pocket is for the charger and nothing else. A side pocket full of snacks and receipts is a side pocket where the charger hides until Chicago.

Spin for who carries the shared stuff

The bags are packed, and now the shared stuff needs owners. Snacks, gifts, chargers, documents, coats, toiletries: all of it belongs to both travelers, which means it belongs to nobody until someone claims it. The argument here is never about weight. It is about who gets stuck with the heavy bag while the other person walks free with a light one.

Load the wheel with Sam, Rosa, split, Sam there, Rosa there, and rotate. Sam means Sam carries the shared bag on this trip, which is the right pick when Rosa already carries the gifts, because one shared bag per person is fair and two shared bags on one person is not. Rosa means Rosa carries it, which suits a trip where Sam carries the coats, because coats in hand plus a shared bag is too much for one person. Split means split the shared stuff between both bags, which is the right pick for most trips, because a split load means neither bag gets too heavy and neither traveler walks free. Sam there means Sam carries it to Chicago and Rosa carries it home, which suits a trip where the shared stuff shrinks, because gifts given away in Chicago mean a lighter bag on the way home. Rosa there means the reverse, which is the right pick when Sam’s bag is already full on the way out. Rotate means swap the shared bag at the gate, which suits a trip with a long layover, because a bag that feels light at takeoff feels heavy after two hours in an airport.

Spin once, and that is the carry plan. The wheel lands on split, which means Sam takes the snacks and the chargers, Rosa takes the gifts and the documents, and the coats go on their owners. One rule helps: the split gets written on a note in Sam’s phone. A split that lives only in someone’s head gets re-debated at the gate, and the gate is a bad place for a debate.

If the room cannot agree who counts as the carrier for the first leg, settle it with the Random Name Picker first. One draw for the shared bag, one spin for the split, and the walk to the gate has no argument in it.

Spin for the first-night bag

The flight lands at midnight in Chicago. Rosa’s parents are asleep. The guests get the spare room, and the bags go in the corner. This is the moment a bad pack shows up: pajamas at the bottom, toothbrush inside a toiletry bag inside a shoe bag, phone charger somewhere under the sweaters. Nobody wants to unpack a whole carry-on at midnight while trying not to wake the house.

Load the wheel with pajamas, toothbrush, charger, book, socks, and snack. Pajamas means pajamas sit on top of the carry-on, which is the right pick when the arrival is late, because pajamas on top mean no digging at midnight. Toothbrush means the toothbrush and toothpaste sit on top, which suits a traveler who brushes before bed no matter what, because a toothbrush at the bottom means a skipped brush and a bad morning. Charger means the charger sits on top, which is the right pick when the phone is low after the flight, because a phone that dies at midnight cannot set a morning alarm. Book means the book sits on top, which suits a traveler who reads before sleep, because a book on top means reading without unpacking. Socks means a fresh pair of socks sits on top, which is the right pick when the flight socks feel gross, because fresh socks at midnight feel like a small reset. Snack means a snack sits on top, which suits a traveler who lands hungry, because a snack at midnight beats a trip to a dark kitchen in a strange house.

Spin once, and that is the top layer. The wheel lands on pajamas, which means Rosa’s pajamas go on top of her bag, Sam’s go on top of his, and the toothbrushes sit right under them. One rule helps: the top layer gets packed last and stays on top. A top layer that gets buried under a last-minute sweater is a top layer that does not exist at midnight.

Try the holiday travel pack wheel

It is 10:50 on the night of December 18, and the bed is clear. The big gift box is ready for the post office Friday morning. The sandwiches are in the fridge. Rosa’s sweaters are packed, Sam’s coat is by the door, the paperback is in the seat pocket, both chargers are in side pockets, the shared stuff is split, and the pajamas sit on top of both bags. The trip still has a 6:15 flight and a cold walk in Chicago, because it is December and that part does not change. The packing just stopped being the hard part.

Try it on the Decision Wheel the night before you fly. Load the gifts, spin for the snacks, and write the split on a note in your phone. Sounds silly, works great. The bags zip, the line at security moves, and the first night ends with pajamas on and lights out, which is more than most holiday arrivals manage.

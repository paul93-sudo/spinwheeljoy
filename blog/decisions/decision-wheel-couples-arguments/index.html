Decision Wheel for Couples: 8 Small Arguments to Outsource

The thermostat, the dishes, whose turn it is to drive. Hand the eight smallest couple arguments to the Decision Wheel and get your evenings back.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 18, 2026 Updated October 18, 2026

  7. Whose family we visit
  8. The last slice
  9. How to run the wheel so nobody disputes it

It’s 9:40pm on a Tuesday. The kitchen is clean except for the one pan nobody wants to scrub, the one with the baked-on rice. My wife and I are both staring at it from the couch like it’s a wild animal. Not angry. Just tired. Neither of us wants to lose this one, because losing tonight means losing it every night forever. That’s the stakes in a marriage: every tiny negotiation sets a precedent.

We have solved this. We put our names on the Decision Wheel and spin. The wheel doesn’t get tired, doesn’t remember who did it last time, and can’t be guilted. Two minutes later the pan is soaking and we’re watching a show. No resentment. The wheel took the hit.

Couples don’t fight about big things most nights. They fight about eight things, over and over. Here’s our list, and how the spin settled each one.

The thermostat

He wants 72. She wants 68. Every house has some version of this, and it goes on for decades. Nobody ever wins the thermostat argument through debate. The numbers are just preferences, and preferences don’t have counterarguments.

Our deal: when we disagree, the wheel decides the setting for that night. The segments are 68, 69, 70, 71, 72. Winner gets their number, loser gets a blanket. What changed wasn’t the temperature. It was the ten minutes of sighing and dramatic shivering that used to come with it. I’ll be honest, I now own two hoodies I didn’t need before marriage, and the wheel is the reason I wear them.

Who scrubs the pan

The cook doesn’t clean. That’s the rule in every house, right? Except then nobody cooks, because cooking is the penalty and cleaning is the reward. Our version: whoever didn’t cook does the dishes. But some nights neither of us cooked. Leftovers night. Takeout night. The pan night.

Names on the wheel, one spin, done. The loser can’t negotiate. The rule that makes it work: the spin happens before dessert, not after. After dessert, motivation is zero and the negotiation gets lawyerly. Before dessert, the loser at least gets to sulk with ice cream.

What to watch

This one eats twenty minutes a night in most couples I know. He scrolls. She vetoes. She scrolls. He falls asleep on the couch. Then it’s 10:30 and you’re watching a true-crime episode you both sort of hate because choosing felt like work.

Each of us puts one thing on the wheel. Only the rule: it has to be something you’d genuinely watch tonight, not your pick for greatest film of all time and not a joke nomination. One spin. We watch whatever lands for twenty minutes, no complaining. If it doesn’t hook us by then, the wheel picks again. We’ve finished three shows this month we’d never have picked on our own, and two of them we loved.

For date-night picks specifically, the Date Idea Wheel does the same job with going-out presets.

The takeout deadlock

Pizza versus Thai. Sushi versus burgers. Every couple has a rotation of about four places and a running score of who picked last. The problem isn’t the food. It’s that whoever picks absorbs the blame if the food is bad.

Wheel fixes this perfectly. All the regular places go on as segments. The loser is whoever’s pick won last time: their favorite is off the wheel for one round. That keeps us from ordering from the same Thai place eleven Fridays in a row, which is what would happen without the rule. The wheel picked Ethiopian last month. I didn’t know I liked Ethiopian. Turns out I do.

Who drives on the road trip

Short drives, someone always volunteers. Long drives, nobody volunteers, and you end up with a tense silence about seventy minutes into a four-hour drive, followed by the world’s smallest argument about directions. We’ve been married long enough to know our pattern: he drives the first leg and then gets drowsy, she takes over and gets grumpy about it.

Now the route goes on the wheel in legs. His name, her name, his name, her name, or however we split the miles. The spin decides leg one, and the pattern alternates from there. No performance of generosity, no martyrdom. I can’t overstate how much this removed from our marriage. It’s four seconds of spinning versus four hours of negotiating from the passenger seat.

Weekend chores

Saturday morning. The store run, the laundry, the thing that needs returning to the other side of town, the sink that’s somehow full again. Couples who split chores by “whoever notices” end up with one person noticing everything and one person noticing nothing. Couples who split by list end up auditing each other, which is worse.

Chores on the wheel, names on the segments. Saturday at 9am, we spin for each job. The spin doesn’t care who noticed what. The job is just assigned, randomly, and it’s over. One adjustment we made: big jobs count double. A spin win on “mow the lawn” excuses you from the next small job. Keeps it fair without a spreadsheet.

Whose family we visit

This is the only item on this list with real emotional weight, so it needs a caveat. The wheel doesn’t decide your relationship with your in-laws. It doesn’t fix feelings. What it does is handle the logistics of a month where both families want you on the same Saturday.

Each family’s event goes on the wheel. One spin decides this Saturday; the other family gets the next open Saturday, no spin needed. The unspoken value: nobody is the person who chose their own parents. The wheel chose. I’ve watched this defuse exactly the kind of simmering thing that used to last all weekend. It’s not therapy. It’s a buffer. Sometimes a buffer is enough.

The last slice

Every couple has a last-slice treaty, spoken or unspoken. Ours was unspoken, which is why it broke down: she assumed I’d offer, I assumed she’d take half. Now the rule is written down, literally, on the fridge. Last slice goes on the wheel. Or split it. Splitting is also allowed, and honestly splitting is what we do most of the time. But when neither of us wants to be the generous one, the wheel is. It takes two seconds and it always, always gets a laugh.

How to run the wheel so nobody disputes it

Two house rules keep the whole system honest. First: the segments are locked before the spin. No editing after it lands. Everyone has seen this move, and it ends marriages faster than any argument. Second: the loser can’t appeal to a re-spin, ever. If you allow one appeal, every spin becomes two spins, and the wheel becomes a prop in the same argument you were trying to skip.

Set it up once on the Decision Wheel with your eight debates saved as presets. For the yes-no ones that come up mid-week (should we go to the party, should we buy the thing), the Yes or No Wheel handles those in one spin. The point was never the wheel. The point is that the argument ends in four seconds, and the evening is yours again. Try it tonight. Put the pan on there.

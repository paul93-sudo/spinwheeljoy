Decision Wheel for Kids: 10 Choices Worth Handing Over

Kids argue over everything: who goes first, what to wear, which story, what's for dinner. Hand these 10 decisions to a decision wheel and get your mornings back.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 17, 2026 Updated October 17, 2026

  2. What to wear
  3. Which bedtime story
  4. What’s for dinner (the side, not the meal)

  8. Movie night pick

It’s 7:15 on a Tuesday morning, and you are already behind. Your six-year-old is crying actual tears because her brother got the blue cup and she got the green one, even though the cups are identical except for the color. Your four-year-old has changed his shirt twice and rejected both. Nobody has eaten breakfast. You have eleven minutes to get three people out the door.

I lived this exact morning for most of a school year. The thing that finally fixed it was not better discipline or a laminated routine chart. It was handing the small decisions to a Decision Wheel.

Here’s the idea: you load the wheel with the choices you’re willing to live with, and the kids spin for the rest. They stop arguing with you and start arguing with the wheel, which is great, because the wheel does not care and it cannot be guilt-tripped. A spun decision feels fair in a way that “because I said so” never will. Kids can smell the difference from across the room.

Below are the ten decisions I handed over, roughly in the order they saved my sanity.

Who goes first

Board game night. Bike rides. The swing at the park. Who goes first has started more sibling wars than any actual disagreement in my house. Load both names on the wheel, let the loser of last round do the spinning, and the matter is settled in six seconds.

The trick is that the loser spins. Handing the spin to the kid who lost last time turns the whole thing into a tiny ceremony instead of a power grab. My son Leo, age seven, takes this job so seriously that he announces the result like a referee.

One rule: don’t put your own name on the wheel to break a kid-versus-kid tie. They notice. Use a Random Name Picker if you need a neutral third party.

What to wear

The clothing fight is not really about clothes. It’s about control. So give them real control inside a boundary: load the wheel with four outfits you already approved, laid out the night before, weather-checked by you.

Mornings went like this in our house: I picked four options Sunday night, hung them on the closet door, typed them into the wheel. Red shirt and jeans. Dinosaur tee and shorts. Blue dress with leggings. Soccer jersey and sweats. Each morning Maya spun, and whatever came up, she wore. The first week she spun the dinosaur tee three days running and wore it happily. By week three the arguments had dropped to roughly zero, because arguing with a spinning wheel is embarrassing even at six.

One caution: don’t sneak in an outfit you’d veto anyway. If it’s on the wheel, it’s a real option. Kids check.

Which bedtime story

Bedtime negotiations eat thirty minutes a night in a lot of houses. The wheel cuts it in half because the negotiation happens once, on Sunday, instead of nightly. Sit down with the kids and the stack of books. Let them each pick two. Those four go on the wheel. Each night, one spin, one book, no swaps.

I’ll be honest: the first week, they tested it. “But I wanted the dragon one.” You spin again tomorrow, and the dragon one is still on there. Within a week they stopped asking for swaps, because the wheel’s answer is final in a way that a tired parent’s answer never is. Also, a surprise: my kids started requesting re-reads of books they’d ignored for months, just because the wheel picked them. Randomness makes old books feel new.

What’s for dinner (the side, not the meal)

You still decide the main dish. That is not up for a vote, and the wheel knows it. But the side dish, the vegetable, the topping? Hand it over.

Our wheel holds: corn, broccoli with cheese, apple slices, cucumber, carrot sticks, peas. Whatever spins, that’s the side, no substitutions. Here’s the thing that surprised me: Maya started eating roasted broccoli, which she had previously treated as a personal insult, because the wheel picked it and therefore it was an adventure instead of a command. I did not plan that. It just worked, three times in four weeks, and now broccoli is a normal food in this house.

Keep the options to things you’re genuinely serving anyway. The wheel is a delivery system, not a restaurant.

Which park on Saturday

Saturday mornings used to start with a twenty-minute debate about which park, during which the actual morning burned away. Now there’s a wheel with the four parks within driving distance, each with its real trade-off written under it: Riverside has swings but no shade. Hilltop has the big slide and a fifteen-minute drive.

One spin, everyone in the car by 9:30. If the weather looks wrong for the winner, that’s a yes-or-no call, not a new debate: keep it or re-spin once. When we need that call, I use the Yes or No Wheel so even the re-spin decision stays out of my hands.

Who picks the snack

After school, two kids, one snack cabinet, endless lobbying. The wheel holds five snacks you already bought and approved. Whoever’s day it is on the family calendar spins; the other kid picks tomorrow.

We tried alternating days without the wheel first. It failed, because “it’s my day” is a claim and a wheel result is a fact. Nobody appeals a fact. The other thing that changed: they stopped asking for the snacks that aren’t on the wheel, because asking me for goldfish crackers when the wheel says pretzels has never once worked.

Chore order

Not which chores. Which order. Big difference.

Seven-year-olds will happily do three chores if they spin the order, and will stage a full protest if you assign it. Our chore wheel holds: clear the table, feed the cat, wipe the counters, sort the laundry basket. One spin stacks all four into a random order. Leo does his in wheel order like he’s following a quest log. The random order also means nobody always gets stuck with the last, worst chore. Fairness they can see beats fairness you promise.

Movie night pick

Friday movie night used to run like this: forty minutes of trailers, two vetoes each, and a final pick nobody was happy about. The wheel holds one movie per family member, chosen earlier in the week, and the Friday spin is final. Vetoes are gone. You got your pick on the wheel; the wheel chose.

Here’s the honest part: the first time the wheel picked my husband’s documentary about bridges, the kids groaned. They watched it anyway. They now know an embarrassing amount about suspension bridges, and it took twenty minutes less than our old negotiation phase. Sounds silly, works great.

Screen-time choice

The iPad is where my “five more minutes” negotiations lived. Now the wheel holds the four approved games, one spin picks the game, and the timer starts when the spin stops. The end of the timer is the end of the timer, but nobody fights the game choice anymore, because they picked it through the wheel, which counts.

If two kids share one device, spin twice: once for the game, once for who plays first. Two spins, two settled questions, zero lobbying.

The one thing you still decide

This matters as much as the list. Some decisions stay with you: bedtime itself, whether homework is done, safety rules, the doctor’s instructions. The wheel works because the kids trust its boundaries. The week I let Maya spin “which bedtime” to push it later, the whole system wobbled for days. She was six and she clocked the inconsistency immediately.

So the rule in our house: the wheel only spins on things I’d say yes to anyway. Everything on it is pre-approved. That’s why they trust it. And that’s why, when I say “this one isn’t a wheel decision,” they accept it. Mostly.

Pick three from this list tonight. Load them on the Decision Wheel, hand your oldest the spin, and watch the next argument evaporate in six seconds. Then tell me which one saved your morning. I’d bet on the outfits.

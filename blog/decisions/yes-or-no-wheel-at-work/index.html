Yes or No Wheel at Work: 9 Small Decisions to Stop Debating

The office runs on tiny yes-or-no questions nobody wants to own. Put these nine small work decisions on the Yes or No Wheel and get your afternoon back.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 18, 2026 Updated October 18, 2026

On this page

  1. Ship it today or hold it until tomorrow
  2. Order lunch in or go out
  3. Take the meeting or send your regrets
  4. Reply now or reply Monday
  5. The thermostat question
  6. Take the window seat or pass
  7. Fix it or buy the new one
  8. Work from home today or come in
  9. Wrap it Friday or leave it for Monday
  10. One rule for all nine

It’s 3:47 on a Wednesday. My coworker Dave leans over my desk and asks whether we should ship the client update today or hold it until tomorrow. Both of us know there’s no real answer. Today is slightly risky. Tomorrow is slightly late. Nobody at this company will remember which one we picked, but we’ll burn the next forty minutes picking it anyway. That is what office life actually runs on: tiny decisions that feel load-bearing and aren’t.

I started spinning for these. The Yes or No Wheel lives in a tab on my browser now, right next to the ticketing system. Here’s the thing: nobody trusts a coin flip at work. A coin flip feels flippant. A wheel feels like a process. Same odds, completely different meeting.

Nine questions that get a spin instead of a debate in my office:

Ship it today or hold it until tomorrow

Every team has a Dave. Mine emails the client update, then hovers, then recalls it, then asks if the timing was right. The update itself takes ten minutes. The dithering takes two days.

My rule now: if the facts are in and the only question left is timing, the wheel answers it in three seconds. YES means it goes out today. NO means tomorrow morning, no exceptions, no reopening it at 11pm. Sounds silly, works great. The unspoken benefit: nobody owns the call, so nobody second-guesses it. The wheel decided. Move on.

Order lunch in or go out

This one eats thirty minutes a day in most offices. Someone suggests ordering in. Someone else says they need air. A third person says they’re broke. Nobody wants to be the one who picks wrong, because lunch picks carry a surprising amount of social weight.

We spin at 11:30. YES, we order in. NO, we walk somewhere. One rule keeps it fair: the spin happens before anyone starts campaigning, or the whole thing turns into a lobbying exercise with salads. The best part is what the spin does to your gut. If YES lands and you feel disappointed, congratulations: you actually wanted to go out. The wheel gives you the answer by showing you the one you were hoping for.

For the harder version of this debate, when the office can’t even agree on what to order, the What to Eat Wheel puts the restaurants themselves on the segments.

Take the meeting or send your regrets

The invite says “quick sync, no agenda.” Your afternoon is already full. You spend fifteen minutes weighing the social cost of declining against the time cost of attending, which is more time than the meeting would take.

Spin for it. YES means you accept and show up on time, no laptop multitasking. NO means you decline with a short note: “Can’t make this one, catch me in the recap.” The honest part nobody admits: about half the time, YES lands and you feel relieved. You wanted to go. You just didn’t want to choose to go. The spin gives you permission without the FOMO.

One caution. Don’t spin your way out of meetings your boss scheduled personally. The wheel is a decision tool, not a career move. If there’s a power dynamic in the room, you already know the answer.

Reply now or reply Monday

It’s 4:55 on a Friday. The email from the vendor isn’t urgent, but it’ll sit in your head all weekend like a tab you forgot to close. Replying takes five minutes. Not replying saves five minutes but costs you the entire weekend of low-grade guilt.

I’ll be honest, this one changed my weekends more than anything else on this list. Spin: YES means you answer it now, properly, and close the laptop for real. NO means you write a two-line holding reply (“Got it, I’ll dig in Monday”) and the inbox stays closed. The trick is that the spin commits you. No more “I’ll decide after dinner.” You decided at 4:55. Done.

The thermostat question

Every office has a thermostat war and nobody wins it. Facilities sets it to 71. Half the floor wears hoodies indoors in June. Someone tapes a paper sign over the thermostat that says DO NOT TOUCH in all caps, like that ever worked.

Here’s the fix our floor actually uses: once a day, if the room feels wrong, one spin. YES means we bump it one degree warmer. NO means one degree cooler, or it stays put. One degree. That’s it. The number barely matters. What matters is the argument never happens, because nobody can argue with a wheel. Arguing with the wheel is like arguing with the weather.

Take the window seat or pass

Two desks open up near the windows. Four people want them. Someone seniority-brags. Someone else guilt-trips about their eyesight. The rest of the team watches the whole thing like it’s a sport they didn’t buy tickets for.

Put YES and NO on the wheel and let each person spin in turn. First YES gets first pick of the two seats, second YES gets the other. NO means you stay where you are and the queue moves on. Three spins, ninety seconds, zero resentment. Last quarter we did this for the corner seats and the losers forgot about it by lunch. That’s the actual product here: decisions people forget by lunch.

Fix it or buy the new one

The office printer jams every third job. Facilities says it can be serviced. The service call costs almost as much as a new printer, and the new one comes with a warranty and a working paper tray. This meeting has happened at every company on earth, and it always ends with “let’s give it one more month.”

Spin. YES means the service call gets booked this week. NO means the printer gets replaced. The wheel is doing one specific job here: it kills the middle option. The middle option is always “discuss it again next month,” and the middle option is why the printer has been jamming since spring. Small budgets die from a thousand delays, not one bad purchase.

Work from home today or come in

Hybrid schedules created a brand-new decision to agonize over: which days do you come in? If your team doesn’t mandate specific days, you spend Sunday night optimizing commute, weather, meeting density, and whether the good snacks are stocked.

If it’s genuinely optional, let the wheel pick the day. YES means you show up. NO means the laptop stays home. Two rules make this work at scale: the team spins together so the office isn’t empty by accident, and client-facing days are exempt from spinning. You don’t leave the sales demo to chance. But that random Tuesday with four optional standups? The wheel can handle Tuesday.

Need to pick who takes notes when you’re all in the room? The Random Name Picker handles the “not it” jobs with the same fairness.

Wrap it Friday or leave it for Monday

The task is 80 percent done at 4pm Friday. Finishing means an hour of overtime. Leaving it means three days of background worry plus the dreaded Monday morning context-rebuild.

I used to agonize over this weekly. Then I started spinning and the answer became obvious: the spin doesn’t matter, the commitment does. YES means you finish it now, close the tab, and the weekend is yours. NO means you write yourself a one-paragraph handoff note, set a Monday reminder, and close the laptop guilt-free. The handoff note is the whole trick. Without it, NO is just procrastination with a spin.

One rule for all nine

The wheel only works if you agree beforehand that the spin is final. That’s the entire system. Every spin above has the same fine print: decide the question, decide the two options, spin once, do what it says. If you let people re-spin “because that one didn’t count,” you’ve just built a very slow coin.

Start with the smallest one. Friday afternoon, 4pm, inbox staring at you. Spin for the email. You’ll wonder why you ever spent twenty minutes on it.

Try the Yes or No Wheel on your next office debate and see how fast the meeting ends.

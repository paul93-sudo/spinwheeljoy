Coffee Run Wheel: Decide Who Grabs the Office Order

One person always ends up on the coffee run. Spin a coffee run wheel once a week and settle the runner, the orders, and who pays, without another Slack thread.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 26, 2026 Updated October 26, 2026

On this page

  1. Spin once on Monday, and the week is settled
  2. The runner should not also be the order-taker
  3. Make the no-repeat rule real
  4. Part-timers and remote days get a fair slice
  5. Settle who pays before the first run
  6. Put a cutoff on orders
  7. No exemptions, including the boss
  8. Try the coffee run wheel

It was 9:40 on a Tuesday when the coffee run standoff started in our office. Someone dropped a message in the group chat: “coffee run?” Fourteen people read it. Nobody answered for six minutes. Then Maya typed “I can go” and three people reacted with a thumbs-up, which is office code for “thank goodness it is not me.”

Maya had gone the day before, too. And the Friday before that. She was not the office manager. She was not the newest hire. She was just the person who hated watching a simple question hang in the air, so she kept volunteering, and the office kept letting her.

Here’s the thing: nobody in that office was selfish. The run just had no system. Every trip was a fresh negotiation about who goes, who pays, and whose oat milk latte gets forgotten. A system fixes all three at once, and the simplest one I have seen work is a wheel with everyone’s name on it.

Load the team into the Random Name Picker, spin it, and the wheel picks the runner. That is the whole idea. The rest of this post is about the small rules that keep it fair once the novelty wears off.

Spin once on Monday, and the week is settled

The best version of this runs weekly, not daily. On Monday morning, spin for each workday and post the five names on the break-room whiteboard: Monday Dan, Tuesday Priya, Wednesday Tom, Thursday Ana, Friday Luis. Done. Nobody has to ask “who goes today” again that week.

Weekly spins beat daily spins for one reason: the runner can plan. Tom knows on Monday that he goes Wednesday, so he brings his reusable cup and leaves his laptop charger at his desk. A daily surprise spin at 9:30 sends someone out the door mid-task, and the trip feels like an interruption instead of a turn.

Post the list where everyone can see it. A whiteboard, a pinned chat message, a printout by the coffee machine. The list is the system. If it lives only in one person’s head, it dies the first time that person calls in sick.

The runner should not also be the order-taker

Here is where most office coffee runs fall apart. The runner walks to the counter with seven orders in their head, forgets one, and comes back to a coworker who says “you forgot my decaf” in a tone that suggests a crime was committed.

Split the jobs. The wheel picks the runner. A shared list collects the orders. The runner reads the list at the counter, not from memory.

Keep the list dead simple. A shared note, a chat thread, a sheet of paper by the machine. Each person writes their name and their exact order: “Priya, large oat latte, one sugar.” No shorthand, no “the usual.” The usual changes, and the runner is the one who pays for the guess.

I’ll be honest, this rule sounds fussy until the first time a runner comes back with every order right. After that, nobody argues about it.

Make the no-repeat rule real

A wheel that can land on Maya three weeks in a row is not a fair wheel, it is a lottery. Turn on the no-repeat setting, or cross names off the list by hand as they go. Everyone goes once before anyone goes twice. That is the rule that makes people trust the system.

This matters more in small offices. With fourteen people, a straight random spin will repeat someone within the first month. People notice. They keep count, even when they say they don’t. A rotation where every name comes up once per round feels fair because it is fair.

When a round finishes, start a new one. Shuffle the order. The second round should use a fresh order, or the Monday person stays the Monday person all year.

Part-timers and remote days get a fair slice

Every office has someone the simple version leaves out. Ana works Monday through Wednesday. Luis works from home on Fridays. If the wheel spins all fourteen names for a Friday run, Luis might get picked on a day he is not in the building, and then someone has to cover for him, and the whole system starts to wobble.

Fix it by spinning only the names of people who are actually in the office that day. On Friday, Luis is off the wheel. On Monday, Ana is on it. The list changes with the roster, and nobody gets picked for a trip they cannot take.

Remote workers can still join on delivery days. If the office orders in instead of walking out, spin the full team and let the picked person place the delivery order and split the bill. Same wheel, same fairness, no commute required.

Settle who pays before the first run

The runner question gets all the attention, but the money question causes more quiet resentment. Someone fronts forty dollars, three people forget to pay them back, and by the end of the month that person is out sixty bucks and annoyed at everyone.

Pick one payment rule and write it next to the runner list. The version that works best in most offices: the runner pays at the counter, and everyone sends their share the same day, before lunch. Same day, no exceptions. Money owed overnight turns into money forgotten.

Some offices use a kitty instead. Everyone drops twenty dollars in at the start of the month, and the runner pays from the kitty. That works too, as long as one person tracks the balance and says out loud when it runs low. The rule itself matters less than the fact that everyone agreed to it in advance.

Whatever you pick, do not let the runner also be the debt collector. Chasing five people for four dollars each is a second job, and it is the job that makes people stop volunteering.

Put a cutoff on orders

A coffee run without a cutoff never leaves. The runner is at the door with their coat on, and someone shouts “wait, add one for me” from the bathroom. Then another person changes their order from hot to iced. Then the runner is standing in line texting the group about whether the seasonal drink is still on the menu.

Set a cutoff time and hold it. Orders close at 9:30. The runner leaves at 9:45. An order that arrives at 9:42 waits until tomorrow. Sounds strict. Works great.

The cutoff also protects the runner’s morning. A trip that leaves on time takes twenty minutes. A trip that absorbs five late changes takes forty, and the runner comes back to a full inbox and a cold coffee of their own.

No exemptions, including the boss

This is the rule that decides whether the wheel survives its first month. If managers are exempt, the wheel is not a fair system, it is a chore chart for everyone below a certain pay grade. People can tell the difference, and they stop trusting the spin.

Put every name on the wheel, including the team lead and the office manager. When the boss goes on a run, something useful happens: the team sees the system apply to everyone, and the boss hears the actual coffee orders, which is a small but real reminder of who works for them.

One exception is fine: someone with a genuine mobility issue or a medical reason to skip the walk should be off the runner wheel and on the order list like everyone else. That still counts as fair: the person orders like everyone else, they just do not walk the route. Say so openly, so it never looks like a quiet favor.

Try the coffee run wheel

Start small. Load your team into the Random Name Picker, spin for this week, and pin the list where everyone can see it. Give it two weeks. If Maya stops being the default runner, the wheel did its job.

And if your office has a different recurring errand with the same problem, the same trick works there too. The Team Generator can split a bigger group into lunch crews, and the Icebreaker Wheel can pick the question when the new hire joins the table. But the coffee run is the place to start, because it is the chore everyone already argues about.

Spin it on Monday. Post the list. Let the wheel take the blame when it lands on the boss.

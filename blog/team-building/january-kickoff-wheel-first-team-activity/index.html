January Kickoff Wheel: First Team Activity of the Year

Skip the two-hour January speech. Spin a kickoff wheel on the first Monday back and pick one short team activity, a tour, or a mixed lunch that people will actually enjoy.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 26, 2026 Updated October 26, 2026

On this page

  1. Pick the first activity before the holidays end
  2. Trivia first, speech later
  3. Give new faces a tour, not a quiz
  4. Mix the lunch tables on purpose
  5. A short walk counts, weather permitting
  6. Keep it under 45 minutes
  7. Hand next January to someone else

It was 9:05 on Monday, January 5, and twelve people were back in the office after two weeks off. Luis, the team lead, had booked the big conference room for a two-hour session. The agenda said “vision and priorities.” Nobody had read past the first line. Two people were still holding travel mugs. One person was quietly checking whether the meeting invite had an end time. It did. It was 11:00.

Here’s the thing: Luis did not plan a bad meeting on purpose. He wanted the year to start with energy. A two-hour speech just does the opposite. People sit, they nod, and they count down to lunch. By 10:30, half the room has stopped listening, and the other half is thinking about the emails that piled up while they were away.

A wheel fixes the shape of the morning. You load six short activities, spin once, and the team does the one that lands. One activity. Forty-five minutes, tops. Then people get back to real work with one shared story from the first day back.

Load the six activities into the Icebreaker Wheel, spin it in front of the team, and let the result stand. The rest of this post is about picking activities that work in January, and about the small rules that keep a kickoff from turning into another long meeting.

Pick the first activity before the holidays end

Do not wait until January 5 to decide what the kickoff will be. Send the wheel link in the last work message before the break, with the six options listed. People come back knowing the morning holds one short activity, and they do not spend the first hour wondering when the real work starts.

Six options is about right. Fewer than four, and the spin feels rigged. More than eight, and half the options are filler that nobody would pick on purpose. Put one option from each kind of activity on the wheel: one quiz-style game, one tour, one lunch, one outdoor option, one building task, and one food task. That way the spin can land anywhere and the morning still works.

Write the date and the start time on the invite, and keep both. “Monday, January 5, 10:00, conference room B.” A kickoff that starts at 10:00 and drifts to 10:20 has already told the team that January will run late.

Trivia first, speech later

Trivia works on the first day back because it asks for almost nothing. Nobody has to share a personal story. Nobody has to perform. A question like “Which teammate started here in January?” gets the room talking in under a minute, and the team that answers first gets a small prize, like first pick of the good conference room chairs.

Keep the questions about the team and the office, and keep them kind. “Who has the longest commute?” is a fine question if the answer is a number. “Who messed up the holiday party playlist?” is not a question. It is a trap, and the person it lands on will remember it all year.

Five questions is enough. Ten questions turns trivia into a test, and someone will start checking their phone by question seven. Stop at five, name a winning table, and move on. If Luis still wants to share priorities for the year, he can do it in ten minutes after the game, while people are still awake.

Give new faces a tour, not a quiz

Every January team has someone who started in December and spent the holidays half-remembering where the bathrooms are. A tour is a better welcome than a quiz about office facts they never had a chance to learn.

Spin the wheel with tour stops instead of activities, if the team is small. Put the stops on the wheel: the kitchen, the quiet room, the supply closet, the good coffee machine, the desk of the person who knows how the printer works. The new hire spins, the group walks to that stop, and someone who actually uses that spot explains it in two sentences.

One rule matters here. The guide should be a teammate, and the guide should not quiz the new person afterward. “Where is the quiet room?” asked as a test on day one just makes a new hire nervous. A tour works when the new person leaves knowing three places and three names. That is a good first morning.

If your team works remotely, run the same idea on video. One person shares their screen and walks the group through the shared drive, the chat channels, and the one document every new hire needs first. Fifteen minutes, no quiz, and the new hire has a map instead of a list of links.

Mix the lunch tables on purpose

Lunch is where a kickoff either builds a team or rebuilds the same four cliques. Left alone, people sit with the three coworkers they already know. That is comfortable, and it wastes the one day when everyone is in a mood to meet someone new.

Use the Team Generator to split the team into mixed lunch tables before the morning starts. Put one person from each department at each table, and put new hires at tables with people who have been around long enough to answer questions. Print the table list, or post it in chat, so nobody has to wander the lunchroom looking lost.

Give each table one question to answer over lunch. Keep it light: “What is one thing you want to learn this year?” or “What was the best meal you had over the break?” One question gives strangers something to say after the hellos run out. Two or three questions turn lunch into homework, so stop at one.

A short walk counts, weather permitting

Not every January activity has to happen indoors. A twenty-minute walk around the block, in pairs, can do more than an hour of forced fun in a conference room. Pairs work better than one big group, because in a group of twelve, four people talk and eight people listen. In a pair, both people have to talk.

Set one rule for the walk: no work talk for the first ten minutes. People can talk about the break, the weather, a show they watched, or nothing much at all. The point is time together without an agenda. After ten minutes, pairs can talk about whatever they want, and most of them will drift to work anyway, because that is what coworkers do.

Check the weather the night before, and have an indoor backup on the wheel. If January 5 brings rain or ice, spin again and pick the indoor option. A walk in bad weather does not build a team. It builds resentment, and someone will catch a cold and blame the kickoff for it.

Keep it under 45 minutes

This is the rule that saves the whole idea. A kickoff activity that runs past 45 minutes stops feeling like a welcome and starts feeling like a meeting with snacks. Put the end time on the invite, say it out loud at the start, and end on time even if the game is going well. Ending on time is how the team learns to trust the next kickoff.

A simple run sheet helps. 10:00, everyone gathers. 10:05, spin the wheel. 10:10, the activity starts. 10:40, wrap up and name one thing the team will do differently this month. 10:45, done. People go back to their desks with a story, and Luis still has the rest of the morning for the priorities he wanted to share.

If the activity needs more than 45 minutes, it is the wrong activity for the first day back. Save the escape room, the cooking class, and the full-day offsite for February, when the team has settled in and the calendar has room. January 5 is for a short, shared start, and then real work.

Hand next January to someone else

The last job of a good kickoff is picking who runs the next one. Spin the Random Name Picker at the end of the activity, and the person it lands on plans next January’s kickoff, with help from Luis and a small budget. Write the name down where the team can see it, so it does not get forgotten by March.

Rotating the planner does two things. First, it stops one person from owning team fun forever, which is a fast way to burn that person out. Second, it brings a fresh idea next year, because the person who plans it will pick the activity they wished this year’s kickoff had included.

Keep the handover light. A short note with what worked, what ran long, and what the team liked is enough. No twenty-page report, no survey with twelve questions. One page, passed from one planner to the next, and the kickoff gets a little better every January.

Try it this year. Load six short activities into the Icebreaker Wheel, spin it on the first Monday back, and end at 45 minutes. The team will remember the morning they played trivia together far longer than they will remember a two-hour speech about priorities, and January will start with a story instead of a sigh.

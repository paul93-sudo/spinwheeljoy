Retro Format Wheel: Pick This Sprint's Retrospective

For six months our retro ran the same format every two weeks, and the same three people did all the talking. A retro format wheel picks the format in front of everyone, so the conversation changes even when the team stays the same.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published November 2, 2026 Updated November 2, 2026

On this page

  1. Stop running start-stop-continue every single time
  2. Pick the format before the meeting starts
  3. Let the wheel choose the check-in question
  4. Draw the facilitator instead of defaulting to the same person
  5. Give the quiet formats a real chance
  6. Spin for the one action item that gets an owner
  7. Keep one wild-card format in the wheel
  8. Try the retro format wheel

For six months, our team’s retro looked exactly the same. Every second Thursday at 4pm, eight of us filed into the same room, someone opened the same Start Stop Continue board, and the same three people did most of the talking. The other five nodded, typed a card or two, and left at 4:45 feeling like nothing had changed. The next sprint, the same problems showed up again.

I’ll be honest. I thought the problem was the team. It wasn’t. The problem was that we ran the same format eighteen times in a row and expected a different conversation each time. A format is a set of questions, and when the questions never change, the answers stop changing either.

A retro format wheel fixes the boring part. Load the Decision Wheel with the formats your team actually likes, spin once at the start of the retro, and run whatever lands. The wheel does not care that Start Stop Continue is comfortable. It does not care that your manager prefers it. And because everyone watches the spin, nobody can say the new format was one person’s idea.

Stop running start-stop-continue every single time

Start Stop Continue is a fine format. It is also the format every team reaches for when nobody wants to decide, which is why it ends up running for months. After the fourth time, people stop thinking and start sorting. Everything good goes under Start, everything annoying goes under Stop, and Continue becomes a junk drawer for cards nobody wants to argue about.

Our team made one rule: no format twice in a row, and the wheel enforces it. We loaded six formats and spun at the end of each retro for the next one. One sprint we got Mad Sad Glad. The next we got Sailboat. The sprint after that, One Word. Nobody had to pitch a new format in a meeting, and nobody had to defend the old one. The wheel just said something different, and we went with it.

The change showed up fast. Mad Sad Glad got people talking about feelings they had been skipping, because the format asked for them directly. Sailboat got the quiet engineers talking, because drawing anchors and wind on a boat felt less like a performance than writing cards on a board. Same team, same hour, different questions, different meeting.

Pick the format before the meeting starts

Spin for the next retro at the end of the current one, not at the start of it. The facilitator needs time to set up. Sailboat needs a boat drawn on the board. A timeline retro needs the sprint dates marked out. One Word needs nothing at all, which is also useful to know in advance.

We learned this the embarrassing way. One Thursday, Dan spun Sailboat live in the meeting and then spent twelve minutes drawing a boat while seven people watched. Twelve minutes of a forty-five minute retro, gone to a whiteboard sketch. The next time, we spun the format on the Tuesday before, Dan drew the boat over lunch, and the retro started on time with the board ready.

Spinning early has a second effect nobody planned for. People show up already thinking. When the format is Mad Sad Glad and everyone knows it two days ahead, they walk in with one mad, one sad, and one glad already in mind. When the format is a surprise, the first ten minutes go to people staring at a blank board and trying to remember what happened two weeks ago.

Let the wheel choose the check-in question

Every retro starts with a check-in, and most check-ins are dead on arrival. Someone asks “how was your sprint” and seven people say “fine” and one person gives a status update. The check-in is supposed to warm the room up. Instead it puts the room to sleep.

Spin the check-in separately from the format. Load the Decision Wheel with four or five check-ins your team tolerates: one word for the sprint, a weather report, rose bud thorn, a traffic light. One spin, one question, everyone answers it. The rule is simple: the wheel picks the question, and nobody gets to pass by saying “fine.”

One Word works better than it sounds. When Maya’s team spun One Word for the first time, the answers were “tired,” “shipped,” “stuck,” “relieved,” and “almost.” That told the facilitator more in thirty seconds than a round of “fine” ever had. The word “stuck” opened the whole retro, because the person who said it finally had a reason to explain it.

Draw the facilitator instead of defaulting to the same person

In most teams, one person runs every retro. Usually it is the scrum master, or the manager, or whoever ran the first one and never stopped. After a while the retro becomes their meeting. They set the tone, they pick the format, they decide when a topic is done. Everyone else just attends.

Load the names on the Random Name Picker and spin for the next facilitator at the end of each retro. The person who gets picked runs the next one, with one rule: they can ask the previous facilitator for help, but they cannot hand it back.

Priya facilitated for the first time in March, after eight months on the team, because the wheel picked her and there was no polite way out of it. She ran a 4Ls retro, kept the action items to three, and ended five minutes early. Two sprints later she volunteered to run another one. The wheel did not make her a better facilitator. It just gave her a turn she would never have asked for, and the turn did the rest.

Give the quiet formats a real chance

Every team has formats nobody suggests. The timeline retro, where you plot the sprint’s events on a line and talk about the weeks that hurt. The 4Ls, where you sort into Liked, Learned, Lacked, and Longed For. The superhero retro, where everyone picks a power the team needs. These formats sit in blog posts and never make it into a real meeting, because suggesting one feels like volunteering to defend it.

The wheel gives them a turn without anyone having to pitch them. When the timeline retro landed for our team, it surfaced something Start Stop Continue had missed for a month: the outage in week one was still bothering people in week three. Cards about it kept showing up under Stop, but nobody connected them until they saw the whole sprint laid out on a line and realized every complaint traced back to the same two days.

Put two formats on the wheel that your team has never tried. If one of them lands and flops, take it off. The wheel is not a commitment. It is a way to try a format once without a debate about whether it is worth trying.

Spin for the one action item that gets an owner

Retros produce too many action items and too few owners. A typical retro ends with twelve cards on the action list, no names next to them, and a silent agreement that someone will do something eventually. By the next retro, eleven of them are still there, and the twelfth got done by whoever felt guilty enough.

Spin for one action item per retro. The item the wheel picks leaves the meeting with a name and a date. The rest go on a backlog list that the team reviews once a month, not once a sprint. One owned item beats twelve unowned ones, and the team can actually check it next time.

Our team’s spin landed on Fix Build in April. Dan owned it, the build time dropped from twenty-two minutes to nine by the next retro, and the team noticed because the number was on the board. The next spin landed on Docs, and the onboarding page finally got the update three new hires had been asking for. The wheel did not make people work harder. It just picked one thing and made it someone’s job in front of everyone.

Keep one wild-card format in the wheel

Put one format on the wheel that is there to break the routine. The movie title retro, where everyone names the sprint as a film. The hot air balloon retro, where the team lists what lifts it and what weighs it down. The superhero retro, where everyone picks a power. These formats sound silly, and that is the point. A team that laughs for five minutes talks more honestly for the next forty.

Run the wild card about once a quarter, not every sprint. If it lands too often it stops being a break and becomes another routine. When it does land, split into pairs with the Team Generator first, so people talk in twos before they talk to the whole room. Pairs are where the honest version of the movie title comes out. The whole-room version is always the polite one.

Sounds silly, works great. Our team’s movie title for the worst sprint of the year was “Two Weeks of Meetings,” and it got a bigger laugh than anything in the retro before it. Then someone said the quiet part out loud: half the sprint had gone to meetings about the sprint. That became the one action item, and the next sprint had three fewer recurring meetings on it.

Try the retro format wheel

Load the Decision Wheel with six formats your team already knows plus one you have never tried. Spin once at the end of your next retro for the one after that. Run whatever lands, even if you would have picked something else. The first spin feels strange. The third spin feels normal. By the fifth, your team will have run five different retros, and the meeting will stop feeling like the same hour every two weeks.

Sub Day Wheel: Plan B Activities When You're Out

It's 6:10 in the morning, you're sick, and a substitute is walking into your classroom in two hours. Load a few plan B activities onto a wheel, leave it in the sub tub, and the day runs itself without a frantic 5 a.m. email.

It’s 6:10 on a Tuesday morning, your throat feels like sandpaper, and the substitute is walking into your room in two hours. You have a fever, a phone full of unread messages, and about forty minutes before you need to be asleep again. This is the worst time to write sub plans from scratch, and every teacher knows it, because every teacher has done it at least once, typing one-handed under a blanket while a thermometer beeps on the nightstand.

Here’s the thing: a sub day does not need your best lesson. It needs a day that runs without you. Put five or six plan B activities on a Student Picker, leave the wheel in the sub tub with one page of directions, and the class spins its way through the day. The kids get choices, the sub gets a clear order, and you get to actually rest instead of fielding “what do I do after lunch” texts from the office phone.

Spin the morning starter, not the lesson plan

The first twenty minutes set the whole day. If the sub walks in with nothing ready, twenty-eight kids will invent their own morning, and it will not be quiet reading. So the first spin of the day picks the starter, and the starter is always something the class can do with zero teaching.

Load the wheel with starters the kids already know: quiet read, draw the day, journal three lines, puzzle table, word sort. The sub spins once at the door, writes the result on the board, and the class starts. No explanation needed, because every option on that wheel is something the class did in September, when you were there to teach it the first time.

One teacher I know keeps the morning wheel taped inside the sub tub lid, so the sub sees it before anything else. The tub has the books, the journals, and the puzzle box right underneath. Spin, open the tub, start. By 8:20 the room sounds like a normal morning, which is the highest praise a sub day can get.

Let the wheel pick the read-aloud

Read-aloud time is where sub days usually fall apart. The sub picks a book at random, reads two pages, loses the room, and spends the next ten minutes negotiating. Or the sub skips it entirely, and the kids spend the afternoon asking when story time is.

Fix it the night before, when you are healthy: put four or five read-aloud choices on the wheel, ones you have already read the first chapter of, or ones that stand alone as picture books. The sub spins, reads what the wheel picks, and stops at a chapter break or a natural pause. The kids argue about the spin, not about the book, and an argument about a spin ends in about four seconds.

Keep a sticky note on each book with the page to start on. “Start at page 41, stop at the picture of the lighthouse.” A sub who has never met your class can follow that. A sub who has to guess where to start cannot, and the guessing is what eats the afternoon.

Rotate the group leaders for the day

A sub day needs helpers, and it needs them picked fairly, in front of everyone. If the sub picks the same loud kid for every job, the rest of the class checks out by lunch. If the sub asks for volunteers, the same three hands go up every time.

Spin for it. One spin picks the line leader, the next picks the paper passer, the next picks the materials manager. Use the Student Picker with the class list, and let the wheel run through names without repeats until every kid has had a turn. The sub writes the names on the board as they come up, and those kids keep their jobs until lunch, when the wheel spins a fresh set.

This does more than fill jobs. It tells the class that today still has rules, and the rules still come from the same place they always come from: the wheel, not the loudest voice in the room. Kids who never get picked on a normal day get picked on a sub day, and they remember that.

Pick the brain-break schedule in advance

Kids cannot sit still for a full sub day, and a sub who tries to make them will lose by 10 a.m. The fix is a brain-break wheel the class spins every hour, on the hour. The breaks are short, two or three minutes, and they are all things a sub can run without equipment: stretch, silent dance, breathe slow, freeze game, stand and shake it out.

Spin it at 9, at 10, at 11, and after lunch. The class knows a break is coming, so the sitting gets easier, and the sub knows exactly what to do when the wiggles start, because the wheel already decided. If your class already has a break routine, the Brain Break Wheel runs the same way, one spin per break, no planning on the day.

I’ll be honest: the first time I watched a sub run this, I expected chaos. Twenty-eight kids doing a silent dance for ninety seconds looked like chaos for about ten seconds, and then it looked like a class that had burned off just enough energy to sit for another hour. That trade is worth it every time.

Spin for the afternoon game, not a worksheet packet

Afternoon is the danger zone. The morning went fine, lunch was loud, and now the class has ninety minutes left and a sub who is out of ideas. This is when the emergency worksheet packet comes out, and this is when the class decides the day is over.

Put the afternoon game on the wheel instead. Review quiz, drawing challenge, building challenge, word game, trivia teams. The sub spins at 1 p.m., and the class plays what comes up. A review game works because the kids already know the material, and a drawing challenge works because it needs nothing but paper. Either way, the afternoon has a shape, and a shaped afternoon ends with kids packing up calmly instead of bouncing off the walls.

One rule matters here: the afternoon wheel never holds a test, a graded assignment, or anything you would need to explain in person. If an activity needs your voice to make sense, it does not belong on a sub day. Save it for Thursday, when you are back and your throat works again.

Assign the end-of-day jobs by spin

The last fifteen minutes decide how the room looks when you return. If jobs go to whoever shouts first, the same kids clean up and the same kids slip out early. If jobs go by spin, everyone has a job, and the jobs get done, because the wheel said so in front of the whole class.

Load the jobs wheel with line leader, paper passer, board cleaner, door holder, chair stacker, and supply checker. Spin through it at 2:30, write the names up, and let the class work. The sub checks the jobs off as they finish, and the last job on the list is always the same: stack the sub note on your desk, with one line about what got done and one line about what did not.

That note is gold. “Finished read-aloud chapter 4, did not finish the afternoon game, Mia helped with papers.” You walk in the next morning, read four lines, and you know exactly where the class stands, instead of reconstructing the day from a pile of half-finished worksheets.

What not to spin

A wheel is a tool, not a substitute for judgment, and a sub day has a few lines it should not cross. Do not spin for behavior consequences. If a kid needs a consequence, the sub follows your written plan, not a wheel. Do not spin for who sits where after a conflict, and do not spin for anything involving a child’s grades, health, or family situation. Those calls belong to an adult with the full picture, and a sub on day one does not have it.

Write those lines on the same page as the wheel. “Spin for activities and jobs. Do not spin for consequences or seating after a conflict. Call the office for anything else.” One paragraph, in plain words, at the top of the sub tub page. It protects the sub, it protects the kids, and it keeps the wheel doing what it does well: picking activities, fairly, in the open.

Build the sub tub once, reuse it all year

None of this works if you build it while sick. Build it once, on a healthy Friday afternoon, and it pays for itself every time you wake up with a fever. The tub holds the wheel printouts, the books with sticky notes, the journals, the puzzle box, the job list, and the one-page directions. Label the lid with the class list and the office number, and tell the teacher next door where the tub lives, so a sub never has to hunt for it.

Update the tub once a month. Swap in a new read-aloud, rotate two activities off the afternoon wheel, add a fresh puzzle. Ten minutes, once a month, and the wheel never goes stale. Kids who have seen the same five activities since September will stop caring about the spin, and the spin is the whole trick. Fresh options keep it working.

A sub day will never be your best teaching day. It does not need to be. It needs to be a day where the kids read something, played something, moved a little, and left the room in decent shape. A wheel in a tub does that, without a 5 a.m. email, without a phone call from the office, and without you spending your sick day writing plans under a blanket. Rest, and let the wheel run the room for one day.

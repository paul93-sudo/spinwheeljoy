New Year's Day Brunch Wheel: Pick the First Meal of the Year

January 1 starts with tired people and different appetites. Put the dishes, the cook, and the start time on a wheel, and let one spin settle the first meal of the year.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 26, 2026 Updated October 26, 2026

On this page

  1. Start with who is actually awake
  2. Spin for the main dish first
  3. Settle sweet versus savory with a second spin
  4. Spin for the cook, and mean it
  5. Spin the start time instead of guessing it
  6. Let the wheel pick the drink order
  7. One spin for cleanup, before anyone eats
  8. Try the New Year’s Day brunch wheel

It was 10:47 on January 1 when my brother texted the group chat: “Brunch?” Nobody answered for nine minutes. My sister was asleep on the couch with a blanket over her face. My dad was already on his second coffee and wanted eggs. My niece, who is nine and very sure of herself, wanted pancakes and nothing else.

Last year we had spent forty minutes on the same question. Someone suggested going out. Someone else said the diner would be packed. My mom offered to cook, then looked tired just saying it. By the time we agreed on pancakes, half the group had eaten cereal standing up in the kitchen, and the “brunch” was four people eating at different times.

I’ll be honest, the problem was never a lack of food. We had eggs, bread, bacon, frozen waffles, and a bag of oranges that had been in the fridge since December 29. The problem was that January 1 is a strange morning. Some people are hungover. Some are up early because the kids are up early. Nobody wants to be the person who decides, because the person who decides also gets blamed if the food is wrong.

A wheel fixes that part. Load the dishes into the What to Eat Wheel, spin once, and the first meal of the year is settled before anyone has time to argue about it. The rest of this post is about the small decisions around that spin, because brunch on January 1 is never just one decision.

Start with who is actually awake

Do not plan brunch for 10 am if half the house went to bed at 2 am. That plan sounds tidy on December 31 and falls apart on January 1, when the first person downstairs is a kid asking for breakfast at 8:15 and the last person downstairs is an adult asking for coffee at 11:30.

Count heads first. Who is in the house, who is still asleep, who has to leave by 1 pm for a drive home. That count tells you whether brunch is a sit-down meal for eight or a rolling meal where people eat as they wake up. Both work. Pretending it is a sit-down meal when it is not does not.

If the count is small, keep it simple. Pancakes for three do not need a plan. If the count is big, the wheel helps more, because eight people will never agree on one dish by talking. They will agree on a spin they watched together.

Spin for the main dish first

Put six dishes on the wheel and spin once. Pancakes, eggs, French toast, bagels, hash, fruit and yogurt. Whatever lands is the main dish. No second spin because someone groans. That rule sounds harsh and it is the whole point. One spin, one dish, done.

Here is the thing: the dish almost never matters as much as the argument about the dish. Last January the wheel landed on hash, and my niece announced she did not eat hash. She ate two plates of it once my dad put a fried egg on top and called it “breakfast hash with a hat.” Kids will eat a dish they watched a wheel pick. They will fight the same dish if an adult picked it.

Keep the list to food you already have. A wheel that lands on eggs Benedict when you have no Canadian bacon and no lemons is not a decision. It is a trip to a store that may be closed. Check the fridge first, then load the wheel.

Settle sweet versus savory with a second spin

Some years the main dish spin still leaves a fight, because half the table wants something sweet and half wants something salty. That is a real split, and one dish will not fix it. Spin again, with two options only: sweet or savory.

If sweet wins, the sweet dish from the first list leads, and the savory side gets a small second plate. Bacon next to pancakes. Sausage next to French toast. If savory wins, flip it. Eggs lead, and a bowl of fruit or a plate of cinnamon toast sits beside them. Nobody eats only what they wanted, and nobody eats nothing they wanted. That balance is enough on a tired morning.

My family learned this after a year when pancakes won and my dad ate three pancakes in silence, then made himself eggs an hour later. He was not being difficult. He just wanted salt. A second plate of bacon would have saved the hour and the silence.

Spin for the cook, and mean it

This is the spin people skip, and it is the one that matters most. Someone has to cook on January 1, and in most houses it is the same person who cooked on December 24, December 25, and December 31. That person is tired. A wheel can say so without a speech.

Put the adults on the wheel. Spin once. Whoever lands cooks the main dish. Everyone else handles one small job: coffee, table, fruit, cleanup. Write the jobs down before the spin, so the cook does not end up cooking and also setting the table and also washing up.

One rule keeps it fair: the cook from last year stays on the wheel. No one gets a free pass because they cooked last time. If they land again, they cook again, and the rest of the group owes them a real break later, like a full afternoon with no dishes. If that sounds harsh, remember the alternative. The alternative is the same person cooking again because nobody else volunteered, and that is harsher.

Spin the start time instead of guessing it

“Brunch at 11” is a guess. On January 1, guesses turn into texts: “Are we eating soon?” at 10:20, “Should I just eat cereal?” at 10:50, “Is anyone cooking?” at 11:15. A spin ends that chain.

Put four start times on the wheel: 10:30, 11, 11:30, 12. Spin the night before, on December 31, when everyone is awake and in a good mood. Write the time on a sticky note on the fridge. Then January 1 has a plan that nobody has to renegotiate while half asleep.

The time spin also settles the early riser problem. If the wheel says 11:30, the early risers get a small snack at 9, fruit or toast, and the real meal still happens at 11:30. Nobody is told to wait hungry for three hours. They are told the meal time, and they get something small until then.

Let the wheel pick the drink order

Coffee is where January 1 mornings stall. One person wants coffee, one wants tea, one wants orange juice, one wants water and quiet. Someone has to make the coffee run to the kitchen, and that someone usually ends up making four different drinks while the food gets cold.

Spin for the drink order, not the drink itself. Put the names on the wheel and spin for who gets served first, or for who makes the coffee pot. Better yet, spin for one shared drink rule: everyone drinks coffee or tea from the same pot, and juice is poured from one carton on the table. One pot, one carton, no custom orders until the meal is over.

Sounds silly, works great. The year we tried it, breakfast was on the table twenty minutes earlier, because nobody was standing at the counter making a fourth drink. The coffee drinkers got coffee. The tea drinker got tea from a kettle that was already on. The juice drinker poured her own glass. Done.

One spin for cleanup, before anyone eats

Cleanup is the January 1 decision nobody wants to make after eating. Everyone is full, someone is washing a pan, and the rest are drifting toward the couch. Spin for cleanup before the first plate is served, when everyone is still at the table and still in a fair mood.

Put the jobs on the wheel: dishes, wipe the table, take out the trash, pack leftovers, sweep, dry. Spin once per person, and take each job off the wheel as it lands. No trading unless both people agree out loud. The cook does not also get dishes. That rule goes on the sticky note with the start time.

Leftovers deserve their own line on that note. Who takes the extra pancakes home, who keeps the bacon, who gets the last orange. A thirty-second spin for the leftover box beats a quiet standoff over a container at 2 pm, when everyone is packing to leave and nobody wants to say they want the pancakes.

Try the New Year’s Day brunch wheel

Tonight, or on December 31 when the house is full, load the dishes into the What to Eat Wheel and spin for the main dish. Then spin for the cook, the start time, and cleanup, and write all four results on one sticky note on the fridge. January 1 will still be tired. It will not be an argument.

If the group cannot agree on the dish list itself, the Decision Wheel can settle the sweet versus savory call first. And if one person keeps reopening a settled spin, the Yes or No Wheel answers the only question left: are we spinning again, yes or no. The answer, after the first spin, is no.

The first meal of the year does not need to be fancy. It needs to be on the table, at a time everyone knows, cooked by someone who did not cook every meal last week, with a plan for the dishes after. Spin for it, eat it together, and let January 1 start with food instead of a debate.

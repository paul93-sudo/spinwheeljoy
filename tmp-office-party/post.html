Office Holiday Party Theme Wheel: Pick This Year's Theme

Your office party committee has spent three meetings arguing about a theme. Load the options onto a wheel, spin once, and spend the remaining time on food, music, and a date that actually works.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 24, 2026 Updated October 24, 2026

Our office party committee met three times last October and still had no theme. One person wanted a winter wonderland. Another wanted a casino night. A third kept pushing tropical, in Minnesota, in December, with a straight face. By the third meeting, someone suggested we just skip the theme and order sandwiches, which is how you know a planning meeting has gone on too long.

I loaded the six themes onto a Decision Wheel during the fourth meeting, spun it in front of everyone, and it landed on ugly sweater. The room went quiet for about two seconds. Then someone said, “Well, at least I already own one,” and we moved on to food. The whole theme argument took ninety seconds. We spent the rest of the meeting on things that mattered, like the date and the playlist.

Here is how to run the same vote at your office, and how to let the theme do real work once it lands.

Spin the theme, stop the meeting loop

The theme argument drags because nobody wants to lose in public. If you vote by show of hands, the person whose idea loses sits there quietly resenting the winner for the next month. If you vote by email poll, the loudest person campaigns in the hallway for a week.

A wheel takes the loss out of one person’s hands. Put every serious suggestion on it, one segment each, and spin it where everyone can see. The theme is not chosen by the boss, or by the person who talks the most, or by whoever sent the last email. It is chosen by a spin, and a spin has no favorites.

Keep the list short. Six themes is about right. More than eight and the segments get hard to read, and someone will complain their theme got a smaller slice. Six real options: ugly sweater, winter wonderland, tropical, casino night, movie night, and cookie swap. If your office has a hard no, like no gambling themes at a company event, leave it off before you spin. A wheel only feels fair if every option on it is one you would actually run.

Spin once, and write the result down in the meeting notes before anyone can suggest a re-spin. That written record is the whole trick. Without it, the second meeting starts with “maybe we should reconsider.”

Let the theme set the dress code

This is where a theme earns its keep. Without one, the dress code email says “festive attire,” and half the office shows up in jeans while the other half shows up in sequins. Both groups feel underdressed or overdressed, and nobody has fun.

With a theme, the dress code writes itself. Ugly sweater means everyone already owns the outfit, or can find one at a thrift store for ten dollars. Winter wonderland means white, silver, and blue, which most people can put together from clothes they own. Tropical means Hawaiian shirts, which the same three people will wear with great pride and everyone else will tolerate.

The rule I use now: if the theme needs anyone to buy something that costs more than twenty dollars, it is the wrong theme for an office party. Ugly sweater passes. Casino night passes, because a blazer and a borrowed bow tie will do. A full 1920s costume party fails, because half your coworkers will spend eighty dollars on a flapper dress they wear once.

Put the dress code in the same email as the theme announcement, with one sentence and one example. “Theme: ugly sweater. Wear the worst one you own.” Done. People like a clear instruction far more than they like a vague suggestion.

Decorations follow the theme, and the budget follows the decorations

Here is the thing about decorating an office party: without a theme, people buy a little bit of everything, and the room looks like a party supply store exploded. With a theme, you buy one set of things, and the room looks like someone planned it.

Ugly sweater night needs almost nothing. A few strings of lights, a table for a sweater contest, and a sign-up sheet. Winter wonderland needs white tablecloths, fake snow on the tables, and blue lights, which you can reuse next year. Tropical needs leis and paper palm leaves, which cost very little and look ridiculous in December in exactly the right way.

Set the decor budget before you shop, not after. Our office uses fifty dollars for a party of thirty people, which sounds low until you realize lights and tablecloths come back every year. Spin a small wheel for the decor splurge if you want: lights, balloons, garland, banners, candles, or fake snow, one splurge item on top of the basics. It gives the decorating crew one fun decision and stops them from buying all six.

And if the theme lands on something your office cannot decorate for, that is useful information too. A theme that needs a rented casino table and a dealer is a theme for a company with a bigger budget than yours. Cross it off next year before you spin.

Food that fits the theme

The fastest way to make a theme feel real is to feed people food that matches it. A winter wonderland party with pizza tastes like a regular Tuesday. A winter wonderland party with hot cocoa, sugar cookies, and a tray of snowball truffles tastes like a party.

You do not need a caterer to pull this off. Match one or two items to the theme and let the rest be normal party food. Ugly sweater night: hot cocoa bar and cookies. Tropical night: pineapple skewers and a fruit punch bowl. Casino night: finger foods people can eat standing up, because nobody eats a full plate while holding cards.

Spin for the main dish if the committee cannot agree. Load the wheel with tacos, pizza, cookies, hot cocoa, sushi, and barbecue, and let it pick. I have seen a committee argue about tacos versus pizza for twenty minutes, which is nineteen minutes longer than that argument deserves. One spin, tacos win, order tacos, move on.

For a bigger office, spin for the dessert table too, or let each team bring one dish that fits the theme. The accounting team brought snowball cookies to our winter party last year, and people still talk about them. Nobody talks about the catering tray.

Music and games that match, without hiring a DJ

A theme also settles the playlist, which is another argument that eats meetings. Winter wonderland gets holiday songs and a few slow ones for the end of the night. Tropical gets beach music, which sounds absurd in December and gets people dancing faster than anything else we tried. Casino night gets jazz in the background, low enough that people can talk over it.

If you want games, let the theme pick those too. Ugly sweater night has an obvious one: a sweater contest, judged by applause, with a prize that costs less than fifteen dollars. Winter wonderland can do a snowball toss with rolled-up socks into a bucket. Casino night can do a poker table with play chips, no real money, which keeps HR happy and the game friendly.

One game is enough. Two games is plenty. Three games means half the party is spent explaining rules to people who just want to eat cookies and talk. The wheel can pick the game if you load it with options, but honestly, the sweater contest picks itself. It is the one office party game where the shyest person in the office sometimes wins, because the worst sweater usually belongs to someone who never tries to stand out.

The one re-spin rule, and when to use it

Every fair system needs an escape hatch, and this one has exactly one. If the theme lands on something the office genuinely cannot run, you get one re-spin, decided in advance, not after the result.

Genuinely cannot run means a real problem: the venue bans open flames and you landed on a candlelit theme, or the theme needs outdoor space and it is snowing. It does not mean someone does not like the result. “I hate ugly sweaters” is not a reason for a re-spin. “Our office has a no-gambling policy and we landed on casino night” is.

Write the re-spin rule down before you spin. “One re-spin, only if the theme breaks a company policy or a venue rule.” Say it out loud. Then spin. If you need the re-spin, use it once, and the second result stands, no matter what it is. Two re-spins is just voting again with extra steps, and everyone in the room knows it.

For the side questions that come up after the theme is set, like the party date or who brings the plates, a Random Name Picker can settle those without another meeting. And if the party planning itself turns into a team event, the Team Generator splits the decorating crew fairly, so the same five people do not end up doing all the work every year.

Try the theme wheel for your party

Load your six themes onto the Decision Wheel, gather the committee in one room, and spin it where everyone can see. Write the result in the meeting notes, send the dress code email the same day, and spend the meetings you just saved on food, music, and a date people can actually make.

Our ugly sweater party last year had forty people, a cocoa bar, and a sweater contest won by a man who had clearly owned his sweater since 1998. It was the easiest party we ever planned, because the theme was decided in ninety seconds and everything else followed from it. Your committee can have the same ninety seconds. Spin, write it down, and get on with the party.

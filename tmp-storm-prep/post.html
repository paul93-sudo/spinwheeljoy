Storm Prep Wheel: What to Buy Before the Shelves Empty

The storm alert hits at 6am and the shelves are bare by noon. Stop buying panic groceries. One wheel buys the right things in the right order, in about forty minutes.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 24, 2026 Updated October 24, 2026

The alert buzzed at 6:12 on a Tuesday morning. Winter storm warning, Thursday through Saturday, eight to fourteen inches. I did what I always do: I opened the fridge like the forecast had personally inspected my pantry and found it wanting.

Two eggs. A quarter carton of oat milk. Leftovers in three containers I did not trust.

By noon the grocery store line wrapped past the frozen pizzas. By three, the water aisle was a wall of cardboard. People were panic-buying gallon jugs of milk like a snowstorm converts your body into a dairy-processing plant. I watched a man fill his cart with fourteen bags of chips and nothing else, and I thought: this man has a plan, and it is a terrible one.

Here is the problem with storm prep. It is not laziness. It is that panic has no priority list. You grab what your eyes land on, which is why the chips disappear and the batteries stay.

Last winter I tried something different. I loaded a Decision Wheel with the supply categories and spun for the order instead of wandering the store in a daze. Sounds silly. Worked great. We were out in forty minutes with everything that mattered.

The triage spin: what goes in the cart first

Panic shopping has no sequence. You buy peanut butter at 9am and remember the prescription refill at 9pm, after the pharmacy closes early.

So the first spin decides the order. Load the wheel with the categories: water, meds and first aid, heat and light, food you can eat cold, baby and pet supplies, hygiene. Spin it. Whatever lands first, that is your first aisle.

Mine landed on meds. Annoying, because I wanted to grab water first like everyone else. But that is the point. The wheel does not have my biases. We got the prescription refill, the kid’s inhaler backup, and a bottle of ibuprofen before the pharmacy counter closed. The water aisle still had plenty when we got there, because water is the thing stores restock first and the thing people buy too much of anyway.

Food you can eat cold

Here is the mistake I used to make: buying food for a normal week, during a week that will not be normal. The power might hold. It might not. Buy like it will not.

Load the wheel with pantry staples: canned soup, canned beans, peanut butter, crackers, bread, dried fruit, nuts, applesauce cups. One spin per slot in the cart. The kids vetoed beans three spins in a row, and I overruled them, because democracy has limits during a weather emergency. They ate the beans Thursday night by lantern light and said they were “actually kind of good,” which in kid language is a five-star review.

The honest rule: if it needs the stove, it is a gamble. If it needs the fridge, it is a bigger gamble. Cans do not care about the grid.

Heat and light: the trip nobody plans

Everybody buys food. Half the people buy batteries. Almost nobody buys what keeps the house livable when the furnace quits.

This is the spin people skip and regret. Load it with flashlights, lanterns, battery packs, blankets, hand warmers, candles and matches, a phone power bank. Spin for each one, one per person, and buy what lands.

My husband spun candles and rolled his eyes. Thursday night the power flickered for six hours and he sat reading by candlelight like a man in a historical drama. He has never admitted he was wrong. He did move the candle box to the top shelf where we can find it, which is the same thing.

For the small calls inside the prep, like whether you drive across town for the last generator or make do, the Yes or No Wheel settles it in ten seconds.

The things everybody forgets

There is a category of storm supply that is not dramatic, which is why nobody buys it. The can opener. You can own thirty cans of soup and stare at them helplessly without one. Cash, because the card reader at the gas station runs on the same grid you are worried about. The phone charger you keep meaning to replace. The full tank of gas, bought Wednesday, not Friday.

Load the wheel with the forgotten list and spin before you leave the house. Ours landed on cash. I pulled out forty dollars and felt ridiculous. Friday morning the gas station card reader went dark like it had been waiting for me to be smug.

One comfort pick each

Storm prep lists are all fear. No joy. By day two of being stuck inside, morale is a supply too, and it runs out fastest.

Give every person in the house one spin for a comfort item. Not survival. Comfort. My daughter spun and got hot chocolate. My son spun and got a new puzzle. I spun and got the bag of fancy coffee I never buy because it costs too much, and on Friday morning, in a quiet house with the snow coming down, that coffee was the best eleven dollars I spent all month.

This is the spin the kids will remember. Not the batteries. The wheel that let them choose something.

The skip-it spin

The wheel is also for what not to buy. The milk thing. Eight gallons of milk for a three-day storm is not prep, it is a science experiment that ends with a sour fridge. Same with a freezer full of meat when the power might die, or three bags of salad that will be soup by Saturday.

Load a wheel with your impulse grabs, spin, and for each one ask: does this survive three days with no power? If the answer is no, it goes back on the shelf. The chips guy from the intro? I hope he had dip.

Split the chores before the storm

Buying the supplies is half the job. The other half is hauling them, filling tubs with water, charging every device in the house, and clearing the driveway. The Chore Wheel splits that list without the argument about whose turn it is to carry the heavy water jugs up the stairs.

We spun it Wednesday night. My son got “charge everything.” He took it seriously. By Thursday morning every device in the house was at one hundred percent, and he announced it like he had personally saved us. He kind of had.

The calm person in the store

Thursday came. Snow. The power flickered but held. We ate beans by lantern light anyway, because the lanterns were there and the mood was right.

The wheel did not make the storm smaller. It made the shopping smaller, which is the only part I can control. Forty minutes, one cart, nothing forgotten, nothing stupid.

So when the next alert buzzes at 6:12 on a Tuesday morning, skip the panic lap. Load the Decision Wheel, spin for your order, and be the calm person in the store. Everybody else will be staring at you like you know something. Now you do.

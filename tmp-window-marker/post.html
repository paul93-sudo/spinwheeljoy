Window Marker Wheel: Holiday Drawings for the Front Glass

Two kids, one front window, and a fistful of washable markers. Put the drawing ideas on a wheel, spin once, and let the glass decide what goes up tonight.

By SpinWheelJoy Team Published October 24, 2026 Updated October 24, 2026

On this page

  2. Give each kid a pane
  3. Run an advent window, one drawing a day
  4. Snowflakes without the photocopies
  5. Face the greeting at the street
  6. Mistakes wipe off, so let kids try the hard one
  7. Keep the keeper for Christmas Eve

Last December my neighbor’s front window changed every few days. One week it was a snowman with a crooked carrot nose. Then a lopsided tree showed up next to it, then a whole row of stars along the bottom. Her two kids did it with washable window markers, the kind that wipe off with a damp cloth, and the whole street could see it from the sidewalk. My own kids saw it on the walk home from school and wanted our window done that same night. That was the start of the argument.

One wanted a reindeer. The other wanted a snowman. I wanted something that did not involve a ladder, a stencil, or me drawing the whole thing myself while they watched. So we put six ideas on a Color Wheel, spun it on the porch, and let the wheel settle it. It landed on snowman. The reindeer kid got the next night. Nobody cried, which for our house counts as a successful craft project. Here is how to run the same thing on your own front glass, without turning December into a second job.

Let the wheel pick tonight’s scene

The hardest part of window drawing is not the drawing. It is picking one idea out of the twenty floating around the kitchen table. Left alone, kids will debate it for half an hour, and by the time they agree, the markers are dry and the light is gone. A wheel cuts that debate down to one spin.

Load the wheel with scenes that fit your window. Snowman, tree, village, stars, reindeer, wreath. Six options is plenty. Each one should be drawable in about twenty minutes by the person who will actually hold the marker. If your youngest is five, do not put a full nativity village on the wheel next to a star. That is not a fair fight, and the village will win on a night when you have no time for it.

Spin on the porch, in front of the window, so the result feels like a decision and not a trick. Whoever spins gets to draw first. The rest of the family adds details around the main scene: stars, snow dots, a scarf, a hat. One scene per night keeps the glass readable from the street. Ten scenes crammed into one pane just looks like a pile of colors.

Give each kid a pane

Most front windows split into panes, and that split solves the fairness problem for free. One pane per kid. No shared drawing, no one person hogging the middle, no older kid redrawing the younger kid’s snowman into something better. Each kid owns a rectangle of glass and answers for what goes in it.

Spin for the order, not the idea. Put the kids’ names on the wheel and let it pick who draws first, who picks the corner pane, who gets the big middle pane. Then each kid draws their own scene in their own pane. My daughter’s pane last year had a snowman family of four. My son’s had a reindeer that looked, to be honest, a lot like a dog with sticks on its head. Both panes stayed up all month. Nobody touched anyone else’s glass.

If you have one big undivided window, tape light lines across it with painter’s tape on the inside to make fake panes. Peel the tape off at the end and the lines come with it. If two kids want the same pane, spin for it and move on. The wheel is faster than a negotiation, and December has enough negotiations already.

Run an advent window, one drawing a day

Here is the version that kept us busy all month without any extra planning. Divide the window into numbered panes, or just add one small drawing a day along the bottom edge. Day 1: a star. Day 7: a tree. Day 14: a snowman. Day 24: whatever the wheel says, drawn after dinner on Christmas Eve.

The advent window works because the job is small. One drawing a day, ten minutes, done. Kids who would groan at a two-hour craft session will happily draw one star before bed. And the window grows in front of them, which is half the fun. By week three, the glass tells the story of the month: the early wobbly stars next to the later, much steadier trees.

Put the numbers on the wheel too. On nights when nobody can agree what to draw, spin a number wheel for the day’s subject instead: 1 is a star, 2 is a tree, 3 is a snowman, and so on up to whatever list you wrote on the fridge. A Random Name Picker can settle who draws on nights when both kids want the same day. The point is the same every time: one spin, one answer, markers out.

Snowflakes without the photocopies

Snowflakes are the default window drawing, and they are also the one that goes wrong fastest. One kid draws a six-point flake, the other copies it, and by the third pane you have the same flake five times. It looks less like a winter scene and more like someone stamped the glass.

Fix it with a pattern wheel. Six-point, swirl, star, lacy, tiny, giant. Each spin picks a different pattern, so the panes fill up with flakes that actually look different from each other. Show the kids one trick before they start: draw the center lines first, then add the arms, then add the small details last. Flakes drawn details-first turn into blobs. Flakes drawn lines-first look like flakes even when the lines wobble.

Keep a damp cloth nearby, not for mistakes, but for the moment someone draws a flake twice the size of the pane. Wipe the outer arms, redraw smaller. Window markers forgive fast. That is the whole reason to use them instead of paint. A kid who knows a mistake wipes off in ten seconds will try a harder pattern. A kid who thinks a mistake is permanent will draw the same safe star all month.

Face the greeting at the street

Most window drawings face the wrong way. Kids draw standing inside the house, so the picture reads fine from the living room and backwards from the sidewalk. Letters are the worst of it. A MERRY CHRISTMAS drawn from inside reads as a mirror puzzle to everyone walking past.

Before the first marker comes out, decide which side the art is for. If the window faces the street, the letters go on the outside of the glass, or the kids draw them standing outside looking in. Yes, it is colder out there. It is also the only way the greeting reads right. Put the greeting words on the wheel: welcome, merry, joy, peace, ho ho, cheer. Spin one, draw it big, and check it from the sidewalk before anyone adds decorations around it.

Draw the greeting first, then the pictures around it. Greetings squeezed in after the snowman and the tree end up small, crooked, and half hidden behind a curtain. A big greeting in the middle with drawings around the edges reads from twenty feet away, which is the distance that matters. Neighbors do not press their noses to your glass. They see it from the walk, in passing, and a big clear word is what they remember.

Mistakes wipe off, so let kids try the hard one

I will be honest about the part I got wrong the first year. I kept steering my kids toward easy drawings. Stars, dots, simple trees. I thought I was saving them from frustration. What I was actually doing was capping the month at the level of the first week. The window stayed safe and a little boring, and by mid-December nobody wanted to add to it.

Window markers changed my mind. A bad reindeer wipes off with a cloth and a little water. A crooked village wipes off. A whole pane of color that looked great in someone’s head and terrible on glass wipes off in two minutes. There is no cost to trying the hard drawing except two minutes of wiping, and there is a real cost to never trying it: a month of the same star.

So let the wheel pick the hard one sometimes. Put the village on it. Put the reindeer on it. When it lands on a hard scene, give the kid the pane and the cloth and step back. The first try might get wiped. The second try is usually the one that stays up all month, and it is the one they point out to visitors.

One practical note: test your markers on a corner of the glass first, especially if the window has a coating or a tint. Most washable markers wipe clean, but a ten-second corner test beats a month of faint ghost lines. If a color leaves a shadow after wiping, switch to a different brand for that color and keep the rest.

Keep the keeper for Christmas Eve

By Christmas Eve the window is full, and that is the night to pick the keeper. Walk outside as a family, look at the whole window from the sidewalk, and spin for which drawing gets a gold star drawn next to it, or a little frame around it. Not a contest with a loser. Just one drawing that gets named the favorite of the year.

Take a photo of the window that night, from outside, with the house lights on behind it. The photo is the part that lasts. The markers come off in January with a cloth and some warm water, and the glass goes back to being a window. My neighbor’s kids still talk about last year’s crooked carrot nose, and they have the photo on the fridge to prove it looked exactly that crooked.

Then, when the glass is clean again, put the wheel away for the year. It did its job: one spin per night, no arguments about what to draw, a window that changed all month, and a photo worth keeping. Load the Color Wheel with your family’s six scenes, grab the markers, and let tonight’s spin pick the first drawing. If it lands on snowman, draw the nose crooked. It looks better that way.
